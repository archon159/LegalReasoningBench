**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of the merits section:

- 'In view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicant, the Court concludes that his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society.'
- 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Yaroslav Belousov, cited above, §§ 168-71).' The Court thus confirms the existence of an assembly by reference to prior case law, finding this element satisfied.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Implied. The Court does not separately discuss whether the applicant participated, but it is implied throughout the analysis. The applicant 'had gone on 6 May 2012 to take part in a peaceful meeting which had been authorised by the Moscow authorities,' and the Court proceeds to assess his personal protection under Article 11, stating 'he enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention.' His participation is thus treated as given.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'As regards the applicant personally, it does not transpire from the description of his offences that he was among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character. Accordingly, he enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court thus finds that, as far as the applicant personally is concerned, the assembly was peaceful (or at least that he was not responsible for its non-peaceful elements), and he therefore falls within the protection of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court also finds that the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for participation in mass disorder constituted an interference with the exercise of the freedom of assembly.' This is a clear and explicit finding of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
Explicitly discussed at the overall level. The Court states: 'The parties made essentially the same submissions on the lawfulness of the interference as in Yaroslav Belousov (cited above, § 173). The Court sees no reason to reach a different conclusion in this case (ibid., §§ 174-75). It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution was lawful...' The Court thus finds the interference was prescribed by law, though it does so by reference to the Yaroslav Belousov judgment without separately elaborating on foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards sub-elements in this judgment.

However, the Court does touch on the safeguards sub-element in the necessity analysis: 'In those circumstances it cannot be said that the applicant enjoyed a procedural safeguard against arbitrary interference with the rights guaranteed by Articles 10 and 11 of the Convention in the domestic proceedings.' This is discussed in the context of necessity rather than as a standalone prescribed-by-law analysis, but it does address the absence of adequate safeguards.

**(i) Foreseeable?** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element; implied as satisfied given the overall finding that the interference was lawful/prescribed by law.

**(ii) Accessible?** Not explicitly discussed; implied as satisfied.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse?** Discussed, but in the context of necessity rather than prescribed by law: 'In those circumstances it cannot be said that the applicant enjoyed a procedural safeguard against arbitrary interference with the rights guaranteed by Articles 10 and 11 of the Convention in the domestic proceedings.' The Court finds safeguards were lacking, but frames this as part of the necessity/proportionality analysis.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution was lawful, and pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.' The aims found are: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages include:
- The Court notes the first-instance court did not verify the applicant's account and refused to question a defence witness, meaning 'it cannot be said that the applicant enjoyed a procedural safeguard against arbitrary interference.'
- 'his staying at the site of the rally and chanting anti-government slogans accounted for an additional two years and two months to his prison term under Article 212 of the Criminal Code. Those acts, unlike throwing pieces of tarmac (already imputed under Article 318 of the Code), were peaceful and constituted an act of protest, a form of expression protected by Article 10 of the Convention.'
- 'the criminal file did not suggest any pre-existing intention by the applicant to participate in such acts.'
- 'the Court does not consider that the risks referred to by the Government – potential civil unrest, political instability and a threat to public order – had any personal relation to him.'
- 'the reasons advanced by the Government could not justify a three-year prison sentence for participation in acts of mass disorder under Article 212 of the Criminal Code. The Court considers that there was no "pressing social need" to give the applicant a sentence of that duration.'
- 'the applicant's criminal conviction, and especially the severity of his sentence, must have had the effect of discouraging him and other opposition supporters, as well as the public at large, from attending demonstrations' (chilling effect).
- Final conclusion: 'his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society.'

**(f) Positive obligation**
Discussed but explicitly set aside as a separate issue. The Court states in the admissibility section: 'the Court is not required to assess the authorities' alleged failure to discharge their positive obligation in respect of the conduct of the assembly at Bolotnaya Square as a separate issue under Article 11 of the Convention. It will examine whether the measures taken against the applicant personally complied with that provision in the light of all the material submitted by the parties concerning the planning, conduct and dispersal of the assembly.' The Court thus declines to examine positive obligations as a standalone issue, folding the relevant factual material into the negative obligation analysis. No separate conclusion on positive obligations is reached.
