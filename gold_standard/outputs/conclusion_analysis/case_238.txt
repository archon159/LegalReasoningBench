**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — i.e., there was an assembly, the applicants participated (as "organisers or participants of public assemblies"), and the assembly was within the protective scope of Article 11. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This implies all three scope sub-elements are satisfied.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "these assemblies" without questioning their existence.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the protection of the article.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the penultimate paragraph: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The existence of interference is also implied by the opening description of "arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The conclusion on interference is affirmative (yes, there was interference), discussed explicitly.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. None of the three sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are discussed. These are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not_discussed, and no aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court also references its prior case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) where similar violations were found. The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this reasoning. This element is not_discussed.
