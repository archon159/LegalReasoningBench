**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness) in the reasoning text. However, scope is implied: the Court states that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred, which logically presupposes that (i) there was an assembly, (ii) the applicants participated in it, and (iii) it was peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). The opening paragraph also describes the applicants as "participants of public assemblies," implying all three scope sub-elements are satisfied.

- **Assembly exists:** Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" being interfered with. Conclusion: yes, implied.
- **Participation:** Implied by the description of the applicants as "participants of public assemblies." Conclusion: yes, implied.
- **Peaceful:** Implied by the fact that Article 11 protection is applied without qualification. Conclusion: yes, implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." The Court then confirms: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming that an interference was found. Conclusion: yes, explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor do any of the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) receive explicit treatment. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, this element is not discussed. Conclusion: null, not discussed.

- **Foreseeable:** not discussed.
- **Accessible:** not discussed.
- **Safeguards:** not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or assessed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. Conclusion: null, not discussed. Aims found: none.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court also references its proportionality case-law (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) as the foundation for this conclusion. Conclusion: no (not necessary), explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). Conclusion: null, not discussed.
