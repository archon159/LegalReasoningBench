**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Analysis of canonical doctrine elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated in it, and it must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies")
- scope_participation: implied yes (applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (no suggestion otherwise; Article 11 protection is applied)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the necessity paragraph, and the opening paragraph describes "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence" as the measures complained of. The existence of interference is thus explicitly acknowledged, though not analyzed in depth as a separate doctrinal step.

Quote: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

Also: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence."

Conclusion: yes, interference is explicitly discussed (implied by the necessity analysis, and explicitly named in the complaint description).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The Court jumps directly to necessity. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive doctrinal element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) without further elaboration.

Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society — this is the violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is not discussed.
