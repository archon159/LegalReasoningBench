**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: 'The foregoing considerations are sufficient to enable the Court to conclude that there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The existence of an assembly (a demonstration) is treated as an established fact throughout the judgment. The applicant 'organised a demonstration' on 4 July 2002, and the Court refers to it as a 'demonstration' and 'peaceful assembly' without questioning whether it qualifies as an assembly. This is implied by the Court's explicit discussion of interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The applicant's participation and organisation of the demonstration is not disputed. The Court notes: 'The applicant argued that his 2008-2009 conviction in relation to the demonstration on 4 July 2002 had amounted to an interference with his right to organise a peaceful demonstration and to take part in it.' The Court proceeds on this basis without questioning it, so participation is implied.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court does not explicitly find the assembly to be non-peaceful in the sense of violence. However, it notes that 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings.' The Court treats the assembly as peaceful (no violence), but finds that the applicant's conduct (blocking traffic) went beyond what is protected. The Court states: 'The freedom to take part in a peaceful assembly does not encompass a person's choice to commit reprehensible acts on such an occasion.' The assembly is implicitly treated as peaceful (no violence), but the conduct was found to exceed the bounds of protected assembly. The peaceful character is implied rather than explicitly resolved as a threshold question.

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference: 'For the Court, the fact that the applicant was convicted on account of his actions during the demonstration is sufficient to conclude that there was an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly (see Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania [GC], no. 37553/05, § 101, 15 October 2015; Barraco, cited above, § 39).' This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The Court explicitly addresses this: 'The Court notes at the outset that the interference had a legal basis in national law, namely section 260(1) of the Criminal Code (this provision having already been held relevant in a similar situation, which occurred in October 1990 (see paragraphs 16 and 17 above)), and was thus "prescribed by law" with sufficient foreseeability within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.'

**(i) Foreseeable?** Explicitly discussed and confirmed: 'prescribed by law with sufficient foreseeability within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.' The reference to the provision 'having already been held relevant in a similar situation' supports foreseeability.

**(ii) Accessible?** Not explicitly discussed. The Court only mentions foreseeability and legal basis, not accessibility as a separate sub-element. This is not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse?** Not explicitly discussed. Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
Explicitly discussed: 'The Court is also satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aim of the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, with a view to preventing disorder and maintaining the orderly circulation of traffic (see Éva Molnár, cited above, § 34; Bukta, cited above, § 30; and Kudrevičius, cited above, § 140).' The aims found are: protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and prevention of disorder.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the proportionality principle and concludes the interference was not disproportionate: 'In light of the above, and balancing the public interest in the prevention of disorder against the interest of the applicant in choosing a particular form of assembly, the applicant's conviction and rather lenient sentence for the criminal offence does not appear disproportionate to the aims pursued.' Key findings include: the element of spontaneity was not present; the blockade caused massive traffic disruption; the applicant failed to show flexibility; the national courts conducted a thorough proportionality assessment; the applicant caused more obstruction than normally arises from peaceful assembly; and the conviction was for obstruction of traffic, not for participation in the demonstration. The Court finds necessity is satisfied (i.e., the interference was necessary), so there is no violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state to facilitate or protect the assembly. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (justification of interference) analysis. Positive obligation is not discussed.
