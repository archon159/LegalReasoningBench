**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the conclusion section:

- Paragraph (c) Conclusion: 'Having regard to the findings in paragraphs 39 and 42 above, the Court considers that the respondent State breached the requirements of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, seen in the light of Article 10. There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court discusses this explicitly, though in a nuanced way. In section (a) on solo demonstrations, the Court notes the applicants' submission that they staged solo demonstrations. In section (b) on spontaneous assembly, the Court states: 'Alternatively, should it be accepted as convincingly established that the applicants did indeed take part in an "assembly" (with each other and/or others), seen in the context of Article 11 of the Convention, the present case falls within the scope of matters relating to so-called "spontaneous assemblies".' The Court does not definitively resolve whether there was a single assembly or multiple solo demonstrations — it proceeds on both hypotheses. However, the domestic courts classified the event as a group 'picket,' and the Court's analysis under Article 11 implicitly accepts that at minimum the applicants' conduct fell within the scope of Article 11. The assembly question is explicitly discussed but left somewhat open (the Court proceeds on both hypotheses).

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly notes that the applicants participated in the demonstrations/assembly. In section (a): 'the Court has taken note of the applicants' submission that they attempted to stage solo demonstrations.' In section (b): 'The Court observes that the applicants wanted to protest against a draft law prohibiting the adoption of Russian children by US nationals.' Participation is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses this. In section (a): 'each applicant's expressive conduct was peaceful and non-disruptive.' In the applicants' submissions: 'The applicants stressed that even if their events were considered to be "unlawful" they had acted peacefully and had not created any disturbance.' The peaceful nature is explicitly confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. In section 2 (The Court's assessment): 'The parties agreed that the termination of the applicants' demonstrations and/or their participation in a group public event, their escorting to the police station and their administrative conviction had constituted an interference with their rights to freedom of peaceful assembly and freedom of expression.' Interference is explicitly found and agreed upon by both parties.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly notes that the questions of compliance with the law and legitimate aim cannot be dissociated from necessity and therefore need not be examined separately: 'in this case the questions of compliance with the law and of the existence of a legitimate aim cannot be dissociated from the question of whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society". It is therefore unnecessary to examine them separately.' The Court does not separately resolve whether the interference was prescribed by law, foreseeable, accessible, or came with adequate safeguards. However, in section (a), the Court does discuss the 'reclassification rule' under section 7(1.1) of the Public Events Act and its application, implying some engagement with the legal basis. The Court's reference to the 'reclassification rule' and its critique of it suggests concerns about the quality of the law (particularly safeguards), but no explicit conclusion is reached on prescribed by law as a standalone test.

- Foreseeable: not explicitly discussed as a standalone sub-element; null.
- Accessible: not explicitly discussed; null.
- Safeguards: The Court implicitly raises concerns about safeguards when it criticizes the 'reclassification rule' and the automatic/inflexible application of notification time-limits, but does not explicitly conclude on this sub-element separately.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states it is unnecessary to examine legitimate aim separately: 'the questions of compliance with the law and of the existence of a legitimate aim cannot be dissociated from the question of whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society". It is therefore unnecessary to examine them separately.' However, in section (a), the Court mentions 'the intended purposes of such a provision (such as affording the authorities an opportunity to take timely and adequate measures to ensure that a given civic initiative could take place in an orderly manner and to secure public safety and protect the rights of the event participants and others)' — this implicitly references public safety and rights of others as potential legitimate aims. The Court also mentions 'no compelling considerations relating to public safety, prevention of disorder or protection of the rights of others were at stake.' These references imply that public safety, prevention of disorder, and rights of others were the candidate aims, but no formal conclusion is reached. The legitimate aim element is not formally resolved.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and resolved. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society.

In section (a): 'no compelling considerations relating to public safety, prevention of disorder or protection of the rights of others were at stake and relied upon when using this factual assertion for convicting each applicant specifically on account of non-observance of the notification procedure... The only relevant consideration was the need to punish them for unlawful conduct arising solely from the fact that they had not complied with the notification procedure. In the absence of any aggravating element, this was not sufficient to justify the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of expression in the circumstances of the case.'

In section (b): 'the Government have not given any convincing reason why it should have been "necessary in a democratic society" to make no exceptions to the application of the notification procedure... the automatic and inflexible application of the notification time-limits without any regard to the specific circumstances of the present case amounted to an interference which was not justified under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.'

Necessity is explicitly discussed and found not satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). Positive obligation is not discussed.
