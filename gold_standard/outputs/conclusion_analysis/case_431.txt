**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and describes "the dispersal of these assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without questioning whether the assembly was outside the scope of protection). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: The opening paragraph states the applicants were "organisers or participants of public assemblies" — this implies an assembly existed, but is not explicitly analyzed as a legal question.
- **scope_participation**: Similarly implied by the description of the applicants as "organisers or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: Not explicitly discussed, but implied by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis without excluding the assemblies from protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the opening paragraph describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. Interference is thus explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. None of the three sub-elements are discussed explicitly or even implied in the reasoning text provided.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches a negative conclusion on necessity (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which constitutes the basis for the violation finding. The Court relies on its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and states it found "no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. There is no mention of a duty on the state to facilitate or protect the assembly. This element is not discussed.
