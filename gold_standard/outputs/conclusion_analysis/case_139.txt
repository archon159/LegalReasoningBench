**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been a peaceful assembly in which the applicants participated for Article 11 to be engaged at all. The opening paragraph also describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies," which implies the existence of assemblies and the applicants' participation. The peaceful nature is implied by the fact that the Court proceeds to analyze the case under Article 11 without excluding it from scope.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court proceeds with the full Article 11 analysis without suggesting the assemblies fell outside protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'," explicitly confirming that an interference occurred.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, which is the element on which it bases its violation finding. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it did not need to address prescribed by law.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and the general principles on proportionality cited (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova). The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is entirely absent.
