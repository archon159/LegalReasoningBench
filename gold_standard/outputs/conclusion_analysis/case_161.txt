**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's explicit finding of an "interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" logically implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated (as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," as stated in the opening paragraph), and the assembly must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

The opening paragraph does state: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This confirms participation and the existence of assemblies by implication.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the penultimate paragraph: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The existence of interference is thus explicitly confirmed, though the Court does not separately analyze it as a distinct step — it is treated as established. The opening paragraph also describes the measures (arrest, dispersal, conviction for administrative offences) that constitute the interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, implying (at most) that prescribed by law was not the dispositive issue, but it is not possible to infer a clear conclusion on this element from the text.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches a negative conclusion on necessity (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court relies on its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent.
