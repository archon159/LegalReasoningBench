**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court states that "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies" and refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly." This language implies that: (i) an assembly existed (the applicants organised and/or participated in "public assemblies"); (ii) the applicants participated (they are described as "organisers and/or participants"); and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (the Court proceeds to assess necessity under Article 11, which only applies to peaceful assemblies — if the assemblies had been non-peaceful, they would fall outside Article 11's protection entirely). None of these sub-elements are explicitly discussed or resolved as separate analytical steps.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate analytical step. The Court refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" as a given, stating: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The existence of interference is thus treated as established (implied by the reference to dispersal, arrest, and conviction described in the opening paragraph: "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as the applicants' arrest followed by their conviction for an administrative offence").

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" requirement is not discussed at all — neither explicitly nor by clear implication. The Court jumps directly to necessity without addressing foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. None of the three sub-elements are discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Legitimate aim is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the Russian authorities. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without first confirming that a legitimate aim was served. This element is therefore not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and states it found "no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion." The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
