**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court reached this conclusion on the basis that the interference was not prescribed by law, as stated earlier: "It follows that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly was not prescribed by law."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicant participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's explicit discussion of interference with the applicant's "right to freedom of peaceful assembly" implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — i.e., there was a peaceful assembly and the applicant participated in it. These elements are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to the applicant's "right to freedom of peaceful assembly" being interfered with, which presupposes an assembly existed)
- scope_participation: implied yes (the applicant was convicted in connection with the assembly, implying his participation)
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court refers throughout to "peaceful assembly" without questioning its peaceful character)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "It was not in dispute between the parties whether there had been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly. The Court considers that the fine imposed on the applicant by the decision of the President of the Criminal and Military Court of Appeal of 1 March 2003 undoubtedly interfered with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly." This is an explicit finding of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element at length. The overall conclusion is that the interference was NOT prescribed by law.

- Foreseeable: The Court explicitly addresses foreseeability, citing Mkrtchyan: "Article 180.1 of the CAO was not formulated with such precision as to enable the applicant to foresee, to a degree that was reasonable in the circumstances, the consequences of his actions, since there was no legal act applicable in Armenia which contained the 'prescribed rules' referred to in that provision." Conclusion: no (not foreseeable).

- Accessible: The Government argued the rules "were accessible and formulated with sufficient precision." The Court implicitly rejects this by finding the USSR Law of 28 October 1988 was no longer applicable, meaning there was effectively no accessible legal basis. However, the Court does not separately and explicitly analyze accessibility as a distinct sub-element — it focuses primarily on foreseeability/precision. The accessibility sub-element is discussed only in the context of the Government's submission and is implicitly rejected. Conclusion: the Court does not make a separate explicit finding on accessibility; it is implied no.

- Safeguards: The Court does not discuss safeguards against abuse as a distinct sub-element. Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states: "Having reached this conclusion, the Court does not need to verify whether the other two requirements (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) set forth in paragraph 2 of Article 11 have been complied with." Legitimate aim is therefore not examined. Conclusion: null, not discussed (beyond noting it is not examined).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Same as legitimate aim — the Court explicitly states it does not need to examine necessity. Conclusion: null, not discussed (beyond noting it is not examined).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning. Conclusion: not discussed.
