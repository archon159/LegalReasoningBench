**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 ECHR. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "There has therefore been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention, read in the light of Article 10."

The Court's reasoning leading to this conclusion is found in the proportionality analysis: "In view of the above considerations, the Court does not find it disproportionate that the domestic courts concluded that the applicants' actions amounted to an administrative offence and imposed small fines on them. The Court considers that the decisions of the national authorities in the present case were based on an acceptable assessment of the relevant facts and contained relevant and sufficient reasons which justified the interference with the applicants' right of assembly and freedom of expression. This interference was proportionate and necessary to prevent disorder or protect the rights and freedoms of others, within the meaning of the second paragraph of Articles 10 and 11."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly treats the gathering as an assembly throughout. The Court explicitly refers to "a public gathering" and "the event" repeatedly. In the interference section: "the applicants were negatively affected by the situation there has been an interference with the exercise of their freedom of peaceful assembly guaranteed by Article 11 § 1 of the Convention, this right being guaranteed to persons organising as well as participating in a public gathering." The existence of an assembly is treated as given and not disputed.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes participation: "the organisers decided to proceed with their event on the scheduled date in the planned location, and the applicants participated in it." The Court also notes: "this right being guaranteed to persons organising as well as participating in a public gathering." Participation is explicitly confirmed.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assembly was peaceful in the sense of examining whether it was violent or not. However, the Court consistently refers to it as a "peaceful assembly" and the entire analysis proceeds on the assumption that it was peaceful. The general principles section discusses the importance of tolerance toward "peaceful gatherings." This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a sub-question.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference: "In view of these considerations, the Court considers that since the applicants were negatively affected by the situation there has been an interference with the exercise of their freedom of peaceful assembly guaranteed by Article 11 § 1 of the Convention." The Court also notes: "In fact, the existence of the interference and the applicants' standing were not disputed by the respondent Government."

The Court identifies the interference as: the notification/endorsement procedure making the non-endorsed event unlawful, its dispersal, and the administrative offence proceedings (fines and detention at police station).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly states: "The Court observes that the interference in the present case was prescribed by law, namely the relevant provisions of the Public Gatherings Act and the Code of Administrative Offences providing for fines to be imposed for breaching the rules of the Act."

However, the Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse in any detail. The applicants had argued that the legislation did not meet the quality of law required (insufficient foreseeability regarding scope of discretion, no mechanism for resolving disagreements), but the Court does not engage with these sub-arguments in detail. The Court simply asserts the interference was prescribed by law without breaking it down into sub-elements. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not_discussed as separate questions.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly identifies legitimate aims in the proportionality section: "the Russian authorities referred to the security of the participants and the need to avoid causing obstruction to pedestrians and vehicles" and concludes "the national authorities acted within their margin of appreciation and provided sufficient reasons, referring to a legitimate aim, for opposing the event in the planned location." In the final paragraph: "This interference was proportionate and necessary to prevent disorder or protect the rights and freedoms of others, within the meaning of the second paragraph of Articles 10 and 11."

The aims found are: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights and freedoms of others (including public safety considerations regarding pedestrians and vehicles).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity/proportionality. Key passages include:
- "The Court considers that in the circumstances of the case the national authorities acted within their margin of appreciation and provided sufficient reasons, referring to a legitimate aim, for opposing the event in the planned location."
- "The Court does not find it disproportionate that the domestic courts concluded that the applicants' actions amounted to an administrative offence and imposed small fines on them."
- "The Court considers that the decisions of the national authorities in the present case were based on an acceptable assessment of the relevant facts and contained relevant and sufficient reasons which justified the interference with the applicants' right of assembly and freedom of expression. This interference was proportionate and necessary to prevent disorder or protect the rights and freedoms of others."
- The Court also notes the availability of an alternative venue: "both before and during the event the applicants were afforded, but did not use, an opportunity to express their views in another venue chosen by the public authority."

The Court concludes necessity IS satisfied (i.e., the interference was necessary/proportionate), so the answer to whether necessity was met is "yes" from the government's perspective — meaning the interference was justified.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this case. The analysis is entirely framed around whether the state's interference (a negative act) was justified. No positive obligation is spelled out or analyzed.
