**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the reasoning states: "There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the first applicant." This conclusion is reached after the Court finds that the domestic authorities failed to provide relevant and sufficient reasons to justify the interferences, and that "[i]t has not been convincingly demonstrated that the interferences were 'necessary in a democratic society' in pursuance of a legitimate aim."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied throughout the reasoning. The text refers to "the static demonstration," "holding a public event," and "the demonstration near the local FSB office" as the subject matter of the case. The existence of an assembly (or planned assembly) is treated as a given.

*Participation:* The Court implies the first applicant participated (or attempted to participate) in the assembly. The text states he "was required to abstain, under the threat of prosecution, from holding a public event as planned and was then prosecuted in that connection." This implies his participation/attempted participation is not in dispute.

*Peaceful:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the right invoked is "freedom of peaceful assembly" and the Court proceeds to analyze interference and justification without questioning the peaceful nature of the demonstration. The goal of the demonstration "was related to the second applicant's arrest and his ongoing detention on remand" — a political/expressive purpose. The peaceful character is implied by the Court's framing and by the fact that no violence or disorder is mentioned.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "The prosecution under the CAO amounted to an 'interference' under Article 11 § 2. In so far as the applicant was required to abstain, under the threat of prosecution, from holding a public event as planned and was then prosecuted in that connection, the municipality's decision of 23 November 2009 amounted to an 'interference' too (compare Karastelev and Others v. Russia, no. 16435/10, §§ 70-75, 6 October 2020, concerning a similar warning procedure under the anti-extremism legislation)." The conclusion is affirmative — there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to resolve the prescribed-by-law question: "The Court does not need to ascertain whether, as the courts adjudged, section 8 authorised restrictions relating to a venue of a public event in view of any 'specificities of the objects [объекты]' located in the vicinity of that venue, or banned such events near FSB buildings... For the reasons stated below the interferences were not convincingly shown to have been 'necessary in a democratic society' in pursuance of some legitimate aim under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." The Court thus skips the prescribed-by-law analysis entirely, proceeding directly to necessity. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are therefore not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim. It considers whether the interference served the aim of "preventing crime" (specifically, interfering with the administration of justice under Article 294 of the Criminal Code), but finds it not established: "Thus it has not been established that the interference was proportionate to the aim of 'preventing crime'." The Court also considers "public safety" and "prevention of disorder" but finds the decision was not "specifically circumscribed to address any precise risk to public safety or a precise risk of disorder." It also considers "protecting the rights of others" (parties to judicial proceedings) but notes the decision contained no reasoning on this. It explicitly notes that "maintaining the authority and impartiality of the judiciary" is not a listed aim under Article 11 § 2. The Court ultimately finds no legitimate aim was convincingly established: "It has not been convincingly demonstrated that the interferences were 'necessary in a democratic society' in pursuance of a legitimate aim." The conclusion on legitimate aim is effectively negative — no legitimate aim was sufficiently established.

Aims considered (but not found to be convincingly established): prevention_of_crime, public_safety, prevention_of_disorder, rights_of_others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses necessity. It states: "the interferences were not convincingly shown to have been 'necessary in a democratic society' in pursuance of some legitimate aim under Article 11 § 2." It further finds: "Faced with the domestic authorities' failure to provide relevant and sufficient reasons to justify the 'interferences', the Court finds that they cannot be said to have applied standards which were in conformity with the principles embodied in Article 11 or to have based themselves on an acceptable assessment of the relevant facts." The conclusion is negative — the necessity requirement was not met.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. The case is analyzed entirely as a negative obligation (interference by the state). Positive obligations are not mentioned.
