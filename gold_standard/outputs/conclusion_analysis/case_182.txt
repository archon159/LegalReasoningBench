**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *Assembly exists*: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *Participation*: Implied by the same passage — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- *Peaceful*: Implied — the Court finds an unjustified interference with freedom of assembly without any suggestion that the assemblies were non-peaceful; the leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The opening paragraph states: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming this element is found affirmatively.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor do any of the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) receive explicit treatment. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. Given that the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, this element is not discussed. It may be implied that the Court accepted this element (since it proceeds to necessity), but the text does not make this clear enough to treat it as "implied" with confidence — the Court's reasoning is extremely compressed and relies on leading cases. I will treat it as implied (yes) since the Court proceeds to the necessity stage, which logically presupposes that the interference was prescribed by law.

- *Foreseeable*: implied yes
- *Accessible*: implied yes
- *Safeguards*: implied yes

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss legitimate aim. As with prescribed by law, the Court's compressed reasoning — moving straight to necessity — implies that a legitimate aim was accepted, but no specific aim is identified in the text. The aims_found list is therefore empty.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation. The Court also references its proportionality case-law: "The Court refers to the principles established in its case-law regarding freedom of assembly... and proportionality of interference with it (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey... and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova...)."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed at all.
