**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies" — this implies that there were assemblies, that the applicants participated (or tried to), and that the assemblies were within the scope of Article 11. The reference to leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) further implies that the factual predicate of a protected assembly was satisfied. None of the three sub-elements are explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the complaint description and the Court's acceptance of the complaints as falling under Article 11.
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the description of applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the protection of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze whether there was an interference, but it is clearly implied. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" presupposes that an interference was found. Additionally, the complaint describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" — the Court's acceptance of these as interferences is implied by proceeding directly to necessity. This is implied rather than explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity, relying on its prior case-law in the leading cases. None of the sub-elements are discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No aims are identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is negative (the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent.
