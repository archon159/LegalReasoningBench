**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no reason to depart from those findings.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to analyze the interference under Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies fell outside its protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used directly, and the opening paragraph describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. These elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing whether the interference served a legitimate aim. This element is not_discussed, and no specific aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that the necessity requirement is NOT met (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation. This is explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is not_discussed.
