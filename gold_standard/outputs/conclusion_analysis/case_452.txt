**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated in respect of the applicants in applications nos. 37565/08, 37581/08, and 42250/08. The relevant passage is in the final paragraph of section B.2:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements, and was moreover not 'necessary in a democratic society'. There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the applicants in applications nos. 37565/08, 37581/08 and 42250/08."

Note: Application no. 39189/09 is examined under Article 10 (not Article 11), and a violation of Article 10 is found there. The complaint in application no. 42250/08 regarding Mr Nikolayev's arrest and detention on 24-26 November 2007 was declared inadmissible (manifestly ill-founded), but the remaining complaints in that application under Article 11 were found to constitute a violation.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's finding of a violation of Article 11 — and its reference to "the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" — implies that the scope requirements were satisfied. The admissibility section also notes that the complaints are not manifestly ill-founded, which implies the Court accepted that Article 11 was engaged. The partial inadmissibility ruling regarding Mr Nikolayev's arrest (application no. 42250/08) is instructive: the Court rejected that specific complaint precisely because it could not establish "a clear and acknowledged link between the measures taken against the applicant and his exercise of the freedom of peaceful assembly," which implies that for the other complaints, such a link was established. Thus scope is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **Assembly exists:** Implied (yes) — the Court proceeds on the basis that public events/assemblies took place.
- **Participation:** Implied (yes) — the Court finds interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly, implying they participated or attempted to participate.
- **Peaceful:** Implied (yes) — the Court applies Article 11 without any suggestion the assemblies were non-peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly states: "the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements." This is an explicit acknowledgment of interference. The Court also refers to "restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of the public events, as well as the allegedly unlawful arrest" in the introductory paragraph. Conclusion: yes, interference is explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this: "the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements." This is an explicit finding that the prescribed-by-law requirement was not satisfied. The Court does not separately discuss foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards as distinct sub-elements — it simply refers to the "quality of law" requirements not being met, by reference to the leading case of Lashmankin and Others. In Lashmankin, the Court examined all three sub-elements, but in this judgment they are not individually spelled out. The overall conclusion on prescribed by law is: no (the requirements were not met).

- **Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element; implied no (as part of the overall quality of law failure).
- **Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed; implied no.
- **Safeguards:** Not explicitly discussed; implied no.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not discuss legitimate aim at all. Given that it found the interference was not prescribed by law and not necessary in a democratic society, it did not need to resolve the legitimate aim question. No aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly states the interference was "moreover not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied. Conclusion: no (not necessary), explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. Not discussed.
