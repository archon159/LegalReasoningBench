**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits section:

'Having regard to the foregoing, the Court concludes that the domestic courts failed to adduce sufficient reasons to justify the proportionality of the interference in the present case.'

'There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention read in the light of Article 10.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly define or analyze whether an 'assembly' existed, but it is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court repeatedly refers to 'the demonstration of 29 November 2019,' describes it as 'part of a series of protests against Parliament's failure to approve electoral reform,' and notes that 'the demonstration itself was peaceful, with a great number of people participating.' The existence of an assembly is treated as a given, implied by the entire framing of the case.

**ii) Participation:** The Court implies that the applicant participated in the assembly. It states: 'By participating in the demonstration in question the applicant wished to express his disapproval of what he considered to be the authorities' failure to enhance the country's democratic process.' This is explicit as to participation.

**iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the demonstration: 'Furthermore, the demonstration itself was peaceful, with a great number of people participating.' As to the applicant's own conduct, the Court notes it was 'non-violent' and that 'His actions were neither violent nor did they cause any injuries to the police officers and could hardly be aimed at causing physical harm to them.' The Court also notes that 'the conduct in question did not result in any escalation of the circumstances on the ground.' The assembly and the applicant's participation are treated as falling within the scope of Article 11 protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly identifies the interference: 'the Court considers that the interference at issue resulted from the applicant's arrest at a demonstration and his subsequent conviction of administrative offences leading to a sanction of eight days' detention.' This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly states: 'The interference in question was "prescribed by law" as it was based on Article 173 of the CAO (see paragraph 28 above).' The Court accepts this element without further elaboration on foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed — the Court simply accepts the 'prescribed by law' requirement is met in a single sentence without breaking it down into sub-components.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly accepts the legitimate aim: 'The Court further accepts that, in the circumstances of the present case, the interference pursued the legitimate aim of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others.' The aims found are 'prevention of disorder' and 'protection of the rights and freedoms of others.'

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central focus of the Court's analysis and is explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court frames its task as determining 'whether the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly, considered in the light of the right to freedom of expression, answered a "pressing social need" and was "proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued."'

The Court finds necessity NOT established (i.e., the interference was not necessary in a democratic society). Key passages include:

- The demonstration concerned a matter of public interest: 'very strong reasons would be required to justify the restriction on the applicant's expression of his opinions during the demonstration.'
- The applicant's conduct was non-violent: 'His actions were neither violent nor did they cause any injuries to the police officers and could hardly be aimed at causing physical harm to them.'
- The chilling effect concern: 'the imposition of a sanction, however lenient, on a person expressing his or her opinion can have an undesirable chilling effect on public speech.'
- The domestic courts' reasoning was insufficient: 'none of the elements identified above were addressed as part of the domestic courts' reasoning regarding their decision to impose the custodial sanction.'
- The grounds cited (applicant's 'personality' and 'seriousness' of conduct) were insufficient: 'the Court does not consider that the grounds cited in the trial court's judgment – the applicant's "personality" and the "seriousness" of the conduct attributed to him – were sufficient, without further elaboration, to render a sanction of eight days' administrative detention proportionate.'
- Conclusion: 'the domestic courts failed to adduce sufficient reasons to justify the proportionality of the interference in the present case.'

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state to protect or facilitate the assembly. The entire analysis is framed as a negative obligation (interference by the state). Positive obligations are not discussed.

**Summary of findings:**
- Scope (overall): implied yes — the case is treated as falling within Article 11 scope throughout
- Assembly exists: implied yes
- Participation: explicit yes
- Peaceful: explicit yes (both the demonstration and the applicant's conduct characterized as non-violent)
- Interference: explicit yes
- Prescribed by law: explicit yes (accepted without sub-element analysis)
- Prescribed by law — foreseeable: not discussed separately
- Prescribed by law — accessible: not discussed separately
- Prescribed by law — safeguards: not discussed separately
- Legitimate aim: explicit yes — prevention of disorder and protection of rights of others
- Necessity: explicit no (violation found on this basis)
- Positive obligation: not discussed
