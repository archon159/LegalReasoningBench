**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of Section II.B states: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The reasoning leading to this conclusion is found throughout Section II.B (Merits).

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly treats the existence of an assembly as established. It refers to "the demonstration of 23 February 2003" and notes the applicant participated in "peaceful demonstrations." The Court states: "the applicant, whose actions were formally qualified as 'minor hooliganism', were in fact convicted for their participation in peaceful demonstrations." The existence of an assembly is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate sub-element.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses participation. It states: "the applicant, whose actions were formally qualified as 'minor hooliganism', were in fact convicted for their participation in peaceful demonstrations" and refers to "the mere fact of attending it, without committing any reprehensible acts." This is explicit.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. It refers to "peaceful demonstrations" and notes that the Court of Appeal "failed to provide details of any acts allegedly committed by the applicant at the demonstration of 23 February 2003 which could be characterised as reprehensible, including any violent or offensive acts." The Court also cites the principle from Ezelin: "the freedom to take part in a peaceful assembly is of such importance that a person cannot be subjected to a sanction... for participation in a demonstration which has not been prohibited, so long as this person does not himself commit any reprehensible act on such an occasion." The assembly is treated as peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "The Court does not see any reasons to reach a different finding in the present case and concludes that the decision of the President of the Criminal and Military Court of Appeal of 1 March 2003 constituted an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly." This is explicit and affirmative.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element: "The Court has already found, in similar circumstances, that an interference in the form of conviction under Article 172 of the CAO complied with the requirement of lawfulness (see Galstyan, cited above, § 107, and Ashughyan, cited above, §§ 81 and 82)." The Court finds the lawfulness requirement satisfied by reference to prior case law. However, the Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards in this judgment — it simply cross-references prior findings. The applicant had argued that "Article 172 of the CAO was too vague" (which relates to foreseeability), but the Court does not engage with this argument separately, instead relying on its prior findings in Galstyan and Ashughyan. The sub-elements are therefore not individually discussed in this judgment.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "similarly to those cases, the conviction in the present case pursued the legitimate aim of the 'prevention of disorder' (see Galstyan, cited above, § 110, and Ashughyan, cited above, § 85)." The aim found is prevention of disorder.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It cites the Ezelin principle: "the freedom to take part in a peaceful assembly is of such importance that a person cannot be subjected to a sanction – even one at the lower end of the scale of disciplinary penalties – for participation in a demonstration which has not been prohibited, so long as this person does not himself commit any reprehensible act on such an occasion."

The Court then finds: "the Court of Appeal failed to provide details of any acts allegedly committed by the applicant at the demonstration of 23 February 2003 which could be characterised as reprehensible, including any violent or offensive acts, and limited itself to a very abstract finding that the applicant had 'violated public order'." It also notes the absence of any legal framework for authorising demonstrations in Armenia at the material time.

The Court concludes: "the very essence of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly would be impaired, if the State chose not to prohibit a demonstration but subsequently imposed sanctions on its participants for the mere fact of attending it, without committing any reprehensible acts" and that "such interferences were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The necessity test is explicitly failed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is analyzed entirely through the negative obligation framework (interference and justification). This element is not discussed.
