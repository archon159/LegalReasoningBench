**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of the necessity assessment and the concluding sentence:

- "Accordingly, the Court concludes that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under Article 11 of the Convention, considered in the light of Article 10, was not 'necessary in a democratic society'."
- "There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. The applicants staged a protest at the Sultanahmet Courthouse on 18 November 2003, during which they "opened a banner, chanted slogans and threw leaflets." The Court treats this as a protest/assembly throughout its analysis. The existence of an assembly is explicitly implied by the entire framing of the case under Article 11, and the Court explicitly refers to it as a "protest" and "demonstration."

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses the applicants' participation: "the applicants were arrested, placed in pre-trial detention, prosecuted and convicted on the basis of their actions during their protest on 18 November 2003 at the Sultanahmet Courthouse." Participation is explicitly confirmed.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
This is explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court examines whether the applicants had violent intentions or committed violent acts. Key passages:
- "none of the witnesses who were present during that protest and who made statements during the ensuing criminal proceedings complained of having suffered any particular bodily harm or of any other kind of damage being inflicted, and nor did they allege that the applicants had engaged in any other kind of violent act."
- "the deputy public prosecutor's incident report dated 18 November 2003 did not note any damage caused by the applicants' conduct."
- "in the absence of any violent intention or violent conduct on the part of the applicants, those factors alone do not suffice for the impugned protest to fall outside the scope of Article 11 of the Convention."
- The Court concludes the protest was within the scope of Article 11, meaning it was sufficiently peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "Accordingly, there has been an interference with the applicants' exercise of their right to freedom of assembly on account of their arrest, detention, prosecution and conviction on the basis of their participation in a protest within the premises of the Sultanahmet Courthouse."

The interference is confirmed as: arrest, pre-trial detention, prosecution, and conviction.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court addresses this briefly but explicitly: "It is not disputed between the parties that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of expression had a legal basis under the domestic law – in particular under Article 113 § 1 of the Criminal Code and that the relevant law satisfied the quality-of-law requirements under the Convention."

The Court accepts that the prescribed-by-law requirement is met, but does so in a summary fashion without separately analyzing foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed — the Court simply states that the "quality-of-law requirements" are satisfied, which implies all three sub-elements are met but none is individually analyzed.

**i) Foreseeable:** Implied as satisfied by the general statement on quality-of-law requirements.
**ii) Accessible:** Implied as satisfied by the general statement on quality-of-law requirements.
**iii) Safeguards:** Implied as satisfied by the general statement on quality-of-law requirements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: "The Court further considers that the interference in question pursued the legitimate aims of protecting public safety and the rights and freedoms of others, and of preventing disorder."

Aims found: public safety, rights of others, prevention of disorder.

The Government also argued national security, health, and morals, but the Court only explicitly endorses public safety, rights of others, and prevention of disorder.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the pressing social need test and proportionality:
- "the Court concludes that the interference in the instant case corresponded to a pressing social need."
- However, on proportionality: "the Court cannot therefore discern, including from the domestic courts' decisions, any justification for sentencing each of the applicants – on account solely of their behaviour at the courthouse – to one year and eight months' imprisonment, which is a particularly severe prison sentence."
- "such lengthy prison sentences were not proportionate to the legitimate aims of protecting public safety and the rights and freedoms of others, or of preventing disorder."
- "all the applicants were also held in pre-trial detention for a period of at least one year, eight months and fourteen days – again very long periods."
- Conclusion: "the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under Article 11 of the Convention, considered in the light of Article 10, was not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), leading to the finding of a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by the state with the applicants' rights). No positive obligation is mentioned or implied.
