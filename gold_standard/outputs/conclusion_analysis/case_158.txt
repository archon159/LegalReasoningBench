**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies" — this implies that there were assemblies, that the applicants participated (or tried to), and that the assemblies were within the scope of Article 11. The reference to leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) further implies that the factual predicate of a protected assembly was satisfied. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the complaint description and the reference to leading cases involving assemblies.
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the description of applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without questioning whether the assemblies were peaceful, which logically presupposes they were.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and earlier the complaint is described as concerning "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Interference is thus explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not_discussed. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are likewise not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not_discussed, and no aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches a negative conclusion on necessity (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court references its case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman, Hyde Park) and leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) to support this conclusion without elaborating further.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not_discussed.
