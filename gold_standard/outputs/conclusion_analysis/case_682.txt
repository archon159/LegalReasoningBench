**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated in it, and (iii) the assembly was at least implicitly treated as peaceful (since the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without questioning the peaceful nature). The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov — all of which involved peaceful assemblies — further implies that the assemblies here were peaceful. Scope is therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the description "participants of public assemblies" and the reference to leading cases involving assemblies.
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the same description — the applicants are described as participants.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied by the Court's proceeding to analyze interference and necessity without any suggestion that the assemblies were violent or outside the protection of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in its conclusion. This confirms that interference is explicitly discussed and found.

Quote: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor do any of the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) receive explicit or implied treatment. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, referencing leading cases and concluding that the interferences were not necessary in a democratic society. Since the Court does not address prescribed by law, it is not discussed. This is consistent with the repetitive-case procedure where the Court focuses on the dispositive element (necessity) without working through every step of the analysis.

- **prescribed_by_law**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity conclusion. Legitimate aim is therefore not discussed, and no aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the explicit finding that drives the violation conclusion. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and the principle of proportionality (citing Oya Ataman and Hyde Park). The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). Positive obligation is not discussed.
