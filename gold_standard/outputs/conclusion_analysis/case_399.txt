**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is in the final paragraph of Section IV.B (Merits): "In the light of the foregoing considerations, the Court finds that there has been a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

Additionally, the preceding paragraph states: "Those findings suffice for the Court to conclude that there was an arbitrary interference with Mr I. Lutsenko's and Mr Y. Verbytskyy's right to freedom of peaceful assembly. There is nothing in the case file capable of demonstrating that the interference at issue, which consisted of the treatment examined under Articles 2 and 3 above (see paragraphs 90-93 above), was 'prescribed by law' or pursued a 'legitimate aim'. Nor is there any ground to suggest that it was 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly treats the Maidan protests as an assembly protected under Article 11. This is implied by the statement: "It is not disputed that Mr I. Lutsenko's and Mr Y. Verbytskyy's participation in the Maidan protests was protected under Article 11 of the Convention." The existence of an assembly is a logical precondition for this finding, though not separately articulated.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses participation: "It is not disputed that Mr I. Lutsenko's and Mr Y. Verbytskyy's participation in the Maidan protests was protected under Article 11 of the Convention." Further: "the Court reiterates its findings that Mr I. Lutsenko and Mr Y. Verbytskyy were abducted... in connection with their participation in the Maidan protests."

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the applicants' participation: "In the present case, there is no evidence to suggest that during their participation in the protests Mr I. Lutsenko or Mr Y. Verbytskyy intended to commit or engaged in acts of violence. Thus, the Court considers that they enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention in that connection." The Court also cites the principle that "an individual does not cease to enjoy the right to freedom of peaceful assembly as a result of sporadic violence or other punishable acts committed by others in the course of a demonstration if the individual in question remains peaceful in his or her own intentions or behaviour."

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: "Furthermore, the Court considers that there was an interference with the right of Mr I. Lutsenko and Mr Y. Verbytskyy to freedom of peaceful assembly on account of the abuses to which they were subjected in that connection... the Court finds that there are cogent and substantial elements demonstrating that those abuses were aimed at punishing or intimidating them on account of their involvement in the Maidan protests and/or preventing their further participation therein."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this element but in a negative sense — finding that the interference was NOT prescribed by law: "There is nothing in the case file capable of demonstrating that the interference at issue... was 'prescribed by law'..." The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed; the Court simply concludes the interference was not prescribed by law at all. These sub-elements are therefore implied (as logical components of the prescribed-by-law test) but not separately analyzed, and the overall conclusion is negative (no, not prescribed by law).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly addresses this element and finds no legitimate aim: "There is nothing in the case file capable of demonstrating that the interference at issue... pursued a 'legitimate aim'." No specific aim from the Article 11(2) list is identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly addresses this element and finds the interference was not necessary: "Nor is there any ground to suggest that it was 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is stated briefly without detailed proportionality analysis, given the prior findings that the interference was not prescribed by law and had no legitimate aim.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court touches on positive obligations implicitly when it notes: "These circumstances clearly called for the authorities to take appropriate measures in order to ensure the peaceful conduct of the rallies and the safety of all citizens" and "there is no information that appropriate measures were taken to restore peace or weed out the violent strand of protesters." However, the Court does not frame this as a standalone positive obligation analysis under Article 11; rather, it forms part of the contextual background. The primary analysis is framed as a negative obligation (interference) case. Positive obligation is therefore implied but not explicitly concluded upon as a separate doctrinal element.
