**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **(i) Assembly exists:** Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies." The Court treats this as given.
- **(ii) Participation:** Implied — the applicants are described as "organisers and participants of public assemblies," and the Court proceeds on this basis without questioning it.
- **(iii) Peaceful:** Implied — the Court finds an interference with "freedom of assembly" without suggesting the assemblies fell outside Article 11's protection due to violence. The leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies, reinforcing this implication.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." The Court then confirms: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming this element is established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, which technically presupposes that the interference was prescribed by law (otherwise the analysis would stop there), but the Court does not make this explicit. Given the Court's summary treatment — referencing leading cases and finding no new facts or arguments — it appears the "prescribed by law" element is simply not addressed, rather than implied. It is best classified as not_discussed, since the Court does not even implicitly resolve it.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is mentioned. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court also references its proportionality case-law: "The Court refers to the principles established in its case-law regarding freedom of assembly... and proportionality of interference with it (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey... and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova...)." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
