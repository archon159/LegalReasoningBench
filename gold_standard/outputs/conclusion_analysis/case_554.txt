**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The reasoning leading to this conclusion is found in the preceding paragraph: "The foregoing considerations are sufficient to enable the Court to conclude that the applicant's conviction, by relying solely on the ban, without any consideration of the specific circumstances of the case, cannot be regarded as necessary within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention."

**Question 2: Canonical Elements**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied throughout the merits section. The Court refers to "the static demonstration," "the public event planned by the applicant," and "the demonstration" throughout, treating the existence of an assembly as a given. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

*ii) Participation:* The Court implies the applicant participated (or attempted to participate) in the assembly. It notes that "the applicant, considering that the authorities' proposals did not answer the purpose of the demonstration, decided to hold it as initially planned. Afterwards, he was arrested and convicted." This implies participation but is not explicitly discussed as a separate doctrinal element.

*iii) Peaceful:* The applicant explicitly argued that "the authorities had not provided any evidence to show that the demonstration had not been peaceful." The Court does not explicitly make a finding on peacefulness as a separate element, but by proceeding to analyze the interference and its justification, it implicitly accepts the assembly was peaceful. The reference to "freedom of peaceful assembly" in the interference paragraph also implies this.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "The Court thus concludes that there has been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly (see Lashmankin and Others v. Russia, nos. 57818/09 and 14 others, §§ 407-08, 7 February 2017)." The Court identifies two acts constituting interference: the refusal to approve the location and the subsequent arrest and conviction under Article 20.2 § 2 of the Code of Administrative Offences.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the "prescribed by law" requirement as a separate element. It notes that "[t]he applicant's conviction under Article 20.2 § 2 of the Code of Administrative Offences on account of holding the demonstration opposite the Constitutional Court of the Republic of Tatarstan had its basis in the statutory ban on holding public events in the vicinity of court buildings." This acknowledges a legal basis exists, but the Court does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. Instead, it moves directly to necessity, citing Lashmankin where the ban was found incompatible with Article 11 § 2. The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. The Government argued that the authorities acted to avoid "a threat to public order, the rights and interests of third parties and the participants' security," which could correspond to public safety, prevention of disorder, or rights of others. However, the Court does not make an explicit finding on legitimate aim, proceeding directly to necessity. Legitimate aim is therefore not discussed as a separate element.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses necessity: "The foregoing considerations are sufficient to enable the Court to conclude that the applicant's conviction, by relying solely on the ban, without any consideration of the specific circumstances of the case, cannot be regarded as necessary within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." The Court also references Lashmankin, where it had already held that "such a general ban is so broadly drawn that it cannot be accepted as compatible with Article 11 § 2." The Court finds that necessity is NOT satisfied — i.e., the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
