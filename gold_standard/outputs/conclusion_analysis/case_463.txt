**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and describes "the dispersal of these assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and ultimately finds a violation). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: The opening paragraph states the applicants were "organisers or participants of public assemblies" — this implies an assembly existed, but is not explicitly analyzed as a legal sub-element.
- **scope_participation**: The same passage implies participation, but again not explicitly analyzed.
- **scope_peaceful**: Not discussed explicitly. The Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis and find a violation implies the assemblies were peaceful, but this is not stated.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" confirms that interference is found, though it is not separately analyzed as a distinct legal step — it is treated as established and then assessed for justification.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. These sub-elements are not discussed and no conclusion is drawn on them.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing this element.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and the general principles from Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania and Oya Ataman v. Turkey, without conducting a detailed proportionality analysis in the text provided.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. There is no mention of a duty on the state to facilitate or protect the assembly. This element is entirely absent.
