**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity, which logically presupposes the assembly was peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply).

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the necessity discussion: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly acknowledged, though not separately analyzed as a distinct step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity, relying on its prior case-law. These elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim pursued by the interference. No aims from Article 11(2) are mentioned. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches a negative conclusion on necessity (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court relies on its prior leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and finds no fact or argument capable of leading to a different conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is not discussed.
