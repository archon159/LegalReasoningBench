**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Analysis of canonical doctrine elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and its description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The Court would not have proceeded to assess interference and necessity without implicitly accepting that the assemblies existed, that the applicants participated, and that the assemblies were peaceful (since the Court applies Article 11 protection only to peaceful assemblies). These are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether assemblies existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were non-peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The Court thus explicitly treats these acts as interferences, though it does not separately reason through whether they constitute interference — it takes this as given and proceeds directly to necessity. This is best characterized as explicit (the word "interferences" is used directly), though the analysis is brief.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and the general principles from Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania, Oya Ataman v. Turkey, and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova. The conclusion is that necessity is NOT satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately justify the interference), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
