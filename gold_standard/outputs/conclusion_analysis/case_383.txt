**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated or tried to participate, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without any suggestion that the assembly fell outside Article 11 protection). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" and "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the description of applicants as "organisers or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied by the Court's proceeding to assess the interference and necessity without any suggestion that the assembly was violent or outside the scope of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the Court also describes in the opening paragraph "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, these sub-elements remain not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It does not state which of the aims listed in Article 11(2) the interference was said to pursue. The legitimate aim element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied, constituting the basis for the violation. The Court references its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) as the analytical foundation, and also cites the general principles from Kudrevičius and Others and the proportionality principles from Oya Ataman and Hyde Park.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
