**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is found in the final paragraph of the Court's assessment (Section B.2):

"In conclusion, the Court considers that the Moldovan authorities failed to take appropriate police measures in order to protect the applicants from violent attack and to effectively investigate the circumstances of the incident. Therefore, the Court considers that the State has failed to comply with their positive obligations under Article 11 of the Convention and that there has been a violation of that Article."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the gathering constitutes an 'assembly' in the legal sense, but it is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to "the demonstration of 3 February 2009" and "the participants at the demonstration" without questioning whether it qualifies as an assembly. This is implied by the Court's entire analysis proceeding under Article 11.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Again not explicitly discussed as a legal question, but implied. The Court refers to the applicants as participants in the demonstration: "the participants at the demonstration of 3 February 2009 were attacked by a group of six men wearing masks" and "Some of the participants in the demonstration, including the third applicant, suffered injuries."

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court implies the assembly was peaceful. It refers to "peaceful demonstrators" (in the applicants' submissions section) and frames the entire case as one where the demonstrators were victims of violence rather than perpetrators. The Court also contrasts the present case with the Hyde Park cases involving "peaceful demonstrations." The assembly's peaceful character is implied rather than explicitly adjudicated as a separate legal question.

**(b) Interference**
The Court does not frame the case in terms of a classic 'interference' by the state with the right to assembly. Instead, the entire analysis is conducted through the lens of positive obligations. The Court notes the failure of police to intervene to protect the demonstrators: "A police patrol in a car parked not far from the place of the event was observing the incident but did not intervene." The state's omission (failure to protect) is treated as the relevant state action, but it is analyzed under positive obligations rather than as a classic interference. Interference in the traditional sense (state restricting assembly) is not explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
Not discussed. Since the case is analyzed exclusively through the positive obligations framework rather than as a restriction on assembly, the 'prescribed by law' test is not engaged. There is no state restriction to justify.

- Foreseeable: not discussed
- Accessible: not discussed
- Safeguards: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**
Not discussed. Again, because the case proceeds entirely on positive obligations, the Court does not examine whether any state action pursued a legitimate aim.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Not discussed as a formal test. The case does not involve a restriction that needs to be justified as necessary. The proportionality/necessity analysis is subsumed into the positive obligations assessment.

**(f) Positive obligation**
This is the central and explicitly discussed element of the judgment.

The Court explicitly articulates the positive obligation framework:
"Genuine, effective freedom of peaceful assembly cannot, therefore, be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere: a purely negative conception would not be compatible with the object and purpose of Article 11. Like Article 8, Article 11 sometimes requires positive measures to be taken, even in the sphere of relations between individuals, if need be (see, Plattform 'Ärzte für das Leben' v. Austria, 21 June 1988, § 32, Series A no. 139)."

"Where individuals act in a way that undermines Article 11 rights, national authorities may be required to intervene in relationships between such private individuals to secure their protection. This may include the need for active police measures to secure such Article 11 rights and furthermore entails the obligation to investigate violent incidents affecting the exercise of those rights."

The Court then finds that the positive obligation was violated:
"In conclusion, the Court considers that the Moldovan authorities failed to take appropriate police measures in order to protect the applicants from violent attack and to effectively investigate the circumstances of the incident. Therefore, the Court considers that the State has failed to comply with their positive obligations under Article 11 of the Convention and that there has been a violation of that Article."

The Court found the positive obligation violated on two grounds: (1) failure to take appropriate police measures to protect the demonstrators from violent attack (noting the 1.5-hour delay despite police being nearby and being called), and (2) failure to effectively investigate (only 2 of 6 attackers convicted, no investigation into who sponsored the attack, no identification of individuals filming the demonstration).
