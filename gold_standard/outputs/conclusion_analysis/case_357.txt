**Question 1: Has Article 11 ECHR been violated?**

Yes. The Court explicitly concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Analysis of canonical Article 11 elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated, otherwise there would be no freedom of assembly to interfere with. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 protection without any suggestion that the assemblies were non-peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming this element is found. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed. Given that the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis and finds a violation there, it appears the Court did not need to address the prescribed-by-law requirement (consistent with its practice in repetitive cases resolved by reference to leading cases). These elements are not_discussed, with conclusion null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. This element is not_discussed. However, since the Court proceeds to the necessity analysis, one might infer that a legitimate aim was assumed, but this is not stated. Given the Court's silence, aims_found is an empty list.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" (the interference was not necessary), which is what grounds the violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not_discussed.
