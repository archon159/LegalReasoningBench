## Analysis of the Judgment

### 1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR

The Court finds violations of Article 11 in respect of all applicants, but through two different routes:

**For Deniz Bakır, Metin Kürekçi, Necla Çomak and Alihan Alhan (convicted under Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code):**
"In the light of the aforementioned considerations, the Court concludes that Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code was not 'foreseeable' in its application, since it did not afford the applicants the legal protection against arbitrary interference with their rights under Article 11 of the Convention... Hence, the interference resulting from the application of Article 220 § 7 was not prescribed by law... Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of Deniz Bakır, Metin Kürekçi, Necla Çomak and Alihan Alhan."

**For Levent Çakır, Uğur Güdük, Latife Canan Kaplan, Serdar Kır, Selçuk Mart, Mehmet Ali Tosun, Filiz Uluçelebi and Mesut Açıkalın (convicted under section 7(2) of Law no. 3713):**
"The foregoing considerations are sufficient to enable the Court to conclude that the criminal conviction of the applicants for disseminating propaganda in support of the MLKP under section 7(2) of Law no. 3713 was not 'necessary in a democratic society'. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of Levent Çakır, Uğur Güdük, Latife Canan Kaplan, Serdar Kır, Selçuk Mart, Mehmet Ali Tosun, Filiz Uluçelebi and Mesut Açıkalın."

---

### 2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine

#### (a) Scope

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the events constituted an "assembly" within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the judgment. The Court repeatedly refers to the applicants' "participation in demonstrations" and their "conduct therein" as the basis for their convictions. For example: "Having regard to the fact that the applicants' criminal convictions were mostly based on their participation in demonstrations and their conduct therein, the Court considers that these complaints should be examined from the standpoint of Article 11 of the Convention." The existence of an assembly is thus implied.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Participation is implied throughout. The Court notes: "the applicants' criminal convictions were mostly based on their participation in demonstrations and their conduct therein." The Court also describes specific acts of participation: "the applicants were present at a legally organised demonstration, had chanted slogans, carried banners, worn clothes and carried pennants with 'ESP' written on them and had had red ribbons attached to their arms during that demonstration." This is implied rather than explicitly framed as a separate doctrinal question.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court explicitly addresses this in the context of the second group of applicants: "There is no indication in the case file that the demonstrations in the present case were not peaceful or that the demonstrators engaged in acts of violence." The applicants themselves also stressed that "the demonstrations in question had been lawful and peaceful." This is explicitly discussed.

#### (b) Interference

The Court explicitly finds interference: "The Court finds that the applicants' criminal convictions for membership of the MLKP and for dissemination of propaganda in support of the MLKP, as well as the imposition of prison sentences on them, constituted an interference with the exercise of their right to freedom of assembly, as guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention."

Also in the admissibility section: "the applicants' criminal convictions under Article 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code, on the basis of Articles 220 § 7 and 314 § 3 of the same Code, and section 7(2) of Law no. 3713, amounted to an 'interference' with the exercise of their right to freedom of assembly."

#### (c) Prescribed by law

The Court explicitly discusses this element, with different conclusions for the two groups.

**i) Foreseeability:**
For the first group (Art. 220 § 7), the Court extensively discusses foreseeability and concludes it was not met: "the Court concludes that Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code was not 'foreseeable' in its application, since it did not afford the applicants the legal protection against arbitrary interference with their rights under Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court also notes: "not only is Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code formulated without a definition of the expressions contained therein, but also the domestic courts have seemingly not developed a consistent judicial interpretation of that provision in the context of demonstrations."

For the second group (section 7(2) of Law no. 3713), the Court notes: "While questions could arise with respect to foreseeability in terms of the manner in which that provision would be applied, in the light of its examination below regarding the 'necessity' of the interference in a democratic society... the Court considers that it is not required to reach a final conclusion on the lawfulness issue." So foreseeability is discussed but left unresolved for this group.

**ii) Accessibility:**
For the first group, the Court explicitly states: "The Court considers that there is no doubt that Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code was accessible." So accessibility is explicitly found to be satisfied.

For the second group, accessibility is not separately discussed.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse:**
The Court discusses this as part of the foreseeability analysis for the first group: "the array of acts that potentially constitute evidence for the application of a severe criminal sanction in the form of imprisonment, under Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code, is so vast that the wording of the provision, including its extensive interpretation by the domestic courts, does not afford a sufficient measure of protection against arbitrary interference by the public authorities." This is treated as part of the foreseeability/quality of law analysis rather than a separate safeguards element, but it is explicitly discussed.

#### (d) Legitimate aim

For the first group (Art. 220 § 7), the Court explicitly states it is not required to determine legitimate aim: "That being so, the Court is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was necessary in a democratic society."

For the second group (section 7(2) of Law no. 3713), the Court explicitly accepts legitimate aims: "The Court is prepared to accept that the national authorities may be considered to have pursued the legitimate aims of protecting national security and preventing disorder and crime."

The Government also submitted: "the interference in question pursued the aims of protecting national security, territorial integrity and public safety, as well as prevention of disorder or crime."

So legitimate aim is explicitly discussed for the second group, with national security, prevention of disorder, and prevention of crime accepted. For the first group, it is explicitly not examined.

#### (e) Necessity in a democratic society

For the first group (Art. 220 § 7), the Court explicitly states it is not required to examine necessity: "That being so, the Court is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was necessary in a democratic society."

For the second group (section 7(2) of Law no. 3713), the Court explicitly examines necessity and finds it was not met: "the Court considers that the Government have not put forward any argument which would require it to reach a different conclusion in the present case." The Court notes the domestic courts failed to examine whether the applicants' conduct had any impact on public order, that the demonstrations were peaceful, and that the applicants were not advocating violence. The Court also considers the severity of the penalties: "the applicants' acts, by which in any case they did not advocate violence or the infliction of injury or harm on anyone, were not at all of a gravity that would justify the imposition of such lengthy prison sentences." Conclusion: "the criminal conviction of the applicants for disseminating propaganda in support of the MLKP under section 7(2) of Law no. 3713 was not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

#### (f) Positive obligation

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by the state through criminal prosecution and conviction). Positive obligations are not mentioned.

### Summary of structured conclusions

Given that the case involves two groups with different analyses, I will reflect the overall picture:
- **Scope (overall):** Implied yes — the Court treats the events as demonstrations falling within Article 11 without explicit discussion of scope as a threshold question.
- **Assembly exists:** Implied yes.
- **Participation:** Implied yes.
- **Peaceful:** Explicitly discussed and found yes (at least for the second group; implied for the first).
- **Interference:** Explicitly found yes.
- **Prescribed by law (overall):** Explicitly discussed. For first group: no (not foreseeable). For second group: left open. Overall conclusion: mixed, but since the Court finds a violation on this ground for some applicants, I mark it as "no" (not satisfied for those applicants).
- **Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed. No for first group; unresolved for second group.
- **Accessible:** Explicitly found yes for first group; not discussed for second group.
- **Safeguards:** Explicitly discussed as part of foreseeability analysis; found insufficient (no) for first group.
- **Legitimate aim:** Explicitly discussed for second group only; yes (national security, prevention of disorder, prevention of crime). Not examined for first group.
- **Necessity:** Explicitly discussed for second group; found no (not necessary). Not examined for first group.
- **Positive obligation:** Not discussed.

Note: Since the overall result is a violation for all applicants (though via different routes), violated = true. For the structured elements, I reflect the dominant/overall picture across both groups, noting that "prescribed by law" was found not satisfied (for the first group) and "necessity" was found not satisfied (for the second group).
