**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of Section B.3: 'Accordingly, the Court concludes that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court reached this conclusion primarily on the basis that the interference was not 'prescribed by law,' without needing to examine the other requirements of Article 11 § 2.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses whether the bi-communal meetings constitute 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11. In Section B.2 (General Principles), the Court states: 'the right to freedom of assembly is a fundamental right in a democratic society... it should not be interpreted restrictively... this right covers both private meetings and meetings in public thoroughfares as well as static meetings and public processions.' In Section B.3, the Court finds: 'the Court considers that, despite the varied nature of the meetings the applicant wished to attend, they all shared a core characteristic: they were bi-communal. Thus, irrespective of the form they took and by whom they were organised, their aim was the same, namely, to bring into contact Turkish Cypriots living in the north and Greek Cypriots living in the south with a view to engaging in dialogue and exchanging ideas and opinions with the hope of securing peace on the island.' The Court thus explicitly affirms that these bi-communal meetings constitute assemblies within the scope of Article 11. The respondent Government had argued that gatherings such as exhibitions, festivals, concerts, fairs and receptions could not be characterised as 'assembly' under Article 11, but the Court rejected this narrow reading.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation (or attempted participation). In Section B.3: 'the Court considers that the refusals to grant permits to the applicant in order to cross into southern Cyprus in effect barred his participation in bi-communal meetings there, preventing him consequently from engaging in peaceful assembly with people from both communities.' The Court also notes that 'only six out of forty-six requests were granted,' confirming that the applicant repeatedly tried to participate. The Court also states: 'whether or not the applicant was to participate in these meetings as the Turkish-Cypriot coordinator of the Movement for an Independent and Federal Cyprus is irrelevant for determining the question of freedom of assembly, given the ambit of the right guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention.' This confirms participation is discussed explicitly.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court does not explicitly analyze whether the assemblies were peaceful as a separate sub-element. However, the Court repeatedly uses the phrase 'peaceful assembly' throughout its reasoning (e.g., 'preventing him consequently from engaging in peaceful assembly with people from both communities'; 'no law applicable in the present case regulating the issuance of permits to Turkish Cypriots living in northern Cyprus to cross the "green line" into southern Cyprus in order to engage in peaceful assembly with Greek Cypriots'). The peaceful nature of the assemblies is thus implied throughout — the Court treats the meetings as peaceful without making it a contested issue. The applicant himself described the meetings as aimed at 'peaceful discourse and intercourse' and 'peaceful political settlement.' This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate sub-element.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly concludes that there was an interference. In Section B.3: 'Accordingly, the Court concludes that there has been an interference with the applicant's right to the freedom of peaceful assembly guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court also notes: 'the Court observes that hindrance can amount to a violation of the Convention just like a legal impediment (see Loizidou (merits), cited above, p. 2237, § 63).' The interference consisted of the repeated refusals to grant permits to cross the 'green line' — 40 out of 46 requests refused, and a complete ban from 2 February 1996 to 14 April 1998 (10 consecutive refusals).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element and concludes the interference was NOT prescribed by law. In Section B.3: 'It must first be examined whether the restriction complained of was "prescribed by law". The Court reiterates that one of the requirements flowing from the expression "prescribed by law" is the foreseeability of the measure concerned. A rule cannot be regarded as "law" unless it is formulated with sufficient precision to enable the citizen to regulate his conduct: he must be able – if need be with appropriate advice – to foresee, to a degree that is reasonable in the circumstances, the consequences which a given action may entail.' The Court then finds: 'the respondent Government did not refer to any law or measures in the "TRNC" regulating the issuance of permits to Turkish Cypriots living in northern Cyprus to cross the "green line" into southern Cyprus for the purposes of attending bi-communal meetings. Furthermore, they did not provide any indication as to when refusal of such permits is allowed.' Conclusion: 'there seems to be no law applicable in the present case... Therefore, the manner in which restrictions were imposed on the applicant's exercise of his freedom of assembly was not "prescribed by law" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.'

**i) Foreseeability:** Explicitly discussed. The Court quotes the foreseeability standard: 'A rule cannot be regarded as "law" unless it is formulated with sufficient precision to enable the citizen to regulate his conduct.' Since no law was identified at all, foreseeability necessarily fails. Conclusion: no.

**ii) Accessibility:** Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element. Since the Court found there was no law at all, accessibility is not separately analyzed. It is implied (no law = not accessible), but not discussed as a distinct criterion. Conclusion: implied no.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court notes the Government 'did not provide any indication as to when refusal of such permits is allowed,' which touches on the absence of safeguards, but this is framed within the general 'prescribed by law' analysis rather than as a distinct safeguards sub-element. This is implied rather than explicit. Conclusion: implied no.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states it does not consider it necessary to examine whether the other requirements of Article 11 § 2 were satisfied, having found the interference was not prescribed by law: 'In the light of the above the Court does not consider it necessary to examine whether the other requirements laid down by Article 11 § 2 of the Convention were satisfied.' Therefore, legitimate aim is not discussed. The respondent Government had argued in their submissions that the exercise of the rights was subject to restrictions permitted under Article 11 § 2, but the Court did not engage with this. Conclusion: not discussed, no aims found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Not discussed, for the same reason as legitimate aim. The Court explicitly declined to examine the other requirements of Article 11 § 2 after finding the interference was not prescribed by law. Conclusion: not discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court mentions positive obligations in its general principles section (Section B.2): 'although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities with the exercise of the rights protected, there may in addition be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these rights (see Christians against Racism and Fascism, cited above, p. 148).' However, the Court does not apply this positive obligation framework to the facts of the case — it resolves the case on the basis of a negative obligation (the interference was not prescribed by law). The positive obligation is mentioned in the general principles but not applied or concluded upon in the application section. Conclusion: discussed (explicitly mentioned) but not applied/concluded upon — the Court does not find a separate positive obligation violation. I will mark this as 'explicit' in terms of discussion but 'null' in terms of conclusion since the Court does not reach a conclusion on it.
