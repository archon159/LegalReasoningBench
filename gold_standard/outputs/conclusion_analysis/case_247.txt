**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is in the merits section (B. Merits > 2. The Court's assessment):

"The Government did not submit that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly was based on a legal provision that was accessible and foreseeable in its application. Accordingly, the interference was not 'prescribed by law', a fact that suffices to constitute a violation of Article 11. This finding dispenses the Court from determining whether it pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society."

The Court found a violation on the basis that the interference was not prescribed by law, and did not proceed to examine legitimate aim or necessity.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "Therefore irrespective of whether the meeting in the present case was to be characterised as private or public it constituted an 'assembly', and being undeniably peaceful it fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court explicitly confirms an assembly existed.

**(ii) Participation:** Implied. The applicants are described as participants in and organisers of the meeting. The Court notes "the applicants could not proceed with their small-scale informal gathering" — their participation is taken as given and not separately questioned.

**(iii) Peaceful:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states the assembly was "undeniably peaceful" and further notes "Clearly, the applicants' meeting was not causing any disturbance." The peaceful character is explicitly confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "In the present case, the applicants could not proceed with their small-scale informal gathering in the presence of the large number of police officers who were accusing them of a failure to notify the authorities of their meeting and who remained in the room until the participants of the meeting left it. It is clear that the meeting was brought to an end prematurely by the authorities' intervention, which therefore constituted 'a restriction', within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention." Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed overall. The Court finds the interference was NOT prescribed by law.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed. The Court recites the standard: "the expression 'prescribed by law' not only requires that the impugned measure should have a legal basis in domestic law, but also refers to the quality of the law in question, which should be accessible to the person concerned and foreseeable as to its effects." The Court then notes that the legal grounds used (failure to notify under public events procedure) were refuted by the supervisory review, and doubts that a municipal instruction to school directors could give police the power to disrupt a gathering. The Government did not submit that the interference was based on a provision that was "accessible and foreseeable in its application." Conclusion: no, not foreseeable.

**(ii) Accessible:** Explicitly discussed. The Court notes the Government "did not submit that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly was based on a legal provision that was accessible and foreseeable in its application." The municipal instruction's text was not even submitted to the Court. Conclusion: no, not accessible.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Explicitly discussed in general terms. The Court states: "For domestic law to meet the qualitative requirements, it must afford a measure of legal protection against arbitrary interferences by public authorities with the rights guaranteed by the Convention. In matters affecting fundamental rights it would be contrary to the rule of law... for legal discretion granted to the executive to be expressed in terms of an unfettered power. Consequently, the law must indicate with sufficient clarity the scope of any such discretion and the manner of its exercise." The Court doubts the municipal instruction could give police such power, implying insufficient safeguards. Conclusion: no (implied from the overall finding that the interference was not prescribed by law).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed on the merits. The Court explicitly states: "This finding dispenses the Court from determining whether it pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society." Conclusion: null, not examined.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Not discussed. Explicitly dispensed with: "This finding dispenses the Court from determining whether it pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society." Conclusion: null, not examined.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed at all in the judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (interference by the state). Conclusion: null, not discussed.
