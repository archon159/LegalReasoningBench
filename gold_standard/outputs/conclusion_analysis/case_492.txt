**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits assessment:

"In the light of the above, the Court concludes that the domestic courts failed in their duty to provide reasons for their decisions and that the reasons adduced to justify the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly were not 'relevant and sufficient'."

"There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. It refers to "the assembly at Freedom Square" and "the post-election protest movement" and "demonstrations held at Freedom Square." The Court states: "the demonstrations held at Freedom Square were found by the Court to constitute a peaceful assembly and, in fact, a platform for expression on a matter of major political importance directly related to the functioning of a democracy." This is explicit.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation. It states: "the applicant's conviction for 'organising mass disorder' was, to a large extent, based on his involvement in the post-election protest movement, including his participation and speeches made at the assembly at Freedom Square." The applicant is described as an active organiser and participant. This is explicit.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses whether the assembly was peaceful: "the demonstrations held at Freedom Square were found by the Court to constitute a peaceful assembly" and "the assembly at Freedom Square...which the Court has already found to have been peaceful without any incitement to violence or acts of violence (see Mushegh Saghatelyan, cited above, § 245, and Myasnik Malkhasyan, cited above, § 72)." The Court also discusses the later violence on 1-2 March 2008 but concludes that "an individual does not cease to enjoy the right to peaceful assembly as a result of sporadic violence or other punishable acts committed by others in the course of the demonstration if the individual in question remains peaceful in his or her own intentions or behaviour." The Court ultimately finds the applicant remained peaceful. This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: "It therefore amounted to an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The interference identified is the applicant's prosecution and conviction for organising mass disorder, which was based on his involvement in the protest movement. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this question: "The Court, in the present case, does not consider it necessary to decide whether the interference was prescribed by law and pursued a legitimate aim having regard to its conclusions set out below, regarding the necessity of the interference." Therefore, the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not discussed. The overall prescribed-by-law question is mentioned but left unresolved (null conclusion). The sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this question as well, in the same passage: "The Court, in the present case, does not consider it necessary to decide whether the interference was prescribed by law and pursued a legitimate aim having regard to its conclusions set out below, regarding the necessity of the interference." No legitimate aim is identified or assessed. This is explicitly mentioned but left unresolved (null conclusion, no aims found).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It applies the "relevant and sufficient reasons" standard and concludes that the domestic courts failed to provide such reasons. Key passages include:

- "The Court reiterates that a criminal conviction for actions inciting to violence at a demonstration can be deemed to be an acceptable measure in certain circumstances... However, peaceful participants may not be held responsible for reprehensible acts committed by others."
- "the domestic courts have failed to provide sufficiently specific examples of reprehensible acts attributable to the applicant, including incitement or instigation of violence"
- "the applicant appears to have been sanctioned for being an active member of the opposition and for having availed himself of his right to freedom of peaceful assembly rather than committing any specific reprehensible acts"
- "the Court concludes that the domestic courts failed in their duty to provide reasons for their decisions and that the reasons adduced to justify the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly were not 'relevant and sufficient'."

The conclusion on necessity is negative (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society). This is explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state to protect or facilitate the assembly. The entire analysis is framed in terms of a negative obligation (the state must not unjustifiably interfere). No positive obligation is spelled out. This element is not discussed.
