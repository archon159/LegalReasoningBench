**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits assessment:

"Having regard to the above, the Court considers that in the instant case the forceful intervention of the police vis-à-vis the applicants was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder or the protection of the rights of others within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

"There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the applicants."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of assemblies. In the interference section: "the case file indicates that the applicants did participate in the demonstrations in question and that those demonstrations were dispersed by the police." The existence of an assembly is explicitly confirmed.

**(ii) Participation:** Also explicitly discussed in the same passage: "the case file indicates that the applicants did participate in the demonstrations in question." The Court rejects the Government's assertion that there was no evidence of participation.

**(iii) Peaceful character:** Explicitly discussed in the merits section: "The applicants claimed that the demonstrations of 14 and 21 May 2012 were peaceful." The Court then accepts this: "The Court therefore accepts the applicants' assertion that the demonstration had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner up until its dispersal (see Gafgaz Mammadov, cited above, § 61)." The Court also notes: "the case file does not contain any information to indicate that the applicants acted in a violent manner" and "There is no evidence to suggest that in the instant case the applicants or other demonstrators presented a danger to public order."

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed under the dedicated sub-heading "(a) Whether there was an interference": "Contrary to the Government's assertion, the case file indicates that the applicants did participate in the demonstrations in question and that those demonstrations were dispersed by the police. Accordingly there was an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly on account of the police intervention in and the dispersal of the assemblies." Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not resolve this element definitively. It explicitly discusses it but assumes it arguendo: "the Court holds that even assuming that the interference was 'prescribed by law' and pursued 'legitimate aims' within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Article 11 – namely the aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others – it remains to be determined whether it was 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court notes that the domestic courts' decisions indicated the demonstrations were dispersed because they were unauthorised, "although no explicit reference was made to the provisions of the domestic law in that connection." The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually discussed — the Court simply assumes the condition is met for the sake of argument. Conclusion: assumed yes (not resolved).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Also assumed arguendo rather than definitively resolved: "even assuming that the interference was 'prescribed by law' and pursued 'legitimate aims' within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Article 11 – namely the aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others." The aims identified (assumed) are prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others. This is explicit but not conclusively determined. The Court then confirms these aims in the necessity conclusion: "the forceful intervention of the police vis-à-vis the applicants was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder or the protection of the rights of others."

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed under "(b) Whether the interference was justified." The Court reiterates the standard: "there must be convincing and compelling reasons to justify an interference with this right." It then examines whether the interference was necessary, noting: the demonstrations were peaceful; the only reason for dispersal was lack of authorisation; there was no evidence of danger to public order; the authorities showed impatience; and there was no sign of tolerance. The Court concludes: "the forceful intervention of the police vis-à-vis the applicants was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder or the protection of the rights of others within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention." Conclusion: no, the interference was not necessary.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court briefly references positive obligations in passing: "States must not only safeguard the right to assemble peacefully, but must also refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon that right." However, the Court does not frame the case as one of positive obligation failure — it is primarily a negative obligation (unjustified interference) case. The positive obligation language is mentioned as part of the general restatement of principles but is not the basis of the finding. No separate positive obligation conclusion is reached. This is implied/mentioned but not the focus of the analysis.
