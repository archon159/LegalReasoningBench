**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of Section III.B (Merits): "Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court's reasoning leading to this conclusion is found in the Merits section: "In so far as the lawfulness of the above interference is concerned, no elements in the present case allow the Court to consider that there was a legal basis for limiting the applicants' right to freedom of assembly. Indeed, Article 191 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (see paragraph 23 above) does not provide for such a surety measure and the Government did not indicate to any other domestic legal provisions which would allow such a measure being imposed on a person released pending trial. This being so, the Court concludes that the interference in question was not lawful under domestic law. This conclusion makes it unnecessary to examine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and whether it was necessary in a democratic society."

**2. Analysis of canonical Article 11 elements**

**(a) Scope**

The scope elements are not explicitly discussed by the Court. However, they are implied by the Court's explicit finding of interference. The Court states: "It is undisputed between the parties, and the Court agrees, that the prohibition imposed on the applicants on participating in public gatherings amounted to an 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11." For there to be an interference with the right to freedom of assembly, there must logically be an assembly, participation (or attempted participation) in it, and the assembly must be peaceful. These sub-elements are therefore implied.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the prohibition on participating in public gatherings on 26 April 2016 presupposes the existence of an assembly.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants were prohibited from participating, implying they sought to participate.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court treats the matter as falling within Article 11's protection of "peaceful assembly," and there is no suggestion the assembly was non-peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed and confirmed. The Court states: "It is undisputed between the parties, and the Court agrees, that the prohibition imposed on the applicants on participating in public gatherings amounted to an 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11."

Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "In so far as the lawfulness of the above interference is concerned, no elements in the present case allow the Court to consider that there was a legal basis for limiting the applicants' right to freedom of assembly. Indeed, Article 191 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (see paragraph 23 above) does not provide for such a surety measure and the Government did not indicate to any other domestic legal provisions which would allow such a measure being imposed on a person released pending trial. This being so, the Court concludes that the interference in question was not lawful under domestic law."

The Court's analysis focuses on the absence of any legal basis in domestic law — i.e., the measure was not prescribed by law at all. The Court does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards as sub-elements; it simply finds there is no legal basis whatsoever.

- **prescribed_by_law** (overall): explicitly discussed, conclusion: no.
- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not separately discussed; the finding of no legal basis at all implies foreseeability is also not met, but this sub-element is not explicitly addressed.
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not separately discussed; same reasoning applies.
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not separately discussed; same reasoning applies.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. The Court explicitly states: "This conclusion makes it unnecessary to examine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and whether it was necessary in a democratic society." Therefore, the legitimate aim element is not examined, and no aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Not discussed. As quoted above, the Court explicitly states it is unnecessary to examine this element given the finding that the interference was not prescribed by law.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The Court's analysis is entirely framed in terms of a negative obligation (the state's interference with the right), and no positive obligation is identified or discussed.
