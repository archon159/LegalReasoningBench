**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm a finding of violation of Article 11.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied.

- **(i) Assembly exists:** Implied by the reference to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" and the description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies..."

- **(ii) Participation:** Implied by the same passage — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."

- **(iii) Peaceful:** Not explicitly discussed, but implied by the Court's willingness to find a violation under Article 11 (the right only protects peaceful assemblies). The Court does not suggest the assemblies were violent.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" confirms that interference is found. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it discuss foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). Since the Court finds a violation at the necessity stage, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement is not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" — the interference was not necessary.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is not discussed.
