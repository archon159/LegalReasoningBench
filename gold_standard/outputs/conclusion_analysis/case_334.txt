**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section:

"In the light of the aforementioned considerations, the Court concludes that Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code was not 'foreseeable' in its application since it did not afford the applicant the legal protection against arbitrary interference with his right under Article 11 of the Convention [...]. Hence, the interference resulting from the application of Article 220 § 7 was not prescribed by law. That being so, the Court is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was necessary in a democratic society. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the gatherings constitute 'assemblies' within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to the applicant's participation in "ten public meetings" and "public gatherings" and treats them as falling within the scope of Article 11 without question. The interference section states: "the Court considers that there has been an interference with the exercise of the applicant's right to freedom of assembly on account of his conviction for membership of an illegal organisation under Articles 220 § 7 and 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code, based on his participation in the public meetings listed in the indictment." This implies that the gatherings were assemblies within the meaning of Article 11.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Also implied. The Court notes that the applicant "attended ten public meetings and had made speeches during some of those events" and that his conviction was "based on his participation in the public meetings." Participation is treated as established throughout.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
This is implied rather than explicitly discussed. The Court refers to the applicant as "a politician and a peaceful demonstrator" in the merits section: "no distinction was made between the applicant – a politician and a peaceful demonstrator – and an individual who had committed offences within the structure of the PKK." The Court also notes that "he had not advocated violence and had not incited other people to hatred or violence during the demonstrations in question" (in the applicant's submissions). The Court's own reasoning treats the assemblies as peaceful, which is implied by its conclusion that the conviction violated Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court devotes a dedicated sub-section to this question ("(a) Whether there was an interference"). It states:

"The Court reiterates that an interference with the exercise of freedom of peaceful assembly does not need to amount to an outright ban, whether legal or de facto, but can consist of various other measures taken by the authorities. The term 'restrictions' in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an act of assembly and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards [...]. Thus, the Court has found in a number of cases that penalties imposed for taking part in a rally amounted to an interference with the right to freedom of assembly."

And: "In the instant case, the Court considers that there has been an interference with the exercise of the applicant's right to freedom of assembly on account of his conviction for membership of an illegal organisation under Articles 220 § 7 and 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code, based on his participation in the public meetings listed in the indictment and the Diyarbakır Assize Court's judgment of 26 September 2006."

Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed in detail under "(b) Whether the interference was justified."

**i) Foreseeability**
Explicitly discussed at length. The Court concludes that Article 220 § 7 was NOT foreseeable:

"[N]ot only is Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code formulated without a definition of the expressions contained therein, but also the domestic courts have seemingly not developed a consistent judicial interpretation of that provision in the context of demonstrations."

"[T]he array of acts that potentially constitute a basis for the application of a severe criminal sanction in the form of imprisonment, under Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code, is so vast that the wording of the provision, including its extensive interpretation by the domestic courts, does not afford a sufficient measure of protection against arbitrary interference by the public authorities."

"[T]he Court concludes that Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code was not 'foreseeable' in its application since it did not afford the applicant the legal protection against arbitrary interference with his right under Article 11 of the Convention."

**ii) Accessibility**
Explicitly discussed briefly: "The Court considers that there is no doubt that Article 220 § 7 of the Criminal Code was accessible." Conclusion: yes, accessible.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse**
This is discussed as part of the foreseeability analysis rather than as a separate sub-element. The Court emphasises that "a rule is 'foreseeable' when it affords a measure of protection against arbitrary interferences by the public authorities and against the extensive application of a restriction to any party's detriment." The Court finds that the provision does NOT afford sufficient safeguards: "the wording of the provision, including its extensive interpretation by the domestic courts, does not afford a sufficient measure of protection against arbitrary interference by the public authorities." This is treated as part of foreseeability rather than a standalone safeguards analysis, but it is explicitly discussed.

Overall conclusion on prescribed by law: NO — the interference was not prescribed by law.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed by the Court in its own assessment. The Court explicitly states: "That being so, the Court is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was necessary in a democratic society." The Government had argued that the aims were "protecting national security and public order as well as preventing crime," but the Court did not adopt or assess these aims. Conclusion: not discussed (null).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Not discussed by the Court in its own assessment, for the same reason: "the Court is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was necessary in a democratic society." Conclusion: not discussed (null).

Note: The Court does make observations about proportionality and chilling effect in the context of the prescribed-by-law analysis (e.g., "the applicant was given a prison sentence of six years and three months... a sanction which is strikingly severe and grossly disproportionate to their conduct"), but these are used to reinforce the prescribed-by-law finding, not as a standalone necessity analysis.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed at all. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (the State interfered with the applicant's rights through criminal conviction). No positive obligation is identified or assessed.
