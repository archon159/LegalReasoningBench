**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether a 'picket' constitutes an assembly within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the reasoning — the Court proceeds directly to analyze the interference with the applicant's "right to freedom of peaceful assembly," which logically presupposes that the planned picket qualifies as an assembly. The reference to "the applicant aimed to attract the attention of the public and of State officials" and the discussion of "freedom of assembly" securing "a forum for public debate" implies the existence of an assembly.

*ii) Participation:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicant participated or attempted to participate. It is implied by the entire framing of the case — the applicant organized/planned the picket and the authorities refused to authorize it, meaning her attempt to participate is taken as given.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses this sub-element: "The guarantees of Article 11 apply to all gatherings except those where the organisers and participants have violent intentions, incite violence or otherwise reject the foundations of a democratic society (see Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania [GC], no. 37553/05, § 92, ECHR 2015). The national authorities and the Government did not assert that the picket organisers had had any of the above intentions." This is an explicit finding that the assembly was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: "It has not been disputed by the parties that the refusals to authorise the applicant's picket by reference to its aim constituted an interference with her right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court thus explicitly finds interference, noting it is undisputed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court references the legal basis — "The refusals were based on sub-paragraph 2 of section 12(1) of the Public Events Act" — and notes that "The Court has already criticised those provisions of the Public Events Act, that confer an unduly wide degree of discretion on the executive authorities to propose changes to the location, time or manner of conduct of a public event, those powers often being used in an arbitrary and discriminatory way (see Lashmankin and Others, cited above, §§ 416-30 and 477)." However, the Court then states: "In the present case, however, the issue of the lawfulness of the interference in question is indissociable from the question of whether that interference was proportionate." This means the Court explicitly declines to separately resolve the 'prescribed by law' question, merging it with the proportionality/necessity analysis. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually resolved. The reference to "unduly wide degree of discretion" and "arbitrary and discriminatory" use hints at a safeguards concern, but the Court does not reach a separate conclusion on lawfulness. The overall 'prescribed by law' question is thus discussed but not conclusively resolved as a standalone finding — the Court folds it into the necessity analysis.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: "Neither the national courts nor the Government explained how that hypothetical failure could have been linked to any of the legitimate aims listed in the second paragraph of Article 11." The Court does not identify any legitimate aim as established. It notes the sole reason for opposing the picket was its subject matter and the organizers' alleged failure to demonstrate it necessitated public discussion, and that no link to any legitimate aim was explained. The Court does not affirmatively find a legitimate aim, but it does not make a definitive standalone finding of 'no legitimate aim' either — instead it folds this into the necessity analysis: "It needs to be assessed, therefore, whether the refusal by the authorities to approve the picket was necessary in a democratic society, while also taking into consideration the issue of whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim." The practical conclusion is that no legitimate aim was established.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court states: "It needs to be assessed, therefore, whether the refusal by the authorities to approve the picket was necessary in a democratic society." It then finds: "The domestic authorities, therefore, took a formalistic approach in opposing the applicant's event on the basis of the contents of its message. However, a demonstration cannot be banned simply because the authorities consider that the demonstrators' 'message' is wrong (see Primov and Others v. Russia, no. 17391/06, § 135, 12 June 2014). Content-based restrictions on the freedom of assembly should be subjected to the most serious scrutiny by this Court (see Navalnyy v. Russia [GC], nos. 29580/12 and 4 others, § 136, 15 November 2018). In the present case, the national authorities did not put forward any convincing argument that would justify such a restriction." The Court concludes the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference case (refusal to authorize). Positive obligations are not mentioned or implied.
