**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is in the final paragraph of the assessment section: 'The Court therefore dismisses the Government's preliminary objection regarding the applicants' alleged lack of victim status and concludes that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The violation is grounded primarily in the finding that the interference was not prescribed by law, as stated: 'The Court concludes that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of peaceful assembly was therefore not prescribed by law.' Because the Court found the interference was not prescribed by law, it explicitly declined to examine legitimate aim and necessity: 'Having regard to this conclusion, the Court does not need to verify whether the other two requirements (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) set forth in Article 11 § 2 have been complied with.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 existed, but it is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to 'the planned march and several of the stationary assemblies' and notes 'the assemblies were eventually held on the planned dates.' The existence of assemblies is taken for granted. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a doctrinal threshold question.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court implies that the applicants were the organisers and participants of the assemblies. It notes 'the applicants took a risk in holding them' and 'the applicants were negatively affected by the refusals to authorise them.' This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate doctrinal element.

**(iii) Peaceful character:** The applicants themselves argued that 'the assemblies to be held were of a peaceful character.' The Court does not explicitly contest this or make a separate finding on it, but it proceeds on the basis that Article 11 applies, implying the assemblies were peaceful. The applicants' submissions reference the peaceful nature, and the Court's framing of the case as one of 'peaceful assembly' throughout implies this element is satisfied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and finds interference. The key passage: 'Hence, the Court is of the view that, when the assemblies were held the applicants were negatively affected by the refusals to authorise them... There has therefore been an interference with the applicants' rights guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court reasons that the official bans, even though the assemblies were eventually held, created a chilling effect and removed the presumption of legality: 'The assemblies were held without a presumption of legality, such a presumption constituting a vital aspect of effective and unhindered exercise of freedom of assembly and freedom of expression.' The Court also notes that 'the refusals to give authorisation could have had a chilling effect on the applicants and other participants in the assemblies.'

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element and concludes the interference was NOT prescribed by law. The key passage: 'The Court concludes that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of peaceful assembly was therefore not prescribed by law.' The Court bases this on two grounds: (1) the domestic appellate authorities themselves found the first-instance decisions unlawful — 'on 22 August 2005 the Local Government Appeals Board found the decision of 3 June 2005 unlawful... on 17 June 2005 the Mazowsze Governor quashed the refusals of 9 June 2005, finding that they had breached the applicants' freedom of assembly'; and (2) the Constitutional Court found the relevant provisions of the Road Traffic Act incompatible with constitutional guarantees of freedom of assembly, including a breach of the proportionality principle.

The Court does not separately and explicitly discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse in the structured way that some judgments do. However, the finding of 'not prescribed by law' is explicit. The reasoning touches implicitly on the lack of safeguards (the Road Traffic Act provisions gave excessive discretion to authorities — 'They lacked precision, leaving the decision as to whether the organisers satisfied them entirely to the discretion of the authorities,' as argued by the applicants, and confirmed by the Constitutional Court's proportionality finding). The foreseeability and accessibility sub-elements are not individually addressed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states it does not need to examine this element: 'Having regard to this conclusion, the Court does not need to verify whether the other two requirements (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) set forth in Article 11 § 2 have been complied with.' No legitimate aim is identified or discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Same as legitimate aim — the Court explicitly declines to examine this: 'Having regard to this conclusion, the Court does not need to verify whether the other two requirements (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) set forth in Article 11 § 2 have been complied with.' Not discussed on the merits.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly discusses positive obligations in the general principles section: 'Genuine and effective respect for freedom of association and assembly cannot be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere; a purely negative conception would not be compatible with the purpose of Article 11 nor with that of the Convention in general. There may thus be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these freedoms... This obligation is of particular importance for persons holding unpopular views or belonging to minorities, because they are more vulnerable to victimisation.' However, the Court does not ultimately decide the case on the basis of a positive obligation violation — it finds a violation through the interference/prescribed by law route. The positive obligation discussion appears in the general principles section and is not applied to reach a separate conclusion. The conclusion reached is that the interference was not prescribed by law, not that a positive obligation was breached. So positive obligations are explicitly discussed in principle but not applied to a separate finding of violation.
