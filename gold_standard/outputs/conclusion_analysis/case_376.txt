**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits section:

'The Court finds that the reason for banning the planned peaceful assembly was, if at all relevant, not sufficient to meet any pressing social need. The ban has therefore not been shown to be necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness) as standalone questions. However, scope is implied by the Court's statement: 'The Court notes that the Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the ban on the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of his rights under that provision. It sees no reason to hold otherwise.' This implies that the assembly existed, that the applicant sought to participate, and that the assembly was peaceful (the Court later refers to it as a 'planned peaceful assembly'). The peacefulness element is also implied by the Court's reference to 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence' and its characterisation of the assembly as 'planned peaceful assembly.'

- **Scope (overall):** Implied as yes — the Court accepts the applicant can rely on Article 11 guarantees without dispute.
- **Assembly exists:** Implied as yes — the planned demonstration is treated throughout as an assembly within the meaning of Article 11.
- **Participation:** Implied as yes — the applicant is treated as someone who sought to organise/participate in the assembly.
- **Peaceful:** Implied as yes — the Court refers to the 'planned peaceful assembly' and notes the demonstrators did not engage in acts of violence.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly addresses interference: 'The Court notes that the Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the ban on the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of his rights under that provision. It sees no reason to hold otherwise.' This is an explicit finding that there was an interference, based on the ban of the demonstration.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this: 'The Court observes that the ban was based on section 8(1) of the Assembly Act and considers that it was thus prescribed by law.' The conclusion is yes — the measure was prescribed by law. However, the Court does not separately discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or adequacy of safeguards. These sub-elements are not discussed at all; the Court simply accepts the legal basis without further analysis.

- **Prescribed by law (overall):** Explicit, yes.
- **Foreseeable:** Not discussed (the overall conclusion is yes but no sub-analysis).
- **Accessible:** Not discussed.
- **Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly addresses this: 'Moreover, the Court is satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others.' Two aims are found: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It states: 'As regards the question as to whether the interference was necessary in a democratic society, the Court refers at the outset to the principles enounced in paragraphs 24 to 27 of the judgment Körtvélyessy v. Hungary.' It then notes that the domestic ban was based exclusively on traffic issues, reiterates that a demonstration may cause some disruption to traffic but that authorities must show tolerance towards peaceful gatherings, and concludes: 'the Court finds that the reason for banning the planned peaceful assembly was, if at all relevant, not sufficient to meet any pressing social need. The ban has therefore not been shown to be necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued.' The conclusion is no — the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (a ban constituting an interference). Positive obligations are not mentioned or implied.
