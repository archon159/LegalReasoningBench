**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of section B.2(b): "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." This follows from the Court's finding that "the interference with the applicant organisation's right to freedom of peaceful assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reached this conclusion after finding that the reasons given for banning the march were not "relevant or sufficient" — specifically: (1) the march was planned more than two months after the post-election clashes and more than a month after the state of emergency expired; (2) there was no evidence the organisers or participants were involved in post-election disorder; and (3) nothing suggested violent intentions, and the Mayor's reliance on the National Security Service opinion was "couched in general terms" and "not supported by any concrete, clear and convincing evidence."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the planned mourning march constitutes an "assembly" within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the judgment — the Court proceeds directly to analyze interference with "the applicant organisation's right to freedom of peaceful assembly," treating the planned march as an assembly without question. The existence of an assembly is implied by the entire structure of the analysis.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss participation as a separate sub-element. However, it is implied: the applicant organisation organised and attempted to hold the march, and "the police officers, who had been informed of the Mayor's ban, prohibited the applicant organisation and the assembled persons from doing so." The Court's finding of interference necessarily implies that the applicant tried to participate.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court does not explicitly address the "peaceful" character of the assembly as a threshold question. However, it is implied: the Court repeatedly refers to "the right to freedom of peaceful assembly" and notes that "nothing suggests that the organisers or the participants of the planned march had violent intentions or that the march might for any other reason degenerate into mass disorder." This statement, made in the context of the necessity analysis, implies the assembly was peaceful. The Court also notes the march was "supposed to be a quiet march marking the anniversary of a tragic event."

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly discusses interference in section B.2(a), titled "Whether there was an interference with the exercise of the freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court states: "The decision in question banned the applicant organisation from holding the event and the fact that a copy of it had not been served on the applicant organisation did not in any way nullify that decision. Furthermore, the applicant organisation was prevented by the police from holding the planned march, apparently on the basis of the Mayor's ban." Conclusion: "There has therefore been an interference with the applicant organisation's right to freedom of peaceful assembly." This is explicit and affirmative.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The Court addresses this briefly and explicitly in section B.2(b): "The Court notes that nothing suggests that the interference was not prescribed by law." This is a very brief, implicit acceptance that the prescribed-by-law requirement is satisfied. The Court does not break this down into sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards).

**(i) Foreseeable?** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element. The Court's blanket statement that "nothing suggests that the interference was not prescribed by law" implies foreseeability is accepted, but it is not analyzed separately.

**(ii) Accessible?** Not explicitly discussed. Same implied acceptance.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse?** Not explicitly discussed. Same implied acceptance.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court explicitly addresses this in section B.2(b): "Furthermore, it can accept that the interference pursued the legitimate aim of preventing disorder or crime." This is explicit. The aims found are prevention of disorder and prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
The Court explicitly and extensively discusses this in section B.2(b). It reiterates that "there must be convincing and compelling reasons to justify an interference with this right" and that the interference must be "proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued" with reasons that are "relevant and sufficient." The Court then examines the specific reasons given for the ban and finds them insufficient: (1) the march was planned more than two months after the post-election clashes; (2) no evidence linked organisers/participants to post-election disorder; (3) no evidence of violent intentions, and the Mayor's reliance on the NSS opinion was unsupported by "concrete, clear and convincing evidence." Conclusion: "the interference with the applicant organisation's right to freedom of peaceful assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is explicit and negative (i.e., necessity was NOT satisfied).

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not discuss positive obligations in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case — the State actively banned the march and the police prevented it. There is no discussion of any duty on the State to facilitate or protect the assembly. This element is not discussed.
