**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's framing of the case — "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies" — implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The Court treats the applicants as participants in public assemblies without questioning whether those assemblies existed, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assemblies were peaceful. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" without questioning their existence.
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the protection of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly confirmed, though the Court does not dwell on it as a contested issue.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly or impliedly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law. None of the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are addressed. The Court jumps directly to necessity. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment. Legitimate aim is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and the general principles from Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania, Oya Ataman v. Turkey, and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova, finding no fact or argument capable of distinguishing the present case.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
