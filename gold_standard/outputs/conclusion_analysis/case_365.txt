**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and its description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The Court would not have proceeded to assess interference and necessity without implicitly accepting that the assemblies existed, that the applicants participated, and that the assemblies were peaceful (since the protection of Article 11 does not extend to non-peaceful assemblies).

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied affirmative — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether assemblies existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied affirmative — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied affirmative — the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were non-peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The earlier passage also notes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences," which describes the governmental acts constituting the interference. The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming this element is met.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment, relying on its leading case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) where similar issues were already found to violate Article 11. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, these elements remain not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. This element is not discussed. Given that the Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment (and finds it not satisfied), it is possible the Court implicitly assumed a legitimate aim existed (as is common in cases where the violation is found at the necessity stage), but this is not stated. The aims_found list is therefore empty.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement is not satisfied, i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and finds no fact or argument capable of leading to a different conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent.
