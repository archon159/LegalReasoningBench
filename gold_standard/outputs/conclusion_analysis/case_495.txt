**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants or organisers of public assemblies" — this implies that there were assemblies (scope_assembly_exists: implied yes), that the applicants participated or organised them (scope_participation: implied yes). The peacefulness of the assemblies is not explicitly discussed, but the Court proceeds to analyse the interference and necessity, which logically presupposes that the assemblies fell within the protective scope of Article 11, implying they were peaceful (scope_peaceful: implied yes).

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyse whether there was an interference, but it is clearly implied. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" confirms that an interference is treated as established, though not separately analysed. This is implied rather than explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law. None of the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are addressed. This element is not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion. The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
