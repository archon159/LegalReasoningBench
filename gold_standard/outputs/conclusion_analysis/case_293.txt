**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of section C.3 (Merits):

'In view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicants, the Court concludes that their criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and protecting the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society.'

And: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Frumkin, §§ 99 and 137, and Yaroslav Belousov, §§ 168-71, both cited above).' The existence of an assembly is confirmed by reference to prior case law.

**(ii) Did the applicants (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court notes: 'As regards the applicants personally, it does not transpire from the description of their offences that the applicants were among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character. Accordingly, they enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention.' This confirms their participation in the assembly.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court addresses the peaceful character of the assembly with respect to the applicants personally: 'it does not transpire from the description of their offences that the applicants were among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character. Accordingly, they enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court also notes later that 'the applicants' conduct therefore, although involving violence, was sporadic in nature and appeared to be a spontaneous reaction to the force used by the police against the protestors, the majority of whom were peaceful.' The Court thus finds that the applicants personally retained the protection of Article 11 as peaceful participants, even though there was broader disorder at the assembly.

**(b) Interference**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court also finds that the applicants' prosecution and criminal conviction for acts they had committed in the course of the dispersal of the demonstration constituted an interference with the exercise of their freedom of assembly.' This is a clear and explicit finding of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The overall 'prescribed by law' question is explicitly discussed, though the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually addressed. The Court states: 'The applicants did not put forward any arguments relating to the alleged unlawfulness of the interference. The Government made essentially the same submissions in that regard as they had made in Yaroslav Belousov (cited above, § 173). The Court sees no reason to reach a different conclusion in this case (ibid., §§ 174-75). It finds that the applicants' pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful...' The conclusion is that the interference was prescribed by law (lawful), but the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually examined — they are resolved by reference to prior case law without separate analysis.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
Explicitly discussed. The Court finds: '...and pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime, and of protecting the rights and freedoms of others.' The aims found are: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court examines whether the criminal conviction was 'necessary in a democratic society' at length. Key passages include:
- The Court notes the severity of the sentences relative to the applicants' conduct.
- 'The Court considers that there was no "pressing social need" to give the applicants sentences of that duration in addition to sentencing them for assaulting police officers.'
- 'It must be stressed, moreover, that the applicants' criminal conviction, and especially the severity of their sentences, must have had the effect of discouraging them and other opposition supporters, and the public at large, from attending demonstrations...'
- 'In view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicants, the Court concludes that their criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and protecting the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society.'
The conclusion is that the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court explicitly addresses the positive obligation question in the Admissibility section, but concludes it need not be examined separately: 'the Court is not required to assess, as a separate issue under Article 11 of the Convention, the authorities' alleged failure to discharge their positive obligation in respect of the conduct of the demonstration at Bolotnaya Square.' The positive obligation issue is thus raised but explicitly set aside as inseparable from the criminal liability complaint. No separate conclusion is reached on whether the positive obligation was violated.
