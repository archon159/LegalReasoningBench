**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court speaks of "public assemblies" and "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly")
- scope_participation: implied yes (the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without questioning the peaceful nature of the assemblies)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The nature of the interference is also described at the outset: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without reaching prescribed by law, these elements remain not_discussed. However, one could argue they are implied as satisfied (since the Court proceeds to necessity), but given the Court's approach of relying on leading cases and not addressing these sub-elements, it is more accurate to treat them as not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. This element is not discussed. Given the Court proceeds to necessity, one might imply a legitimate aim was assumed, but no aim is named.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that necessity was not satisfied, leading to the violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is not discussed.

**Summary of implied elements:** The scope elements (assembly exists, participation, peaceful) are implied by the Court's framing of the case as one involving "public assemblies" and "organisers or participants." Interference is explicitly confirmed. Prescribed by law and legitimate aim are not discussed (the Court relies on leading cases and goes straight to necessity). Necessity is explicitly found not to be satisfied. Positive obligation is not discussed.
