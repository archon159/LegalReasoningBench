**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and its description of the facts as involving "participants of public assemblies" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The Court would not have proceeded to analyze interference and necessity without implicitly accepting that the assemblies existed, that the applicants participated, and that the assemblies were peaceful (since the leading cases cited also involved peaceful assemblies).

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied as "yes" — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "participants of public assemblies" without questioning whether assemblies existed.
- **scope_participation**: implied as "yes" — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied as "yes" — the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies were violent, and the leading cases cited all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the passage: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The existence of interference is thus explicitly confirmed. The factual description also notes "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence" as the measures complained of.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. By jumping directly to the necessity analysis and finding a violation on that ground (following the leading cases), the Court implicitly treats the "prescribed by law" element as either satisfied or not determinative. Given that the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, this element is not discussed — it is neither explicitly nor clearly implied.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. The Court moves directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that the necessity requirement was NOT met (i.e., the government did not adequately justify the interference), which is why a violation is found.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
