**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is in the final paragraph of the merits assessment:

"In these circumstances the Court finds a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the dispersal of both demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions."

This conclusion is reached after analyzing both the first case (demonstration of 31 July 2010) and the second case (demonstration of 10 March 2013), relying heavily on the Gafgaz Mammadov precedent.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court implicitly accepts that assemblies (demonstrations) took place. It refers throughout to "the demonstration of 31 July 2010" and "the demonstration of 10 March 2013" without questioning whether these constituted assemblies. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation. In the first case: "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention (ibid., §§ 63-64)." In the second case, the same language is repeated: "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention (ibid., §§ 63-64)." Participation is thus explicitly discussed and confirmed.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies. In the first case: "a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner." In the second case, the Court similarly notes the "unauthorised peaceful demonstration." The peaceful character is explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied throughout. The Court discusses the dispersal of demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions as the measures at issue. The passage "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies" confirms that these acts constituted interference, though the Court does not use the formal label "interference" explicitly. It is implied by the entire structure of the analysis.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" element, particularly regarding foreseeability and safeguards.

*i) Foreseeable:* The Court explicitly notes "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies" (referring to Gafgaz Mammadov, § 55). The applicant also argued that "the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly did not comply with the principles of foreseeability and precision." The Court's conclusion on foreseeability is negative (concerns exist), though it does not make a definitive finding on this sub-element alone since it proceeds to find a violation on necessity grounds.

For the second case, the Court actually accepts that the dispersal was lawful: "the Court is ready to accept that the dispersal of the demonstration of 10 March 2013 was lawful" — implying that the prescribed-by-law requirement was met for that case.

*ii) Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed. The Government submitted that "the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly was precise and foreseeable, and was in line with European standards," but accessibility as a distinct sub-element is not addressed.

*iii) Safeguards against abuse:* The Court explicitly notes "the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed" (referring to Gafgaz Mammadov, § 55), which relates to the absence of adequate safeguards. This is explicitly discussed in the context of the first case.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued "in general terms that the dispersal of both demonstrations had been necessary for the protection of public safety and the prevention of disorder or crime, and had been proportionate to the aims pursued." The Court does not explicitly accept or reject these aims — it proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without formally endorsing a legitimate aim. The legitimate aim element is thus mentioned but not conclusively resolved; the Court appears to assume arguendo that a legitimate aim existed and focuses on necessity. The aims mentioned (public safety, prevention of disorder, prevention of crime) are referenced in the parties' submissions but not formally adopted by the Court.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and decided. The Court states: "Turning to the question of whether the dispersal of the demonstration of 10 March 2013 and the applicant's conviction were necessary in a democratic society..." It concludes that "the authorities in the present case did not adduce relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration" and that "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention." The Court also finds a "chilling effect" on freedom of assembly. The necessity requirement is explicitly discussed and found not to be satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
