**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passages are:

- "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the measures applied to the applicant did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society."
- "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

These appear in the final paragraphs of Section B (Merits).

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly define or discuss whether the gathering constitutes an "assembly" within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the entire analysis — the Court proceeds on the basis that a public event/assembly took place: "In the present case the authorities displayed zero tolerance towards the assembly, even though it was peaceful and involved only four participants." The existence of an assembly is thus implied.

*ii) Participation:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicant participated in the assembly. It is implied by the entire framing of the case — the applicant organised and participated in the public event, and was arrested for doing so. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses this: "In the present case the authorities displayed zero tolerance towards the assembly, even though it was peaceful and involved only four participants. There is no evidence that it created any disruption of ordinary life." The assembly is explicitly found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly frame the government's actions as an "interference" with Article 11 rights using that specific doctrinal term. However, it is clearly implied throughout the merits analysis. The Court discusses the refusal to approve the event, the termination by police, the arrest, and the administrative offence proceedings — all of which constitute interference. The Court's reference to "measures applied to the applicant" and its analysis of whether those measures were "necessary in a democratic society" implies that interference is taken as given. The interference is implied rather than explicitly labeled as such.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" element in the context of the notification time-limit: "The Court notes in this connection that it is primarily for the national authorities, notably the courts, to interpret and apply domestic law. The 'law' is therefore the enactment in force as the courts with competence have interpreted it... the Court cannot question the way in which the domestic courts have interpreted and applied national law, except in cases of flagrant non-observance or arbitrariness... The Court cannot discern any such flagrant non-observance of domestic law or arbitrariness in the interpretation or application of the domestic provisions concerning the notification time-limit in the present case. Nor have the applicant questioned the 'quality' of those domestic provisions."

The Court thus accepts that the refusal was lawful (prescribed by law). However, the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually and explicitly discussed. The Court notes that the applicant did not question the "quality" of the domestic provisions, which is an implied acceptance that the law met the quality requirements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These sub-elements are therefore implied as satisfied rather than explicitly analyzed.

*i) Foreseeable:* Implied as satisfied — the applicant did not challenge the quality of the law, and the Court accepted the law's application.
*ii) Accessible:* Implied as satisfied — same reasoning.
*iii) Safeguards:* Implied as satisfied — same reasoning.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued that the head of the Investigations Committee "was entitled to protection against offensive and abusive attacks to be able to enjoy public confidence in conditions free of undue perturbations," which could be read as invoking "protection of the rights and freedoms of others" or "prevention of disorder." However, the Court does not explicitly identify or accept any legitimate aim. The Court moves directly from accepting the lawfulness of the refusal to the necessity analysis, without explicitly finding a legitimate aim. The legitimate aim element is not discussed by the Court itself — it is not_discussed in terms of the Court's own analysis, though the Government's submissions touch on it.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central and most explicitly discussed element. The Court states: "The Court reiterates that the authorities should show a degree of tolerance towards a peaceful, albeit unlawful, assembly which creates only minimal or no disruption of ordinary life... In the present case the authorities displayed zero tolerance towards the assembly, even though it was peaceful and involved only four participants. There is no evidence that it created any disruption of ordinary life. The domestic authorities made no attempt to verify the extent of the risks posed by the protestors, or to verify whether it had been necessary to terminate the public event. Nor was there any noticeable assessment of whether the applicant's escort to the police station and administrative arrest had been necessary in the circumstances."

And: "the Court considers that in the instant case the measures applied to the applicant did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society."

The Court explicitly finds that the necessity requirement was NOT satisfied — i.e., the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the government in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
