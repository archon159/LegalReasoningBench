**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final substantive paragraph of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities did not correspond to a pressing social need and was thus not necessary in a democratic society. [...] These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 and of Article 14 in conjunction with Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court explicitly states there is "a breach of Article 11," confirming a violation.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the scope elements (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated or tried to participate, or whether the assembly was peaceful). However, these elements are implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies" and the applicants' "applications for holding these events," which implies that assemblies were planned, that the applicants sought to participate, and that the assemblies were peaceful (since the Court proceeds to analyze the ban as an interference with Article 11 rights rather than excluding the case from scope). The reference to Alekseyev v. Russia as a leading case on similar issues further implies that the scope requirements are met.

- **Assembly exists:** Implied — the Court refers to "LGBT public assemblies" and "applications for holding these events," implying assemblies were planned.
- **Participation:** Implied — the applicants are described as having made applications to hold the events, implying they sought to participate.
- **Peaceful:** Implied — the Court proceeds to analyze the case under Article 11 without questioning the peaceful nature of the assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze interference as a separate step, but it is clearly implied. The Court states: "the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities" — this implies that a governmental act (the ban) interfered with the applicants' freedom of assembly. The Court's reference to Bączkowski and Others v. Poland on "proportionality of interference" further implies that interference is taken as given.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, suggesting it either assumed this element was satisfied or found it unnecessary to address given the finding on necessity.

- **Foreseeable:** Not discussed.
- **Accessible:** Not discussed.
- **Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing this element, which is consistent with its approach in Alekseyev v. Russia where it similarly focused on necessity.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities did not correspond to a pressing social need and was thus not necessary in a democratic society." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met, constituting the core of the violation finding. The Court also references Bączkowski and Others v. Poland on "proportionality of interference" and Alekseyev v. Russia as a leading case on similar issues.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely in terms of a negative obligation (the ban as an interference), and no positive obligation is spelled out or analyzed.
