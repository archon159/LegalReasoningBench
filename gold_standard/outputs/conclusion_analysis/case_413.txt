**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies")
- scope_participation: implied yes (the applicants are described as "organisers and/or participants")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court proceeds to find a violation under Article 11, which only protects peaceful assemblies; no suggestion of violence)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the penultimate paragraph. The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." This is explicit, and the conclusion is affirmative.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is negative (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is what leads to the violation finding. The Court also references its prior case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) where similar violations were found.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
