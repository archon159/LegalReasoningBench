**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and its description of the applicants as "participants or organisers of public assemblies" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The Court would not have proceeded to assess interference and necessity without implicitly accepting that the assemblies existed, that the applicants participated, and that the assemblies were peaceful (since the Court applies Article 11 protection only to peaceful assemblies).

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied as "yes" — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether assemblies existed.
- **scope_participation**: implied as "yes" — the applicants are described as "participants or organisers of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied as "yes" — the Court proceeds under Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies were non-peaceful; the leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court also describes the measures as "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants or organisers of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly confirmed, though the Court does not dwell on it as a contested issue.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not_discussed. The Court jumps directly to necessity, implying (by proceeding to that stage) that prescribed by law was either not contested or assumed to be satisfied, but this is not stated. Given the Court's brevity and reliance on leading cases, it is most accurate to treat these as not_discussed rather than implied, since the Court does not signal that it has passed through this stage.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It does not name any of the aims listed in Article 11(2). The Court moves directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not_discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court also references its proportionality case-law: "The Court refers to the principles established in its case-law regarding freedom of assembly... and proportionality of interference with it (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey... and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova...)."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is not_discussed.
