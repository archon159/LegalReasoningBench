**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion on admissibility and merits.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, these elements are implied. The Court states that "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies" and refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly." The use of the phrase "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" implies that an assembly existed, that the applicants participated (as organisers or participants), and — since the Court proceeds to assess the interference under Article 11 without excluding the assembly from protection — that the assemblies were peaceful. These are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." Earlier, the facts describe "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. The Court treats these as interferences with freedom of assembly, making this element explicit (though the discussion is brief).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. Since the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis and finds a violation there, it does not need to address prescribed by law. This element is not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without specifying which legitimate aim the interference pursued. This element is not discussed, and no aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to a different conclusion. The necessity element is explicitly discussed and resolved against the government (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary — meaning the government failed this test, which is why there is a violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is not discussed.
