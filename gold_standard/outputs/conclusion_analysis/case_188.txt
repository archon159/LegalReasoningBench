**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies," and the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity, which logically presupposes that the assemblies fell within the scope of Article 11. The word "participants" implies both that assemblies existed and that the applicants participated. Peacefulness is not discussed but is implied by the fact that the Court treats the gatherings as protected under Article 11 without any suggestion that they were violent.

- **Assembly exists:** Implied. The text refers to "public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies" without questioning whether they qualify as assemblies under Article 11.
- **Participation:** Implied. The applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- **Peaceful:** Implied. The Court treats the assemblies as protected under Article 11 without any suggestion of violence or non-peaceful conduct.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the factual background describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. Interference is thus explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor do the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) receive any treatment. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. These sub-elements are not discussed and no conclusion is drawn on them in this text.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches a negative conclusion on necessity (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), relying on its prior case-law in Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, and the general principles from Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania and Oya Ataman v. Turkey. The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (i.e., unjustified interference by the state). No positive obligation is spelled out or analyzed.
