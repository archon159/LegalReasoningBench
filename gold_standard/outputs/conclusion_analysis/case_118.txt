**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

- "the Court considers that in the instant case the measures applied to the applicants as peaceful participants in the public assemblies did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society."
- "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied by the reference to "peaceful public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "The applicants complained of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of peaceful public assemblies." The existence of an assembly is thus implied.

*ii) Participation:* Similarly, the applicants' participation is implied rather than explicitly analyzed. The Court refers to them as "participants" and "peaceful participants in the public assemblies," implying participation is taken as given.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court does not explicitly analyze whether the assemblies were peaceful, but it repeatedly characterizes them as such: "participants of peaceful public assemblies" and "peaceful participants in the public assemblies." This is implied rather than explicitly reasoned through.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze whether there was an interference with the right to freedom of assembly. However, it is implied by the reference to "disproportionate measures taken against them" (arrest and conviction for an administrative offence) and by the Court's proceeding to assess necessity, which logically presupposes an interference. The interference is thus implied.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, either explicitly or by clear implication. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are likewise not discussed. The Court jumps directly to necessity, relying on its leading case-law. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the measures applied to the applicants as peaceful participants in the public assemblies did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society." The Court also references its proportionality case-law (Oya Ataman v. Turkey; Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and leading cases (Navalnyy; Frumkin; Navalnyy and Yashin; Kasparov and Others). The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
