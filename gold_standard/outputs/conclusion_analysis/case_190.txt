**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the conclusion section:

"There have therefore been violations of Article 10 in relation to the events on 26 November 2012 and 27 June 2013 and of Article 11 of the Convention in relation to the other four events."

The four events covered by Article 11 are the rallies on 4 and 6 December 2011, 31 December 2012, and 5 April 2013.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses the existence of assemblies. It refers to "rallies" and "public events" throughout. For instance: "It transpires from the available material that at least two events, on 4 and 6 December 2011, were spontaneously held in the context of the election to the State Duma." The Court also discusses the "performances" in the context of whether they constitute assemblies under the Public Events Act, noting: "The autonomous (meaning of the) concept of 'assembly' under Article 11 § 1 of the Convention protects against improper classifications in national law, those classifications only having relative value and constituting no more than a starting point." The Court treats the rallies as assemblies without question, and even for the performances, it proceeds on the basis that Article 11 principles are relevant. The existence of assemblies is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court explicitly confirms the applicant's participation: "It was not established that the applicant had been in charge of organising or running any of those public events... The applicant was convicted as an ordinary demonstrator." And: "the only specific act held against him was his participation in non-approved events." Participation is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court explicitly addresses this: "It is uncontested that the events and the applicant's participation in them were peaceful." And: "demonstrators did not engage in acts of violence." Also: "Nothing suggests that the applicant uttered any threats, engaged in any reprehensible conduct or caused any harm or significant inconvenience to others." Peacefulness is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly finds interference: "The Court considers that there has been an 'interference' with the applicant's right to freedom of expression on account of his conviction in relation to the 'performances' on 26 November 2012 and 27 June 2013, and his right to freedom of peaceful assembly on account of his conviction in relation to the rallies on 4 and 6 December 2011, 31 December 2012 and 5 April 2013." Interference is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" requirement in a dedicated section. It states: "The Court reiterates that the expression 'prescribed by law' requires that the impugned measure should have a basis in domestic law. It also refers to the quality of the law in question, which should be accessible to the persons concerned and foreseeable as to its effects..."

**(i) Foreseeable?**
The Court discusses foreseeability explicitly: "the Court considers that it ought to have been foreseeable to an event participant, who was made aware that the event had not received official approval from the competent authority, that his or her failure to comply with orders by the police made during that event and relating to the lack of that approval... could constitute a breach of his or her obligation under section 6 § 3 (1) of the PEA and therefore could entail prosecution under Article 20.2 § 5 of the CAO." The Court thus finds foreseeability to be present (at least conditionally), but ultimately decides to "dispense with reaching a conclusion relating to lawfulness" because the convictions were not necessary in a democratic society. So foreseeability is explicitly discussed but no definitive conclusion is reached — the Court leaves it open.

**(ii) Accessible?**
Accessibility is mentioned as part of the general standard ("accessible to the persons concerned") but is not separately analyzed in detail. The Court does not make a specific finding on accessibility. It is implied by the general discussion but not separately concluded upon.

**(iii) Sufficient safeguards against abuse?**
The Court does not separately analyze safeguards against abuse. It notes the broad discretion of Russian authorities: "the Russian authorities' excessively wide discretion in imposing restrictions on those events through rigid enforcement" — but this is discussed in the context of necessity rather than as a safeguards analysis under "prescribed by law." Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element of prescribed by law.

Overall on prescribed by law: The Court explicitly states it will "dispense with reaching a conclusion relating to lawfulness" — so the overall conclusion on prescribed by law is left open/null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "The Government stated, in substance, that the applicant's convictions had the aim of enforcing the Public Events Act, specifically the notification requirement and a participant's obligations under section 6 § 3 of that Act, namely the obligations to comply with lawful orders issued by the police and to respect the 'public order'. The Court will presume that the convictions sought to prevent disorder and protect the rights of others." The Court presumes (without definitively finding) that the aims of prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others were served. Legitimate aim is explicitly discussed and presumed to be present.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
This is the central and most extensively discussed element. The Court finds that the convictions were NOT necessary in a democratic society.

For the rallies on 4 and 6 December 2011: "the applicant's convictions in relation to the events on 4 and 6 December 2011 were not convincingly shown to have been necessary in a democratic society."

For the rallies on 31 December 2012 and 5 April 2013: "even assuming a conviction for the failure to disperse might be justified as 'necessary in a democratic society' in certain circumstances, the Russian courts' perfunctory reasoning makes it impossible for the Court to ascertain that they adequately established the relevant facts and applied the principles under Article 11 of the Convention. The sentences were not proportionate in the circumstances of the case."

The Court applies the general principles including: tolerance toward spontaneous assemblies, proportionality of penalties, the chilling effect of high fines, and the perfunctory reasoning of national courts. Necessity is explicitly discussed and found to be absent (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not explicitly frame any part of its analysis in terms of a positive obligation under Article 11. The case is analyzed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by the state through convictions). Positive obligation is not discussed.
