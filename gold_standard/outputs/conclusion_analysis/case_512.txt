**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final substantive paragraph of the reasoning section:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion on the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicant's freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And the concluding sentence: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to the leading case of Lashmankin and Others v. Russia, finding no reason to depart from that precedent.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicant participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicant's freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicant participated, otherwise there would be no interference with freedom of assembly to speak of. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to "interferences with the applicant's freedom of assembly" presupposing an assembly existed)
- scope_participation: implied yes (same reasoning — the applicant is the subject of the interference)
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court applies Article 11 without any suggestion the assembly was non-peaceful; the leading case framework is applied)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicant's freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming that governmental interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly is found. Additionally, the introductory paragraph states: "The applicant complained about the restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of a public event," which further confirms interference. Conclusion: yes, explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses the "prescribed by law" / "quality of law" requirement. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicant's freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the "quality of law" standard was not met (at least in some instances). However, the Court does not separately and explicitly discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse — it simply refers globally to the "quality of law" requirements not being met, by reference to Lashmankin and Others. The sub-elements are therefore implied (since "quality of law" encompasses foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards), but not individually spelled out in this text.

- prescribed_by_law overall: explicitly discussed, conclusion: no (did not meet quality of law requirements)
- prescribed_by_law_foreseeable: implied, conclusion: no
- prescribed_by_law_accessible: implied, conclusion: no (though the Court does not specify which sub-element failed; it uses "either...or" suggesting not all sub-elements necessarily failed)
- prescribed_by_law_safeguards: implied, conclusion: no

Note: The "either...or" formulation means the Court found that either the quality of law was not met OR necessity was not met (or both), so it is possible that in some instances the law did meet quality requirements but necessity failed, and vice versa. The sub-elements of prescribed by law are thus implied as potentially not satisfied, but not individually confirmed as failing in every case.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. The Court jumps from finding interference to assessing quality of law and necessity. Legitimate aim is not discussed. Conclusion: not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly addresses necessity. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicant's freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit (if brief) finding that necessity was not satisfied in at least some instances. Conclusion: explicitly discussed, conclusion: no.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (restrictions/interferences imposed by authorities). Conclusion: not discussed.
