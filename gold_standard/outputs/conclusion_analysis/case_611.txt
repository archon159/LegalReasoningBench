**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection. These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly argued.

Specifically, the introductory paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This implies all three scope sub-elements are satisfied, but none are explicitly analyzed.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate analytical step. The Court refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the necessity paragraph: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This confirms that interference is treated as established (implied), since the Court proceeds directly to assess necessity without separately justifying the existence of an interference. The factual description of arrests and convictions in the opening paragraph also implies interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" requirement and its three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not discussed at all — neither explicitly nor by implication. The Court jumps directly to necessity without addressing this intermediate step. This is consistent with the Court's practice in repetitive cases decided under a pilot/leading case procedure, where it focuses on the dispositive issue (necessity).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim element is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the Russian authorities. However, since the Court proceeds to assess necessity (which logically presupposes a legitimate aim), one might argue it is implied that some legitimate aim was assumed. But the Court does not name any aim, so this is at best a very weak implication. Given the absence of any mention, it is most accurate to treat this as not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no new facts or arguments to depart from those conclusions.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. There is no mention of a duty on the state to facilitate or protect assemblies. This element is entirely absent.
