**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section: 'Accordingly, the Court holds that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court reaches this conclusion after finding that the interference was not 'necessary in a democratic society': 'the Court cannot find that the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly as protected by the Convention was outweighed by any interests on the part of the respondent State in restricting the exercise of that freedom with a view to preventing disorder. The reasons relied on by the respondent State did not correspond to a pressing social need. Even assuming that they were relevant, they are not sufficient to show that the interference complained of was "necessary in a democratic society"... the Court considers that there was no reasonable relationship of proportionality between the restrictions placed on the applicant's right to freedom of assembly and any legitimate aim pursued.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'It has been the Court's constant approach to regard the notion of an assembly as an autonomous concept... Given the format of the gathering that the applicant has described as a flash mob, the Court considers that it fell within the notion of "peaceful assembly" contained in Article 11.' The Court thus explicitly finds that the flash mob constituted an assembly within the meaning of Article 11.

**(ii) Participation:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The applicant intended to take part in this assembly and never denied it; even if he did not consider it a "public event" or a "static demonstration" subject to notification under the applicable national law, he had been exercising his right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention.' This confirms the applicant's participation.

**(iii) Peaceful:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'nothing in the actions of the applicant and the other participants in the flash mob could be described as incitement to violence or rejection of democratic principles. They did not do anything capable of causing disorder or disruption to ordinary life. Indeed, seven people standing in silence with their mouths sealed with adhesive tape and holding blank sheets of paper hardly represent a threat to public order.' The assembly is treated as peaceful throughout, and the Court also refers to it as 'a peaceful assembly' and 'their peaceful gathering' multiple times.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'Even though the Government have not contested before the Court the applicant's allegation that there had been an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly, the Court considers it appropriate to emphasise the following... In the Court's view, the dispersal of the assembly and the ensuing sanctions constituted "a restriction", within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11, and thus an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly as protected by the first paragraph of this Article.' The conclusion is affirmative — there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this question: 'In view of its findings below it is unnecessary to decide whether the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was "prescribed by law" or pursued one or more legitimate aims... The Court will focus on assessing whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society".' However, the Court does discuss the legal framework at some length, noting that 'the definition of a static demonstration under the Public Events Act... is broad to the extent that a vast array of social situations may fall under it' and that 'the Russian regulatory framework governing public gatherings provides for a broad interpretation of what constitutes a gathering subject to notification and allocates to the authorities excessively wide discretion in imposing restrictions on such gatherings through rigid enforcement.' This discussion touches on foreseeability and safeguards concerns but the Court explicitly leaves the prescribed-by-law question unresolved.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Not formally decided. The Court's observations about the breadth of the definition and wide discretion implicitly raise foreseeability and safeguards concerns, but no formal conclusion is reached. The discussion is implied rather than a formal finding.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed at all.

**(iii) Safeguards:** Implied concern. The Court notes 'excessively wide discretion' allocated to authorities, which relates to the safeguards sub-element, but no formal conclusion is reached.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this question formally: 'it is unnecessary to decide whether the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was "prescribed by law" or pursued one or more legitimate aims.' However, the Government invoked 'the prevention of disorder' as the legitimate aim, and the Court proceeds to assess necessity by reference to this aim: 'the Court will examine... whether the measures taken against the applicant were proportionate to legitimate aim invoked by the Government, namely "the prevention of disorder".' The Court thus works with this aim for the purposes of the necessity analysis without formally confirming it as legitimate. The conclusion on legitimate aim is therefore null/unresolved, though prevention of disorder is the aim referenced.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages include:
- 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings if the freedom of assembly guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention is not to be deprived of all substance'
- 'the domestic authorities did not show the requisite degree of tolerance towards their peaceful gathering despite the absence of any risk of insecurity or disturbance'
- 'the domestic judicial bodies in the course of the administrative-offence proceedings against the applicant did not seek to strike this balance giving the preponderant weight to the formal unlawfulness of the presumed static demonstration'
- 'the Court cannot find that the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly as protected by the Convention was outweighed by any interests on the part of the respondent State... The reasons relied on by the respondent State did not correspond to a pressing social need. Even assuming that they were relevant, they are not sufficient to show that the interference complained of was "necessary in a democratic society"... there was no reasonable relationship of proportionality between the restrictions placed on the applicant's right to freedom of assembly and any legitimate aim pursued.'

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by the State), and the Court does not spell out any positive obligation for the government.
