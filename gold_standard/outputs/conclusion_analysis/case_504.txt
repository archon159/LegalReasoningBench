**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The scope is not explicitly discussed in the reasoning text. However, it is implied by the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and the description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies." The Court proceeds directly to assess the interference and necessity, which logically presupposes that the assembly existed, that the applicants participated, and that it was peaceful.

- **(i) Assembly exists:** Implied. The text states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies." The Court does not question whether an assembly existed.

- **(ii) Participation:** Implied. The applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," and the Court proceeds on this basis without questioning it.

- **(iii) Peaceful:** Implied. The Court applies Article 11 without any suggestion that the assemblies were non-peaceful. The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov — all involving peaceful assemblies — further implies this finding.

**(b) Interference**

The interference is explicitly discussed, though briefly. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used without qualification, confirming that an interference was found. Additionally, the opening paragraph describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of, which the Court treats as interferences.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

This element is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not address whether the interference was prescribed by law, foreseeable, accessible, or accompanied by sufficient safeguards. Given that the Court jumps directly to necessity and finds a violation on that basis (following its leading cases), the "prescribed by law" sub-elements are not discussed even by implication in this abbreviated reasoning.

- **(i) Foreseeable:** Not discussed.
- **(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.
- **(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify which legitimate aim the interference pursued. In the leading cases referenced (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov), the Court typically accepted that the interference pursued a legitimate aim (e.g., prevention of disorder) before finding a violation at the necessity stage. However, in this abbreviated reasoning, no legitimate aim is named or implied.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and the finding that no new fact or argument was presented to justify a different conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

No positive obligation is discussed or implied in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference by the state through arrests and convictions).
