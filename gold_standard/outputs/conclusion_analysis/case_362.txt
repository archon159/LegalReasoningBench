**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection. These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly argued.

Specifically, the introductory paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

The reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the necessity paragraph further implies that the Court accepted the assembly fell within the scope of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate analytical step. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" confirms that the Court treats interference as established, but it does not separately reason through whether an interference occurred — it proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. The description of "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" in the opening paragraph also implies that interference is taken as given.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" requirement and its three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not discussed at all — neither explicitly nor by implication. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis without addressing whether the interference was prescribed by law. This is consistent with the Court's approach in repetitive cases decided under the pilot/leading case procedure, where it focuses on the dispositive issue (necessity) without working through every element.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim element is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the Russian authorities. However, since the Court proceeds to assess necessity — which logically presupposes that a legitimate aim exists — one might argue that a legitimate aim is implied. That said, the Court does not name any aim, so it is more accurate to treat this as not discussed, with the necessity analysis being the Court's sole focus.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the Court's explicit conclusion on the merits, and it is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and the general principles from Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania and Oya Ataman v. Turkey.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. There is no mention of a duty on the government to facilitate or protect the assemblies. This element is entirely absent.
