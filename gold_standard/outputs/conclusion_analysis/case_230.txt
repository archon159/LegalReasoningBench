**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "Consequently, there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court's holding on necessity reads: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses whether the occupation of St Bernard's Church constitutes an "assembly." The Government argued that "in view of its duration – several months – could the occupation constitute an 'assembly'." The Court implicitly rejects this argument by proceeding to find an interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly. The Court states: "the Court finds that the evacuation of the church amounted to an interference in the exercise of the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly." This implies the Court found an assembly existed. The discussion is explicit (the Government raised it) and the Court's conclusion is implied by its finding of interference.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes the applicant's participation: "The Court notes that the applicant was a member of a group of aliens without valid residence permits who decided to take collective action to draw attention to the difficulties they were having in obtaining a review of their immigration status in France. Their campaign culminated on 28 June 1996 with a decision to occupy St Bernard's Church, in which the group took up residence for approximately two months." This is explicit and affirmative.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. The Government argued the assembly was not peaceful because it involved a breach of immigration rules and interfered with freedom of worship. The Court implicitly rejects this by finding an interference with the right to "freedom of peaceful assembly": "the Court finds that the evacuation of the church amounted to an interference in the exercise of the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court also notes: "Neither the priest nor the parish council of the church objected to their presence and the religious services and various ceremonies proceeded as planned and without incident." Later, in the necessity section, the Court confirms: "even though it was peaceful and did not in itself entail any disturbance of public order or prevent churchgoers from attending services..." This is explicit and affirmative.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed under section A. The Court states: "the Court finds that the evacuation of the church amounted to an interference in the exercise of the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly." Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed under section B.1. The Court finds: "The Court finds that the interference in issue was prescribed by law: in particular, under the Law of 9 December 1905 it is the local authorities, not the parish priest, who are responsible for supervising religious ceremonies and they are empowered to act either at the priest's request or on their own initiative." Conclusion: yes.

The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually and explicitly broken down by the Court. The Court's brief statement simply concludes the interference was prescribed by law without separately analyzing foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. These sub-elements are therefore not discussed explicitly or even clearly implied — the Court simply accepts the government's position with a brief reference to the legal basis. I will treat them as implied (since the overall "prescribed by law" conclusion is reached, the sub-elements are logically implied to be satisfied), though the Court does not separately address them.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed under section B.2. The Court states: "The Court notes that the evacuation was ordered to put an end to the continuing occupation of a place of worship by persons, including the applicant, who had broken French law. The interference therefore pursued a legitimate aim, namely the prevention of disorder." Conclusion: yes. Aim found: prevention of disorder.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed under section B.3. The Court finds that the interference was not disproportionate: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2." The Court acknowledges the methods used were regrettable ("the methods used by the police in an intervention that came without warning and was indiscriminate, went beyond what it was reasonable to expect the authorities to do when curtailing the freedom of assembly. The Court regrets that such methods were employed") but ultimately finds the interference justified given the deteriorating health conditions and the wide margin of appreciation. Conclusion: yes (necessity satisfied, no violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly spell out a positive obligation for the government in this case. The case is framed as a negative interference case (the state actively evacuated the church). There is a passing reference to Plattform "Ärzte für das Leben" v. Austria in the context of the margin of appreciation, but no positive obligation analysis is conducted. Conclusion: not discussed.
