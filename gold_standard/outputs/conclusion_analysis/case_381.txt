**Question 1: Has Article 11 been violated?**

Yes. The Court explicitly concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is: "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." And then: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. The description of the complaints as concerning "organisers and participants of public assemblies" and the reference to "dispersal of these assemblies" further implies the existence of assemblies and participation. The peaceful nature is implied by the Court's willingness to apply Article 11 protection (Article 11 only protects peaceful assemblies). These are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies" without questioning their existence.
- scope_participation: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers and participants of public assemblies."
- scope_peaceful: implied yes — the Court applies Article 11 protection without any suggestion that the assemblies were not peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The complaints also describe "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence" as the measures complained of. The Court's use of the word "interferences" (plural) confirms that interference is found. This is explicit, though the Court does not separately analyze whether interference occurred — it proceeds directly to necessity, treating interference as established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it did not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law, specifically the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, and the principles in Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania and Oya Ataman v. Turkey. The conclusion is that necessity is NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
