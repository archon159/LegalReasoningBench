**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "In the light of the above considerations, the Court finds that the Russian authorities did not adduce any 'relevant and sufficient' reasons which could have justified the interference with the applicant's rights to freedom of expression and assembly. That the amount of the fine was relatively small does not detract from the fact that the interference was not 'necessary in a democratic society'. There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention, interpreted in the light of Article 10."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In the interference section, it states: "The applicant in the present case held a picket in front of a regional court." Earlier, the Court also notes: "The Court reiterates at the outset that the right to freedom of assembly covers both private meetings and meetings on public thoroughfares, as well as static meetings and public processions." The picket is treated throughout as an assembly within the meaning of Article 11. This is explicit.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation. The interference section states: "The applicant in the present case held a picket in front of a regional court. Some time later he was found administratively liable and fined for having breached the procedure for organising and holding a public assembly." The applicant is treated as both organiser and participant. The Court also notes: "the right can be exercised both by individual participants and by those organising the assembly." This is explicit.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court implies that the assembly was peaceful. It never suggests otherwise, and repeatedly invokes the principle that "any demonstration in a public place inevitably causes a certain level of disruption to ordinary life" and that authorities must show "a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings." The Court also states: "The materials distributed by the applicant and the ideas he advocated during the picket were not shown to contain any defamatory statements, incitement to violence or rejection of democratic principles." The peaceful nature is implied throughout rather than explicitly adjudicated as a threshold question.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference. Under section (a) "Whether there was interference": "The Court considers that the administrative prosecution amounted to an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly, interpreted in the light of his right to freedom of expression. Accordingly, its task is to determine whether the interference was justified." The Court also reiterates that "The term 'restrictions' in paragraph 2 of Article 11 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during the public assembly, and those, such as punitive measures, taken after the meeting."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly accepts that the interference was prescribed by law: "The administrative liability for failure to comply with the established procedure for organising or holding a public assembly was foreseen by Article 20.2 §§ 1 and 2 of the Russian Code of Administrative Offences, which was the legal basis for the applicant's conviction in the present case. The Court accepts that the interference was 'prescribed by law'..."

The Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. It simply accepts the "prescribed by law" requirement without further breakdown. Therefore:

- **Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element; the overall "prescribed by law" conclusion is accepted, implying foreseeability is satisfied.
- **Accessible:** Not discussed.
- **Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly accepts that the interference pursued legitimate aims: "The Court accepts that the interference was 'prescribed by law' and that it pursued 'legitimate aims' within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Articles 10 and 11, those of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others." The aims found are: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It concludes that the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages include:

- "It remains to be determined whether it was 'necessary in a democratic society'."
- The Court examines each of the three grounds for the applicant's conviction (belated notification, obstruction of passage, discrepancy in distributed materials) and finds none of them constitutes a "relevant and sufficient" reason.
- On notification: "the Court considers that a merely formal breach of the notification time-limit was neither relevant nor a sufficient reason for imposing administrative liability on the applicant."
- On obstruction: "the Court is not satisfied that the alleged obstruction of passage, especially in the circumstances where the applicant gave evidence of his flexibility and readiness to cooperate with the authorities, was a relevant and sufficient reason for the interference."
- On distributed materials: "it was not a relevant or sufficient ground for imposing liability on the applicant for the exercise of his right to freedom of expression and assembly."
- Final conclusion: "the Court finds that the Russian authorities did not adduce any 'relevant and sufficient' reasons which could have justified the interference... the interference was not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly discuss a positive obligation in this case. There is one passing reference to the general principle that "States must not only safeguard the right to assemble peacefully but also refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon that right," but this is framed as part of the necessity analysis rather than as a standalone positive obligation analysis. No finding of violation of a positive obligation is made. This element is not discussed as a separate issue.
