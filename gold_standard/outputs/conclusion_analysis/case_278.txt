**Question 1: Violation of Article 11**

The Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the Merits section (B. Merits):

'The Government have admitted that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention. In the circumstances of the case, the Court finds no reason to hold otherwise. There has, accordingly, been a violation of this Article.'

The Court relies on the Government's own admission (made in observations lodged on 1 April 2010) and finds no reason to depart from that concession.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

- *Assembly exists:* The applicant association complained of a ban on holding a demonstration. The Government agreed there was an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly. The existence of an assembly (a planned demonstration) is implied by the framing of the entire case and the Government's concession of interference. Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element, but implied.
- *Participation:* The applicant association is the one that sought to hold the demonstration and was banned from doing so. Participation is implied by the same logic — the applicant tried to hold/participate in the assembly. Not explicitly discussed, but implied.
- *Peaceful:* The assembly is referred to throughout as a 'peaceful assembly' and the complaint is framed under the right to 'peaceful assembly.' The Court notes the complaint is about 'its right to peaceful assembly.' This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a sub-element.

**(b) Interference**

The interference is explicitly discussed. The Government, in their observations of 15 September 2008, 'agreed that there was an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly.' The Court proceeds on this basis. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The applicant claimed the ban 'had not been in accordance with the law.' However, the Court does not explicitly analyze this sub-element in the Merits section. The Government initially argued the interference was 'prescribed by law' in their September 2008 observations, but subsequently conceded a violation. The Court does not separately address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The applicant claimed the ban 'had not pursued any legitimate aim.' The Government initially argued it had a legitimate aim (referencing the Orthodox Christian majority and popular requests to ban the assembly), but later conceded a violation. The Court does not explicitly analyze whether a legitimate aim was pursued. Not discussed in the merits analysis.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The applicant claimed the ban 'had not been necessary in a democratic society.' The Court does not explicitly analyze this element. Not discussed in the merits analysis.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. Not discussed.

**Summary:** The judgment is extremely brief on the merits. The Court simply notes the Government's concession of a violation and adopts it. The only element explicitly discussed is interference (conceded by the Government). Scope elements (assembly exists, participation, peaceful) are implied by the framing. The prescribed-by-law sub-elements, legitimate aim, necessity, and positive obligation are not discussed in the merits, though the applicant raised them in its complaint and the Government initially addressed some of them before changing its position.
