**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court explicitly concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passages are found in the final paragraphs of Section B.3 (The Court's assessment):

'Having regard to the facts of the present cases and their clear similarity to those of the Gafgaz Mammadov case on all relevant and crucial points, the Court sees no particular circumstances that could compel it to deviate from its findings in that judgment, and finds that in the present cases each applicant's right to freedom of assembly was breached for the same reasons as those outlined above.'

And conclusively: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of demonstrations (e.g., 'the demonstration of 2 April 2011'). The existence of an assembly is treated as given and not contested. This is implied by the entire analysis.

**(ii) Participation:** The applicants' participation is explicitly discussed — they were arrested and convicted for participating in the demonstrations. The Court notes 'the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention' (citing Gafgaz Mammadov, §§ 63-64). This is explicitly discussed.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses this: 'The Court notes from the outset that none of the official records or the domestic court decisions submitted by the parties suggests that the demonstration of 2 April 2011 was violent. Therefore the Court accepts the assertion of the applicants in applications nos. 67977/11, 69411/11 and 69421/11 that that demonstration had been intended to be peaceful and was conducted in a peaceful manner up to the point of their arrest.' The Court also references 'a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration, which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner' (citing Gafgaz Mammadov, § 61). This is explicitly discussed with a positive conclusion.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is explicitly discussed and clearly established. The Court refers to 'the dispersal of the demonstrations by the police and their arrest and conviction for an administrative offence' as the subject of the complaint, and the entire analysis proceeds on the basis that there was an interference. The Court's reference to the Gafgaz Mammadov findings — including doubts about the credibility of the formal grounds relied on to arrest and convict participants, and the lack of reasons justifying dispersal — confirms that interference is treated as established. This is implied (the entire analysis presupposes it) but also effectively explicit given the framing of the complaint and the Court's adoption of the Gafgaz Mammadov analysis.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the 'prescribed by law' requirement, particularly through its reference to the Gafgaz Mammadov findings:

'(i) there were serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed (ibid., § 55)'

This addresses foreseeability and safeguards against abuse. The applicants also argued that 'the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly did not comply with the principles of foreseeability and precision' and that 'the system of prior authorisation... allowed for arbitrary interference with freedom of assembly and permitted abusive banning or dispersal of public gatherings.'

- **Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed — 'serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation.' Conclusion: no (not sufficiently foreseeable).
- **Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element. Not discussed.
- **Safeguards:** Explicitly discussed — concerns about 'the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed' and 'arbitrary interference.' Conclusion: no (insufficient safeguards).

Overall 'prescribed by law': The Court raises serious concerns but does not definitively resolve the case solely on this ground — it proceeds to examine other elements as well (following Gafgaz Mammadov). The conclusion on prescribed by law is effectively negative (concerns raised), though the Court does not formally stop the analysis there.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued that 'the dispersal of the demonstrations had pursued the aim of protecting public safety and preventing disorder or crime.' The Court does not explicitly accept or reject this claim as a standalone finding. Instead, it proceeds to find a violation on other grounds (lack of relevant and sufficient reasons, no acknowledgment of Article 11 protection). The legitimate aim is mentioned in the parties' submissions but the Court does not make an explicit finding accepting it. Given the Court's approach of following Gafgaz Mammadov wholesale, and that judgment's structure, the legitimate aim question is touched upon but not conclusively resolved in the applicants' favor or the government's favor as a standalone step. The aims mentioned by the Government are public safety and prevention of disorder/crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses necessity/proportionality through the Gafgaz Mammadov findings:

'(iv) there was a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration, which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner (ibid., § 61); and (v) there had been no acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention (ibid., §§ 63-64).'

Also: 'the authorities had failed to take into consideration the fact that the demonstration had been notified (ibid., § 60).' These findings go to the heart of the necessity/proportionality assessment. The Court concludes that the interference was not necessary in a democratic society. Conclusion: no (not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (unjustified interference). Not discussed.
