**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the section on the merits:

"The Court therefore concludes that the interference in question did not meet the Convention requirement of lawfulness. That being so, it is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was proportionate to the aim pursued. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed as a threshold question, but it is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court repeatedly refers to "demonstrations," "rallies," "protest movement," and "ongoing protests" as the factual backdrop. For example: "the applicant was a member of the political opposition and a known public figure who took active part in the post-election demonstrations and whose arrest happened when the rallies were in full swing." The existence of an assembly is thus implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a doctrinal sub-element.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Again implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a doctrinal sub-element. The Court notes: "the applicant was a member of the political opposition and a known public figure who took active part in the post-election demonstrations and whose arrest happened when the rallies were in full swing and was indirectly linked to his participation in the ongoing protests, as the applicant was alleged to have been illegally armed while on his way from a demonstration." The applicant's participation is treated as an established fact underpinning the interference analysis.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assembly was peaceful as a doctrinal sub-element. However, the Court's framing of the case — including references to PACE condemnation of the "de facto crackdown on the opposition" and the characterization of the protests as opposition rallies — implies that the assembly was peaceful. The Court's willingness to find an interference with the "right to freedom of peaceful assembly" implies this sub-element is satisfied.

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly discusses interference in the section titled "Whether there has been an interference with the exercise of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court states: "The Court is, therefore, prepared to assume that the entirety of the facts on which the applicant's prosecution and conviction were based can be regarded, on arguable grounds, as an instance of an 'interference' with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court also reiterates the general principle: "The term 'restrictions' in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an assembly, such as a prior ban, dispersal of the rally or the arrest of participants, and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards, including penalties imposed for having taken part in a rally." The conclusion on interference is affirmative, though expressed with some caution ("prepared to assume").

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The Court explicitly discusses this element. It concludes that the interference was NOT prescribed by law: "the impugned interference with the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly could only be characterised as manifestly arbitrary and, consequently, unlawful for the purposes of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court's reasoning focuses on the arbitrary nature of the prosecution — the applicant was formally prosecuted for criminal acts but the true reason was his participation in the protest movement. The Court does not separately analyze the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) in the traditional sense. Instead, it focuses on the concept of arbitrariness as the basis for finding the interference not prescribed by law, citing Hakobyan and Others and Huseynli and Others.

**i) Foreseeable?** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element. The Court's finding of unlawfulness is based on arbitrariness rather than lack of foreseeability of the legal provisions themselves.

**ii) Accessible?** Not explicitly discussed.

**iii) Sufficient safeguards against abuse?** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element, though the finding of arbitrariness implicitly relates to the absence of adequate safeguards against abuse of the criminal process.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court explicitly states it is not required to examine legitimate aim: "That being so, it is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was proportionate to the aim pursued." The Government had argued the aim was "preventing crime and protecting public safety," but the Court did not adopt or assess this. No legitimate aim is found by the Court.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Not discussed. The Court explicitly states it need not examine this given its finding on lawfulness.

**(f) Positive obligation**
Not discussed. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by the state through prosecution and conviction).

**Summary of findings:**
- Scope (assembly exists): implied yes
- Scope (participation): implied yes
- Scope (peaceful): implied yes
- Interference: explicitly discussed, conclusion yes (with caution)
- Prescribed by law: explicitly discussed, conclusion no (manifestly arbitrary)
- Prescribed by law foreseeable: not discussed as sub-element
- Prescribed by law accessible: not discussed
- Prescribed by law safeguards: not discussed
- Legitimate aim: explicitly noted as not needing examination; no aim found
- Necessity: not discussed
- Positive obligation: not discussed
