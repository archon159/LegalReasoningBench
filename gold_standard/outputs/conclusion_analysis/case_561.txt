**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits assessment: "In view of the foregoing, the Court concludes that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses this element. It refers to "demonstrations against the G8 summit" and notes that "the applicants intended to take part in future demonstrations against the G8 summit." The existence of assemblies (demonstrations) is treated as established and uncontested. The Court also notes that "a number of mass demonstrations were scheduled to take place over several days." This is explicitly discussed and answered affirmatively.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court notes that at the time of their arrest, the applicants intended to take part in future demonstrations against the G8 summit." And: "as a result of their detention, ordered by the domestic courts for the entire duration of the G8 summit, the applicants were prevented from taking part in demonstrations against that summit." Answered affirmatively.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
Explicitly discussed at length. The Court reiterates that "Article 11 of the Convention only protects the right to 'peaceful assembly'. That notion does not cover a demonstration where the organisers and participants have violent intentions." It then examines whether the demonstrations and the applicants' own intentions were peaceful. It finds: "There is nothing to indicate that the organisers of the demonstrations in which the applicants intended to participate had violent intentions." Regarding the applicants themselves: "the Court is not satisfied that it has been shown that they had violent intentions in seeking to participate in G8-related demonstrations." It notes no weapons were found, the slogans were ambiguous, the applicants gave a plausible non-violent interpretation, and neither had prior convictions for violent conduct. Conclusion: the assembly was peaceful and within the scope of Article 11 § 1.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court considers that, as a result of their detention, ordered by the domestic courts for the entire duration of the G8 summit, the applicants were prevented from taking part in demonstrations against that summit." And: "The applicants' detention thus interfered with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 § 1. This is uncontested between the parties." Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element but leaves it open. It states: "It observes that it is contested between the parties whether the applicants' detention was prescribed by a law – section 55(1), paragraph 2, of the PSOA – which was sufficiently precise to be foreseeable in its application in the circumstances of the applicants' case. The Court considers that it can leave that question open and examine the case on the assumption that the interference was 'prescribed by law' for the reasons which follow."

**i) Foreseeable?**
Explicitly discussed but left open. The Court notes the contestation and assumes (without deciding) that the law was sufficiently foreseeable. Conclusion: null (left open).

**ii) Accessible?**
Not explicitly discussed. The Court only mentions foreseeability in the context of "prescribed by law" and does not separately address accessibility. Conclusion: not discussed.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse?**
Not explicitly discussed. Conclusion: not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court is satisfied that the aim of the authorities in ordering the applicants' detention was to prevent them from committing a crime, namely inciting others to liberate prisoners by force. This aim as such is legitimate under Article 11 § 2." The Government also argued public safety. The Court accepts the aim of prevention of crime as legitimate. Conclusion: yes, legitimate aim found — prevention of crime. (Public safety was also argued by the Government and not rejected, but the Court specifically identifies prevention of crime.)

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the proportionality test, examining whether the interference corresponded to a "pressing social need" and was proportionate. Key findings include:
- The applicants were not shown to have had violent intentions; at most they could have negligently incited others.
- Their protests were aimed at participating in a debate of public interest (effects of globalisation, criticism of police management of detentions during the G8 summit).
- The detention had a chilling effect on expression and public debate.
- A detention of almost six days was a "considerable sanction" and not proportionate.
- Less intrusive measures were available: "it would have been sufficient to seize the banners in question."
- "In view of the foregoing, the Court concludes that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'."
Conclusion: no, the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (the State interfered with the applicants' rights through detention). No positive obligation analysis is undertaken. Conclusion: not discussed.
