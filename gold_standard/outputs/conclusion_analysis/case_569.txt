**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage states: "Having examined all the material before it, the Court concludes that they also disclose violations of the Convention in the light of its well-established case-law (see ... Kasparov and Others v. Russia no. 21613/07, §§ 84-97, 3 October 2013, and Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, §§ 102-42, 5 January 2016, concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against participants of public assemblies, and Yartsev v. Russia, no. 16683/17, §§ 28-38, 20 July 2021, related to the administrative conviction for waving banners with slogans that did not correspond to the declared aims of the public event)."

The Court does not cite specific paragraph numbers within this decision for the Article 11 analysis itself — it proceeds by reference to well-established case-law and an appended table, finding violations without conducting a full step-by-step analysis in the text provided.

**Question 2: Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope:**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, by referencing Kasparov and Others and Frumkin (concerning "disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against participants of public assemblies") and Yartsev (concerning "administrative conviction for waving banners with slogans that did not correspond to the declared aims of the public event"), the existence of a public assembly and the applicants' participation in it are implied. The peaceful nature of the assembly is not discussed but is implied by the framing (no suggestion of violence). These elements are implied rather than explicit.

**(b) Interference:**
The Court does not explicitly discuss interference as a separate element. However, the references to "disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against participants" and "administrative conviction for waving banners" imply that there was governmental interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly. This is implied.

**(c) Prescribed by law:**
The Court does not discuss the "prescribed by law" requirement at all — neither foreseeability, accessibility, nor safeguards are mentioned. The Court jumps directly to the conclusion of a violation by reference to well-established case-law, without conducting a structured analysis of the justification elements. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim:**
The Court does not discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. Not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:**
The Court references Kasparov and Frumkin as cases "concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against participants of public assemblies." The word "disproportionate" implies a necessity/proportionality analysis, but this is not explicitly conducted in the text provided. The necessity element is implied (negatively — i.e., the measures were not necessary/proportionate) by the reference to those precedents.

**(f) Positive obligation:**
The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. Not discussed.
