**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. This is stated in the final paragraph of the section: "Consequently, there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The penultimate paragraph also states: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that an assembly existed. It notes: "the applicant and others clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in the Ayios Kassianos area of Nicosia." The word "demonstrating" implies an assembly. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court implicitly accepts that the applicant participated. It states: "The demonstration was dispersed and some of the demonstrators, including the applicant, were arrested." This is implied.

**(iii) Peaceful character:** This sub-element is explicitly discussed, though the Court's treatment is nuanced. The Government argued the demonstration was "clearly outside the scope of Article 11" due to its violent character. The Court does not exclude the demonstration from Article 11's scope on grounds of violence — it proceeds to analyze the interference — but it does note the violent character of the demonstration as relevant to the necessity/proportionality analysis. The Court states: "the intervention of the Turkish and/or Turkish-Cypriot forces was not due to the political nature of the demonstration but was provoked by its violent character and by the violation of the 'TRNC' borders by some of the demonstrators." The Court also notes: "interferences with the right guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention are in principle justified for the prevention of disorder or crime and for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others where, as in the instant case, demonstrators engage in acts of violence." The Court implicitly treats the assembly as falling within Article 11's scope (since it proceeds to analyze interference and justification) but acknowledges the violent character. The peaceful sub-element is thus explicitly discussed, with the Court finding the assembly was not entirely peaceful, yet still within scope.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that there has been an interference with the applicant's right of assembly (see Protopapa, cited above, § 104)." Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "This interference had a legal basis, namely sections 70 and 71 of the Cypriot Criminal Code (see paragraphs 27-28 above) and section 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law (see paragraph 31 above), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." The Court also recalls its finding that "when, as in the Foka case, an act of the 'TRNC' authorities was in compliance with laws in force within the territory of northern Cyprus, it should in principle be regarded as having a legal basis in domestic law for the purposes of the Convention."

However, the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are NOT explicitly discussed. The Court simply concludes the interference was "prescribed by law" without separately analyzing these three sub-requirements. They are therefore not discussed (or at best implied by the overall conclusion).

**(i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed; implied by the overall "prescribed by law" conclusion.
**(ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed; implied by the overall "prescribed by law" conclusion.
**(iii) Safeguards:** Not explicitly discussed; implied by the overall "prescribed by law" conclusion.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed. The Government submitted that the interference pursued legitimate aims "including the protection of national security and/or public safety and the prevention of disorder and crime." The Court proceeds on the basis that a legitimate aim was pursued (it does not reject this claim), and its analysis of necessity implicitly accepts the legitimate aims. The Court states: "There remain the questions whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society," and then proceeds to analyze the violent character of the demonstration in a way that implicitly accepts the aims of public safety, prevention of disorder, and prevention of crime. The aims found are: national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed. The Court engages in a proportionality analysis: it references the fundamental principles of Article 11 jurisprudence, notes the authorities' duty to take appropriate measures, acknowledges the wide margin of appreciation, and concludes: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2." The Court also notes: "interferences with the right guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention are in principle justified for the prevention of disorder or crime and for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others where, as in the instant case, demonstrators engage in acts of violence." Conclusion: the interference was necessary (i.e., proportionate), so the necessity test is satisfied from the government's perspective.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court briefly mentions the positive obligation dimension: "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey, no. 74552/01, § 35, 5 December 2006). However, they cannot guarantee this absolutely and they have a wide discretion in the choice of the means to be used." This is mentioned in passing as part of the general principles, but the Court does not separately analyze whether the respondent government violated a positive obligation. The mention is in the context of justifying the interference, not finding a separate positive obligation breach. Conclusion: mentioned but not concluded upon as a separate finding; no violation of positive obligation found.
