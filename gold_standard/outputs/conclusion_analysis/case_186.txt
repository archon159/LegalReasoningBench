**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court explicitly finds a breach of Article 11, relying on its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finding no new facts or arguments to depart from those conclusions.

**Question 2: Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

(i) Assembly exists: The Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies." The existence of assemblies is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a doctrinal step. The Court proceeds on the assumption that assemblies existed.

(ii) Participation: The Court refers to the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," implying their participation is accepted without explicit doctrinal analysis.

(iii) Peaceful: The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assemblies were peaceful. However, by proceeding to analyze the interference and necessity, it implicitly treats the assemblies as falling within the protection of Article 11, which requires them to be peaceful. This is implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used directly, and the opening paragraph describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. Interference is thus explicitly acknowledged, though not analyzed in depth as a separate doctrinal step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed. Since the Court jumps directly to necessity and finds a violation there, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court also references proportionality case-law (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and the leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). The necessity element is explicitly discussed and resolved negatively (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is not discussed.

**Summary of implied elements:** Scope (assembly exists, participation, peaceful) and interference are implied by the Court's direct engagement with the necessity question — one cannot assess necessity without first accepting that there is an assembly, that the applicants participated, that it was peaceful, and that there was an interference. However, none of these are explicitly analyzed as separate doctrinal steps in this abbreviated reasoning.
