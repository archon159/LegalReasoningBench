**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court reaches this conclusion by finding that the interference with the applicant's right of assembly "cannot be considered lawful under domestic law," i.e., it was not prescribed by law.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly confirms that an assembly existed by stating "the applicant's protest was staged in accordance with the law concerning assemblies." This is explicit — the Court directly references the protest as an assembly governed by assembly law.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court implicitly treats the applicant as a participant in the assembly. It discusses his arrest in the context of his protest, and the entire analysis proceeds on the assumption that he was participating. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly addresses this: "he remained peaceful and did not disturb public order in any way" and "He continued to be peaceful and polite even after being manhandled by the police and did not resist the abusive arrest in any way." The conclusion is that the assembly was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "the Court considers that the applicant's arrest constituted 'interference by [a] public authority' with his right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11." The Government had argued there was no interference (the arrest was for failure to cooperate, not for protesting), but the Court rejected this and found interference explicitly.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court addresses this element, though it collapses the three sub-elements into a single conclusion rather than analyzing them separately. The Court states: "the interference with his right of assembly cannot be considered lawful under domestic law." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards — it simply concludes the arrest was not lawful under domestic law. The overall "prescribed by law" question is explicitly discussed and answered negatively. The three sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are not individually discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not discuss legitimate aim. Having found the interference was not prescribed by law, it did not proceed to examine whether a legitimate aim was served. No aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court does not discuss necessity. The analysis stops at the "prescribed by law" stage, consistent with the Court's standard approach of not proceeding to later stages once an earlier stage fails.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (interference by the state), and no positive obligation is identified or analyzed.
