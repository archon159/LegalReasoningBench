**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of Section III.B (Merits): 'There has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The reasoning leading to this conclusion is found in the preceding paragraphs of Section III.B, particularly: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society"'.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court refers to 'his participation in the rally' and 'a public gathering' and 'an unauthorised political gathering.' The existence of an assembly is implied throughout the reasoning — the Court proceeds directly to analyze the interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly without questioning whether an assembly existed. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate element.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes: 'the applicant was arrested and charged with an administrative offence for having participated in an unauthorised political gathering thirty minutes after the start of the rally.' Participation is thus implied as established, though not separately analyzed as a threshold question.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly: 'The Government did not suggest that he had demonstrated a violent behaviour or had in other way disrupted peace or public order.' Earlier, the Court also references its consistent case-law: 'Having examined the proportionality of the interference with the applicant's rights, the Court discerned, in the absence of the acts of violence on the part of the demonstrators, no "pressing social need" for their arrest.' The peaceful character is thus explicitly discussed, though in the context of necessity rather than as a standalone scope question.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly treats the arrest and administrative proceedings as an interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly. The Court states: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society".' The existence of interference is also implied by the entire structure of the analysis — the Court proceeds to assess proportionality, which presupposes interference. The Government itself acknowledged the arrest and fine, arguing they were proportionate. The Court's reference to 'the interference with the applicant's rights' confirms this element is treated as established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law. It does not analyze the foreseeability, accessibility, or adequacy of safeguards of the legal basis for the arrest and fine. The Court jumps directly to necessity. Accordingly, prescribed by law and its sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued the measures were taken 'for the aim of maintaining public order.' The Court does not explicitly accept or reject this as a legitimate aim — it proceeds directly to necessity without formally concluding on legitimate aim. The Government's framing implies 'prevention of disorder' or 'public safety' as the asserted aim, but the Court does not formally endorse it. The legitimate aim element is thus not explicitly discussed by the Court itself, though the Government's submissions imply 'prevention of disorder' / 'public safety.'

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society".' The Court reasons: (1) it has consistently found violations in similar Russian cases where participants were arrested solely because authorities deemed the gathering unauthorised; (2) 'in the absence of the acts of violence on the part of the demonstrators, no "pressing social need" for their arrest and conviction for an administrative offence' was discerned; (3) 'The Government did not suggest that he had demonstrated a violent behaviour or had in other way disrupted peace or public order'; (4) the authorities failed to explain 'why it was impossible for the authorities to allow the demonstrators, including the applicant, to complete their assembly and to impose a reasonable fine on the spot or later on.' The necessity element is explicitly discussed and resolved negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative interference. This element is not discussed.
