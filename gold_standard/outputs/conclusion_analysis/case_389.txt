**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraphs of section B.2.(c):

'Concerning in particular the applicant's involvement in the gathering and its dispersal, even assuming that the forceful terminating of the gathering pursued a legitimate aim and in so far as nothing in the materials before the Court suggests that the applicant committed any reprehensible act during the demonstration, the above findings on the unjustified use of force against her suffice for the Court to conclude that there was a disproportionate interference with her rights under Article 11 of the Convention. In particular, it entailed termination of her participation in the gathering (compare Oya Ataman, cited above, §§ 38-44).'

And the final sentence: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses whether the gathering falls within the scope of Article 11. The Government argued that the 'tumultuous' gathering did not correspond to the legal objective of the demonstration and was therefore not a protected assembly. The Court addresses this in the Admissibility section, noting that 'the right to freedom of assembly is a fundamental right in a democratic society' and 'should not be interpreted restrictively.' The Court also notes that 'Article 11 of the Convention only protects the right to "peaceful assembly", a notion which does not cover a demonstration where the organisers and participants have violent intentions.' The Court implicitly confirms that an assembly existed — both the official demonstration and the spontaneous gathering afterwards — by proceeding to assess whether it was peaceful and whether the applicant participated. In the Merits section, the Court explicitly states: 'It has not been disputed between the parties that the facts of the present application do not relate, as such, to the duly notified demonstration organised by Asociación parad@s en movimiento Valladolid but to the gathering that took place afterwards, when some fifty to sixty participants continued marching spontaneously after the end of the demonstration.' The existence of an assembly is thus explicitly confirmed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation. In the Admissibility section: 'no proceedings have been instituted against the applicant. She was not arrested or detained, nor has she been held responsible of any impugned conduct in respect of the protest.' In the Merits section: 'The Court notes that the applicant's conduct was not established to have been of violent character either during the official demonstration or at the informal gathering afterwards, but she was injured during the police dispersal of the latter.' The Court also notes in the necessity section: 'nothing in the materials before the Court suggests that the applicant committed any reprehensible act during the demonstration.' Participation is explicitly confirmed.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly discusses whether the assembly was peaceful. The Government argued it was not peaceful because of the 'tumultuous' gathering and traffic obstruction. The Court responds in the Admissibility section by noting that 'an individual does not cease to enjoy the right to freedom of peaceful assembly as a result of sporadic violence or other punishable acts committed by others in the course of a demonstration if the individual in question remains peaceful in his or her own intentions or behaviour.' The Court concludes: 'there was no indication whatsoever that the applicant, as one of the protesters, engaged in actions that were violent or not peaceful... or undermining the foundations of a democratic society.' In the Merits section, the Court further confirms: 'the authorities dispersed the spontaneous gathering despite the fact that it had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner up to that point.' The peaceful character is explicitly confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference in section B.2.(a). It reiterates that 'interference with the exercise of freedom of peaceful assembly does not need to amount to an outright ban, legal or de facto, but can consist in various other measures taken by the authorities.' The Court then concludes: 'the Court considers that the facts of the case disclose interference directly related to the applicant's exercise of her right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention on account of the dispersal of the gathering.' Interference is explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court addresses this in section B.2.(b). It notes: 'the restriction imposed on the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly was based on domestic legislation (section 5 of the Right of Assembly Act and section 23 of the Protection of Public Safety Act...), the wording of which was clear. Therefore, the requirement of foreseeability was satisfied.'

**i) Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed and found satisfied: 'the wording of which was clear. Therefore, the requirement of foreseeability was satisfied.'

**ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court mentions the domestic legislation but does not separately address accessibility. This sub-element is not discussed.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court does not address whether the legislation contains adequate safeguards against abuse. This sub-element is not discussed.

However, the Court ultimately declines to fully resolve the lawfulness question, stating: 'given the nature and scope of the applicant's grievances, and in view of its findings below regarding the proportionality of the impugned interference, the Court does not need to delve into matters relating to the legality of the interference and the pursuance of legitimate aims.' So while foreseeability is explicitly found satisfied, the overall prescribed-by-law conclusion is left open (the Court moves on without a definitive overall finding).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly mentions the legitimate aims question but declines to resolve it definitively. In section B.2.(b): 'the Court does not need to delve into matters relating to the legality of the interference and the pursuance of legitimate aims, namely whether the forceful terminating of the gathering intended to pursue the legitimate aims of the "prevention of disorder" and "protection of the rights and freedoms of others" because of the disruption to the movement of road traffic, as the Government stated.' In the necessity section, the Court proceeds 'even assuming that the forceful terminating of the gathering pursued a legitimate aim.' The aims mentioned (but not confirmed) are 'prevention of disorder' and 'protection of the rights and freedoms of others.' The Court does not make a definitive finding on legitimate aim, leaving it open.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly and extensively discussed in section B.2.(c). The Court finds that the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society (i.e., it was disproportionate). Key passages:

- 'the authorities dispersed the spontaneous gathering despite the fact that it had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner up to that point. This already calls into question the Government's assertion concerning the necessity of the dispersal.'
- 'It has not been argued or demonstrated that it would have been difficult for the police to contain or redirect the protesters, or control the situation otherwise, protect public safety and prevent any possible disorder or crime.'
- 'the authorities have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration.'
- 'the nuisance caused by the applicant and her fellow protesters, on a Sunday morning and mostly concentrated on pedestrian streets, caused a certain disruption to ordinary life, but it did not in those specific circumstances exceed the level of minor disturbance that follows from normal exercise of the right of peaceful assembly in a public place.'
- 'the method used by the police to disperse the demonstration was not proportionate.'
- 'the above findings on the unjustified use of force against her suffice for the Court to conclude that there was a disproportionate interference with her rights under Article 11 of the Convention.'

Necessity is explicitly discussed and found NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the State to protect or facilitate the assembly. The entire analysis is framed in terms of a negative obligation (the State must not unjustifiably interfere). Positive obligation is not discussed.
