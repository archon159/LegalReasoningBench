**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was within the scope of Article 11 protection. The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov — all of which involved peaceful assemblies — further implies that the assemblies were peaceful. These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: The opening paragraph states the applicants were "organisers or participants of public assemblies," implying an assembly existed. Implied, conclusion: yes.
- **scope_participation**: The same passage — "organisers or participants of public assemblies" — implies participation. Implied, conclusion: yes.
- **scope_peaceful**: Not explicitly discussed, but implied by the overall framing and reference to leading cases involving peaceful assemblies. Implied, conclusion: yes.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the penultimate paragraph: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Interference is thus explicitly acknowledged. Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. Since the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis (finding the interference not necessary in a democratic society), it does not resolve the prescribed-by-law question. These elements are not_discussed, conclusion: null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim pursued by the interference. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing whether the interference served a legitimate aim. Not discussed, conclusion: null, aims_found: [].

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and the principles in Kudrevičius, Oya Ataman, and Hyde Park. Explicitly discussed, conclusion: no (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary, which is why there is a violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this reasoning. Not discussed, conclusion: null.
