**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

'Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not "necessary in a democratic society".

These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention.'

The Court explicitly states that the complaints 'disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention,' confirming a violation.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness) in the reasoning text. However, scope is implied: the Court refers to 'organisers or participants of public assemblies' and to 'interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly,' which logically presupposes that there were assemblies, that the applicants participated (or tried to), and that the assemblies were within the protective scope of Article 11. The passage 'The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies' implies all three sub-elements are satisfied.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the text refers to 'public assemblies' without any question as to whether an assembly existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as 'organisers or participants of public assemblies.'
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the protection of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not "necessary in a democratic society".' The word 'interferences' is used directly, and the earlier passage describes 'disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence.' This confirms that interference is found to exist.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The sub-elements of 'prescribed by law' (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not explicitly discussed. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, referencing leading cases and concluding that the interferences were not necessary in a democratic society. There is no discussion of whether the legal basis was foreseeable, accessible, or accompanied by adequate safeguards. These elements are not_discussed, and no conclusion can be drawn from the text.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Legitimate aim is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the Russian authorities. However, by proceeding to the necessity analysis, one might imply that a legitimate aim was assumed (since necessity presupposes a legitimate aim), but the Court does not spell this out. Given the brevity of the reasoning and the reliance on leading cases, legitimate aim is at best implied but not explicitly stated, and no specific aim is identified in the text.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not "necessary in a democratic society".' This is the central finding, and the Court concludes that necessity is not satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why a violation is found.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Positive obligations are not discussed anywhere in the reasoning text. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (i.e., unjustified interference by the state). There is no mention of any positive duty on the government to facilitate or protect the assemblies.
