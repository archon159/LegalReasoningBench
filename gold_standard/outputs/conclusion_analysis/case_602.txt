**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final substantive paragraph of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities did not correspond to a pressing social need and was thus not necessary in a democratic society. [...] These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 and of Article 14 in conjunction with Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court explicitly states there is "a breach of Article 11," confirming a violation.

**2. Analysis of canonical Article 11 elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated or tried to participate, and whether the assembly was peaceful). However, these elements are implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies" and to the applicants' "applications for holding these events," which implies that: (i) assemblies were planned/existed; (ii) the applicants sought to participate (they were the organizers/applicants); and (iii) the assemblies were peaceful (the Court applies the Article 11 framework without suggesting otherwise, and refers to Kudrevičius on freedom of peaceful assembly). The peaceful nature is also implied by the fact that the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without questioning whether the assemblies fell outside the protection of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly discuss interference as a separate analytical step, but it is clearly implied. The Court states: "the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities" — this language implies that a governmental act (a ban) interfered with the applicants' freedom of assembly. The Court also refers to the applicants' complaint of "the ban on holding LGBT assemblies and of the discriminatory manner in which the national authorities had treated their applications." Interference is thus implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a standalone element.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law. None of the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are addressed. The Court jumps directly to necessity, which is the only justification element it explicitly addresses. These sub-elements are not discussed and no conclusion is drawn on them.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without first establishing whether the ban served a legitimate aim. No aims are found or discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only justification element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities did not correspond to a pressing social need and was thus not necessary in a democratic society." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied. The Court also references Bączkowski and Others v. Poland on "proportionality of interference" as part of its case-law framework.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. The case is framed entirely in terms of a negative obligation (the state imposed a ban), and no positive obligation analysis is undertaken.

**Summary of implied elements:** Scope (all three sub-elements) and interference are implied by the Court's framing of the case and its reference to "the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies" and the applicants' attempts to hold such events. Prescribed by law and legitimate aim are not discussed at all — the Court appears to have followed the Alekseyev v. Russia leading case approach of proceeding directly to necessity without addressing earlier steps, or treating them as resolved by the leading case reference.
