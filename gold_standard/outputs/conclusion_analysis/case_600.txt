**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to analyze the interference under Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies fell outside its protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The Court then states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming this element is found affirmatively.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its leading case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without reaching prescribed by law, these elements remain not_discussed. No conclusion can be drawn.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches a negative conclusion on necessity (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court references its case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and the leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) as the basis for this conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
