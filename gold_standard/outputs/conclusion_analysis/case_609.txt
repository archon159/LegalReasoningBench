**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits assessment: "Having regard to the above, the Court considers that in the instant case the forceful intervention of the police vis-à-vis the applicant was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder or the protection of the rights of others within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention. There has accordingly been a violation of that provision."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly refers to the event as a "demonstration" and an "assembly" throughout the judgment. For example: "The Court observes that the demonstration which was held on 26 November 2005 in Galaba Square was peaceful and was carried out according to the rules on holding of assemblies." The existence of an assembly is treated as undisputed and is explicitly discussed.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation. For example: "It is undisputed by the parties that the assembly was conducted peacefully until 5 p.m." and references to "the demonstrators, including the applicant" appear throughout. The applicant's participation is explicitly discussed and undisputed.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly addresses this: "The Court observes that the demonstration which was held on 26 November 2005 in Galaba Square was peaceful and was carried out according to the rules on holding of assemblies." Further: "It is undisputed by the parties that the assembly was conducted peacefully until 5 p.m." and "there is no evidence to suggest that in the instant case the applicant or other demonstrators presented a danger to public order." The peaceful nature is explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "The Court notes that there is no dispute between the parties, and the Court agrees, that there was an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly." This is explicit and affirmative.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this: "The potential dispersal of a demonstration by the police and the use of force by the latter in this respect were set out in Articles 7 and 14 of the Law on Freedom of Assembly, which were the legal basis for the police intervention in the present case. The Court accepts that the interference was 'prescribed by law'..." The Court accepts the prescribed-by-law requirement is satisfied, but does so in a summary fashion without separately analyzing foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed — the Court simply accepts the overall prescribed-by-law requirement without breaking it down.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "The Court accepts that the interference was 'prescribed by law' and that it pursued 'legitimate aims' within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Article 11, those of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others." Two aims are found: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. Key passages include: "It remains to be determined whether it was 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court then reasons: "The Court cannot, however, consider acceptable the forcible dispersal of a peaceful demonstration by the police and their treatment of the applicant, merely on account of a delay on the part of the demonstrators in dispersing." And: "there is no evidence to suggest that in the instant case the applicant or other demonstrators presented a danger to public order." And: "where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings if the freedom of assembly guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention is not to be deprived of all substance." The Court concludes: "the forceful intervention of the police vis-à-vis the applicant was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder or the protection of the rights of others." Necessity is explicitly discussed and found not to be satisfied.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly frame any part of its analysis in terms of a positive obligation. The analysis is entirely framed as a negative obligation (unjustified interference). However, there is a passing reference to the general principle that "States must not only safeguard the right to assemble peacefully, but must also refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon that right" — but this is stated as a general principle and not applied as a distinct positive obligation analysis. Positive obligation is not discussed as a separate element in this case.
