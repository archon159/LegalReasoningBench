**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies")
- scope_participation: implied yes (the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court proceeds to find a violation under Article 11, which only protects peaceful assemblies; no suggestion of violence is made)

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze whether there was an interference as a separate step, but it clearly implies it. The opening paragraph states the applicants complained of "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The Court then states "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'," which presupposes that an interference existed. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a standalone element.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing this step. The legitimate aim element is not discussed, and no specific aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law, specifically citing Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia as leading cases finding violations "in respect of issues similar to those in the present case," and noting it found "no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion." The necessity element is explicitly discussed and answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary, hence a violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
