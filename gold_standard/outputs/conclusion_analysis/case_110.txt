**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of section B.2(b): "There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court reached this conclusion primarily on the basis that the interference was not prescribed by law: "the manner in which restrictions were imposed on the applicant's exercise of her freedom of assembly was not 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In section B.2(a), it states: "the applicant was refused permit to attend a meeting held on 20 June 1997 in southern Cyprus" and describes it as "a bi-communal meeting." In the general principles section, the Court notes that the right "covers both private meetings and meetings in public thoroughfares as well as static meetings and public processions." The meeting organised by a radio station qualifies as an assembly. This is explicitly discussed and the conclusion is affirmative.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's attempt to participate: "the refusal of the authorities to grant a permit to the applicant barred her participation in a bi-communal meeting there, preventing her consequently from engaging in peaceful assembly with people from both communities." The applicant had applied in advance for permission to cross the green line to attend the meeting. This is explicitly discussed and the conclusion is affirmative.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court refers to the assembly as involving "peaceful assembly" in section B.2(a): "preventing her consequently from engaging in peaceful assembly with people from both communities." The Court also notes in the prescribed-by-law section that there is "no law applicable in the present case regulating the issuance of permits to Turkish Cypriots living in northern Cyprus to cross the 'green line' into southern Cyprus in order to engage in peaceful assembly with Greek Cypriots." The peaceful nature of the assembly is thus explicitly affirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference in section B.2(a): "In view of the above, the Court concludes that there has been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention." The Court notes that "the refusal of the authorities to grant a permit to the applicant barred her participation in a bi-communal meeting there" and that "that hindrance can amount to a violation of the Convention just like a legal impediment."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element in section B.2(b). It reiterates the foreseeability requirement: "one of the requirements flowing from the expression 'prescribed by law' is the foreseeability of the measure concerned. A rule cannot be regarded as 'law' unless it is formulated with sufficient precision to enable the citizen to regulate his conduct."

**(i) Foreseeability:** Explicitly discussed and found to be absent. The Court states: "the respondent Government referred to general restrictions concerning crossing of the 'green line'... They did not refer to any law or measures in the 'TRNC' regulating the issuance of permits to Turkish Cypriots living in northern Cyprus to cross the 'green line' into southern Cyprus for the purposes of attending bi-communal meetings. Furthermore, they did not provide any indication as to when refusal of such permits is allowed." The Court concludes: "there seems to be no law applicable in the present case regulating the issuance of permits... Therefore, the manner in which restrictions were imposed on the applicant's exercise of her freedom of assembly was not 'prescribed by law'." Conclusion: no.

**(ii) Accessibility:** Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element. The Court focuses on foreseeability and the absence of any applicable law, but does not separately address accessibility. However, the finding that there is no applicable law at all implies that accessibility is also not satisfied. This is implied rather than explicit. Conclusion: no (implied).

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court notes that the government "did not provide any indication as to when refusal of such permits is allowed," which touches on the absence of safeguards, but this is not framed as a separate sub-element analysis. This is implied by the overall finding of no law. Conclusion: no (implied).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states: "In the light of the foregoing, the Court does not consider it necessary to examine whether the other requirements laid down by Article 11 § 2 of the Convention were satisfied." Since the interference was found not to be prescribed by law, the Court did not proceed to examine legitimate aim. This element is not discussed. No aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Similarly, the Court explicitly declined to examine this element: "the Court does not consider it necessary to examine whether the other requirements laid down by Article 11 § 2 of the Convention were satisfied." Not discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court mentions positive obligations in the general principles section: "although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities with the exercise of the rights protected, there may in addition be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these rights (see Christians against Racism and Fascism, cited above, p. 148)." However, the Court does not apply this concept to the specific facts of the case or reach a conclusion on whether a positive obligation was violated. It is mentioned in passing as a general principle but not applied. This is mentioned but not discussed in application — it is best characterized as explicitly mentioned but not applied to reach a conclusion, so the conclusion is null.
