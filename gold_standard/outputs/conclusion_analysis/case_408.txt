**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court finds a violation of Article 11 ECHR in respect of all applicants. There are two separate findings:

For the first group (Mr Mahammad Majidli, Mr Arif Hajili, Mr Fuad Gahramanli, Mr Tural Abbasli, Mr Babak Hasanov, Mr Elshan Hasanov, Mr Sahib Karimov, Mr Zulfugar Eyvazov and Mr Rufat Hajibeyli): 'There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of Mr Mahammad Majidli, Mr Arif Hajili, Mr Fuad Gahramanli, Mr Tural Abbasli, Mr Babak Hasanov, Mr Elshan Hasanov, Mr Sahib Karimov, Mr Zulfugar Eyvazov and Mr Rufat Hajibeyli (applications nos. 56317/11, 67932/11, 59661/12, 27472/12, 6814/13, 7990/13, 12717/13, 6903/13 and 25680/14).'

For the second group (Mr Arif Alishli and Mr Ulvu Guliyev): 'There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of Mr Arif Alishli and Mr Ulvu Guliyev (applications nos. 28563/13 and 23365/14).'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court explicitly discusses scope in the Admissibility section. It addresses all three sub-elements:

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly confirms there was an assembly: 'The Court has previously held that the assembly in Baku on 2 April 2011 had been intended to be peaceful and therefore fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention.' The existence of the assembly (a demonstration on 2 April 2011) is treated as established.

**ii) Participation:** The Court implies participation by the applicants. It states: 'the applicants enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention' and 'the applicants were arrested, detained and convicted for participation in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration.' Participation is not separately analyzed but is clearly implied as a given.

**iii) Peaceful character:** The Court explicitly discusses this: 'it does not transpire from the case files that the applicants had violent intentions and were responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's peaceful character.' And: 'the assembly in Baku on 2 April 2011 had been intended to be peaceful.' For the second group (Alishli and Guliyev), the Court acknowledges some violent conduct but finds it was 'sporadic in nature and appeared to be a spontaneous reaction to the force used by the police' and that 'the applicants were not responsible for the deterioration of the assembly's peaceful character.' The assembly is thus treated as peaceful for Article 11 purposes for all applicants.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: 'The Court also finds that the applicants' arrest and ensuing prosecution and criminal conviction for participation in the said demonstration constituted an interference with the exercise of freedom of assembly.' The Government also conceded interference: 'The Government submitted that there had been an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly due to the dispersal of the demonstration of 2 April 2011.'

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to examine this element: 'The Court considers that in the circumstances of the present cases there is no need to examine whether the interference was "prescribed by law" or pursued one or more of the aims set out in Article 11 § 2, having regard to its conclusions set out below regarding the necessity of the interference.' The Court then proceeds on the assumption that the measures complied with domestic law: 'even assuming that the dispersal of the demonstration as well as the applicants' arrest, criminal prosecution and conviction complied with domestic law...' Thus, prescribed by law is not decided — the sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are also not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this but proceeds on an assumption. For the first group: 'even assuming that the dispersal of the demonstration as well as the applicants' arrest, criminal prosecution and conviction complied with domestic law and pursued one of the legitimate aims enumerated in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention – presumably, prevention of disorder and crime and protection of the rights and freedoms of others...' For the second group: 'even assuming that the applicants' criminal convictions complied with domestic law and pursued one of the legitimate aims enumerated in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention – presumably, prevention of disorder and crime and protection of the rights and freedoms of others...' The Court thus identifies 'prevention of disorder and crime' and 'protection of the rights and freedoms of others' as the presumed aims, but does not formally find them established.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and decided. For the first group, the Court finds: 'the measures in question were disproportionate and could not but have the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies. Undoubtedly, those measures had a serious potential also to deter other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations and, more generally, from participating in open political debate.' For the second group, the Court finds: 'they cannot be considered as proportionate measures in the circumstances of the cases. Their convictions, and especially the severity of their sentences, could not but have had the effect of discouraging them and other opposition supporters, as well as the public at large, from attending demonstrations.' The necessity test is thus explicitly decided in the negative (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
