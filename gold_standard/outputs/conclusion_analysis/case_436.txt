**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is found in the final two paragraphs of the merits assessment:

"In the light of the above, the Court concludes that the domestic courts failed in their duty to provide reasons for their decisions and that the reasons adduced to justify the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly were not 'relevant and sufficient'."

"There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The existence of an assembly is explicitly discussed. The Court refers to "the nine-day uninterrupted assembly at Freedom Square" and the "post-election protest movement" and "rallies which gripped the Armenian capital." More specifically, the Court states: "the demonstrations held at Freedom Square were found by the Court to constitute a peaceful assembly and in fact a platform for expression on a matter of major political importance directly related to the functioning of a democracy." The assembly's existence is treated as established and uncontested.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The applicant's participation is explicitly discussed. The Court notes: "the applicant's conviction for 'organising mass disorder' concerned his involvement in the post-election protest movement, including his participation and speeches made [at] the assembly at Freedom Square." The Court also notes he was "an opposition member of parliament and an active supporter of Mr Ter-Petrosyan who took part in the rallies."

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
This is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court is mindful that it has already scrutinised the circumstances of the assembly at Freedom Square and found that it had been peaceful, without any incitement to violence or acts of violence (see Mushegh Saghatelyan, cited above, § 245, and Myasnik Malkhasyan, cited above, § 72)." The Government disputed this, arguing the assembly was not peaceful, but the Court rejected that argument.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the applicant's conviction for 'organising mass disorder' concerned his involvement in the post-election protest movement, including his participation and speeches made [at] the assembly at Freedom Square, and therefore amounted to an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court also reiterates the general principle that "restrictions" include "punitive measures, taken afterwards, including penalties imposed for having taken part in a rally." Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this question: "In the present case, the Court does not consider it necessary to decide whether the interference was prescribed by law and pursued a legitimate aim having regard to its conclusions set out below, regarding the necessity of the interference." The applicant had argued that Articles 225 and 300 of the CC lacked clarity and foreseeability, but the Court did not rule on this. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are therefore not discussed or resolved.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Also explicitly not decided. The Court states: "the Court does not consider it necessary to decide whether the interference was prescribed by law and pursued a legitimate aim having regard to its conclusions set out below, regarding the necessity of the interference." No specific legitimate aim is found or confirmed. The applicant mentioned "prevention of disorder and crime or the protection of the health, morals or the rights of others" as possible aims the Government might invoke, but the Court did not rule on this.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the necessity test in detail. Key passages include:

- "the right to freedom of assembly, one of the foundations of a democratic society, is subject to a number of exceptions which must be narrowly interpreted and the necessity for any restrictions must be convincingly established."
- "peaceful participants may not be held responsible for reprehensible acts committed by others."
- "the organisers of the event should not be held responsible for the conduct of its participants as long as they themselves do not commit, incite or condone any reprehensible acts."
- "the domestic courts failed to provide sufficiently specific examples of any reprehensible acts attributable to the applicant, including incitement or instigation of violence."
- "the applicant appears to have been sanctioned for being an active member of the opposition and for having availed himself of his right to freedom of peaceful assembly rather than committing any reprehensible acts."
- "the domestic courts failed in their duty to provide reasons for their decisions and that the reasons adduced to justify the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly were not 'relevant and sufficient'."

Conclusion: the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society (i.e., the necessity test was not satisfied by the government).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly discuss a positive obligation of the state to protect or facilitate the assembly. The entire analysis is framed in terms of a negative obligation (unjustified interference). Positive obligations are not discussed.

**Summary of structured conclusions:**
- Scope (overall): yes, implied (assembly exists, applicant participated, assembly was peaceful — all confirmed)
- Assembly exists: yes, explicit
- Participation: yes, explicit
- Peaceful: yes, explicit
- Interference: yes, explicit
- Prescribed by law: null (not decided), explicitly noted as not decided
- Prescribed by law foreseeable: null, not discussed
- Prescribed by law accessible: null, not discussed
- Prescribed by law safeguards: null, not discussed
- Legitimate aim: null (not decided), explicitly noted as not decided; aims_found: []
- Necessity: no (interference not necessary), explicit
- Positive obligation: not discussed
