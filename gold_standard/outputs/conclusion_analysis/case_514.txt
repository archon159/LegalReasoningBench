**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of Section B (Merits): "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court's reasoning leading to this conclusion is found in the Merits section: "This consideration is sufficient to enable the Court to conclude that the interference was devoid of a basis in domestic law and cannot as such be regarded as 'prescribed by law'. It is therefore not necessary to embark on an examination of its legitimate aim or necessity in a democratic society."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The scope elements (assembly exists, participation, peaceful) are not explicitly discussed by the Court. However, they are implied. The Court states: "it was not in dispute between the parties that there had been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly." This statement implies that: (i) there was an assembly (the planned demonstration); (ii) the applicant participated or intended to participate; and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (the Court uses the phrase "peaceful assembly" without qualification). All three sub-elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly analyzed.

**(b) Interference**

The interference element is explicitly discussed, though briefly. The Court states: "The Court notes at the outset that it was not in dispute between the parties that there had been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly – and it has no reason to hold otherwise." The Court thus explicitly confirms the existence of an interference, accepting the parties' common position.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

This element is explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court states: "Turning to the legal basis of the interference in the present case, the Court notes that section 8(1) of the Assembly Act provides for two reasons for banning a planned demonstration... However, the authorities dealing with the applicant's case did not rely on any of these reasons." The Court further notes that the Constitutional Court found the lower court had "unlawfully expanded the grounds for banning a demonstration," and that "this ruling has effectively, if retroactively, removed the legal basis of the impugned measure." The Court concludes: "the interference was devoid of a basis in domestic law and cannot as such be regarded as 'prescribed by law'."

Regarding the sub-elements:
- **Foreseeability**: Not explicitly discussed as a standalone sub-element. The Court's analysis focuses on whether the legal basis existed at all (the authorities used grounds not listed in the Assembly Act), rather than on whether the law was foreseeable in its application. This is not_discussed as a separate sub-element.
- **Accessibility**: Not discussed.
- **Safeguards**: Not discussed.

The overall "prescribed by law" conclusion is negative (no, the interference was not prescribed by law), discussed explicitly.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly declines to examine this element: "It is therefore not necessary to embark on an examination of its legitimate aim or necessity in a democratic society." The Government had argued the aims of "protecting the private life and free movement of others and the statutory right of official dignitaries to protection" (which would fall under "rights of others"), but the Court did not make any finding on this. The element is mentioned but not decided.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Similarly, the Court explicitly declines to examine this element: "It is therefore not necessary to embark on an examination of its legitimate aim or necessity in a democratic society." Not decided.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference case. This element is not discussed.
