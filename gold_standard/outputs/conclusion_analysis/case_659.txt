**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court finds a violation of Article 11 ECHR in two separate respects:

1. Regarding the demonstration planned in front of the homes of the prime minister and the bank CEO: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the demonstration planned in front of the homes of the prime minister and the bank CEO.'

2. Regarding the demonstration planned before the Kúria: 'It follows that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the demonstration planned in front of the Kúria.'

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied throughout the reasoning. The entire analysis presupposes that the planned demonstration constitutes an assembly within the meaning of Article 11. The Court refers to 'the demonstration planned by the applicant' and treats it as falling within the scope of Article 11 without question. This is implied.

**(ii) Participation:** The applicant is described as the organiser of the demonstration. The Court states 'the competent authorities banned the demonstration planned by the applicant.' The applicant's participation/organisation is implied throughout but not explicitly discussed as a separate element.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court refers throughout to 'freedom of peaceful assembly' and the applicant's 'right to freedom of peaceful assembly.' The Court notes that the organisers agreed to amend their programme and not use megaphones, and that the planned demonstration did not amount to 'contempt of court or otherwise wrongful acts.' The peaceful nature is implied rather than explicitly analysed as a separate sub-element.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court considers that the ban on holding the demonstration at those locations constituted an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly. The fact that the applicant was able to organise the demonstration at other venues is of no relevance in this respect.' The Government also agreed: 'The Government agreed that there had been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly.' Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed, but the analysis differs between the two parts of the case.

**Regarding the homes of the prime minister and bank CEO:** The Court finds the interference was NOT prescribed by law. It notes: 'The Court notes that the list of circumstances in section 8(1) of the Assembly Act was exhaustive... the national law, as in force at the material time, did not provide for the possibility of prohibiting planned assemblies under other circumstances than those listed in section 8(1) of the Assembly Act... The protection of the rights of residents, invoked by the police and the Administrative and Labour Court, was set out in section 2(3) of the Assembly Act and together with section 14 provided for a separate procedure... Thus, the provision relied on by the police and the Administrative and Labour Court could not serve as the grounds for banning a demonstration.' The Court concludes: 'the interference was devoid of a basis in domestic law and cannot as such be regarded as "prescribed by law".' Conclusion: no.

**Regarding the Kúria:** The Court notes 'It has not been disputed by the parties that the restriction had a legal basis, namely section 8 of the Assembly Act.' Conclusion: yes (not disputed).

**(i) Foreseeable:** The Court recites the foreseeability standard: 'a norm cannot be regarded as a "law" unless it is formulated with sufficient precision to enable the citizen – if need be, with appropriate advice – to foresee, to a degree that is reasonable in the circumstances, the consequences which a given action may entail.' In the context of the homes demonstration, the Court finds the interference lacked a legal basis entirely, which encompasses foreseeability. The Court does not separately analyse foreseeability as a distinct sub-element beyond the general 'prescribed by law' analysis. For the Kúria, the legal basis was not disputed. Conclusion: no (for homes demonstration), yes (for Kúria, implied).

**(ii) Accessible:** The Court recites the accessibility requirement as part of the standard: 'the expressions "prescribed by law" and "in accordance with the law" in Articles 8 to 11 of the Convention not only requires that the impugned measure should have a legal basis in domestic law, but also refers to the quality of the law in question, which should be accessible to the person concerned and foreseeable as to its effects.' However, the Court does not separately analyse accessibility as a distinct sub-element; it focuses on the absence of a legal basis. Not separately discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards:** The Court mentions that the Constitutional Court 'stated that the legislative framework did not provide for any procedure for the relevant authorities to resolve the supposed conflict of fundamental rights – that is the right to freedom of peaceful assembly and the rights of others – when deciding whether to authorise demonstrations, leading to discrepancies in the system.' This touches on the absence of safeguards but is not framed as a separate 'safeguards' analysis. Implied rather than explicit.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed for the Kúria demonstration. The Court states: 'the Court has already found that a ban on holding public events in the immediate vicinity of court buildings may have served a legitimate interest, namely that of protecting the judicial process in a specific case from outside influence, and thereby protecting the rights of others, namely the parties to judicial proceedings... as well as that of "preventing disorder"... Thus, the Court accepts that the restriction pursued "legitimate aims" within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Article 11, those of protecting the rights of others and preventing disorder.' Aims found: rights_of_others, prevention_of_disorder.

For the homes demonstration, the Court explicitly states it is 'not necessary to embark on an examination of its legitimate aim or necessity in a democratic society' given the finding that the interference was not prescribed by law. So legitimate aim is not discussed for that part.

Overall conclusion: yes (for Kúria), not discussed (for homes).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed for the Kúria demonstration. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary. Key passages: 'the facts of the case call into question the assertion concerning the potential level of disruption to the functioning of the judiciary'; 'the demonstration planned in front of the Kúria was to last only a brief period of time (ten minutes only)'; 'the demonstrators showed flexibility in changing the manner in which they intended to convey their message'; 'the domestic authorities relied on the statement of the vice-president of the Kúria without further inquiry into the circumstances of the planned demonstration... In practice this approach excluded any balancing of the interests of the demonstrators against the interest in preserving the appearance of an independent judiciary'; 'the reason put forward by the national authorities of protecting the Kúria from outside influence was relevant but not sufficient in the circumstances of the present case to justify an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly.' Conclusion: no (not necessary).

For the homes demonstration, necessity was not examined: 'It is therefore not necessary to embark on an examination of its legitimate aim or necessity in a democratic society.'

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The Court's analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). There is no mention of positive obligations in the reasoning. Conclusion: not discussed.
