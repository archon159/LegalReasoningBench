**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits assessment:

"In the light of the above, the Court concludes that the domestic courts failed in their duty to provide reasons for their decisions and that the reasons adduced to justify the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly were not 'relevant and sufficient'."

"There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court refers to "the rallies held at Yerevan's Freedom Square between 20 February and 1 March 2008" and the "post-election protest movement" as an assembly. More specifically, in the interference section: "the applicant's conviction for 'organising mass disorder' concerned his involvement in the post-election protest movement, including his participation and speeches made at the assembly at Freedom Square." The Court also references prior findings: "the demonstrations held at Freedom Square were found by the Court to constitute a peaceful assembly and in fact a platform for expression on a matter of major political importance" (citing Mushegh Saghatelyan, §§ 230-33 and 246). Conclusion: yes.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court notes: "the applicant's conviction for 'organising mass disorder' concerned his involvement in the post-election protest movement, including his participation and speeches made at the assembly at Freedom Square." The applicant is described as "an opposition politician and a high profile member of Mr Ter-Petrosyan's pre-election campaign who took part in the rallies." Conclusion: yes.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court is mindful that it has already scrutinised the circumstances of the assembly at Freedom Square and found that it had been peaceful, without any incitement to violence or acts of violence (see Mushegh Saghatelyan, cited above, § 245, and Myasnik Malkhasyan, cited above, § 72)." And further: "the demonstrations held at Freedom Square were found by the Court to constitute a peaceful assembly." The Government disputed this, arguing the assembly "had not been peaceful and had had violent intentions," but the Court rejected this view. Conclusion: yes.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the applicant's conviction for 'organising mass disorder' concerned his involvement in the post-election protest movement, including his participation and speeches made at the assembly at Freedom Square, and therefore amounted to an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly. For the same reason, the Court rejects the Government's objection as regards the lack of victim status." The Court also reiterates the general principle: "The term 'restrictions' in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an assembly... and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards, including penalties imposed for having taken part in a rally." Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this question: "In the present case, the Court does not consider it necessary to decide whether the interference was prescribed by law and pursued a legitimate aim having regard to its conclusions set out below, regarding the necessity of the interference." Therefore, the overall "prescribed by law" question is discussed (explicitly noted as not needing resolution) but left unresolved. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually examined. The applicant had argued that Articles 225 and 300 CC "lacked clarity and foreseeability," but the Court did not engage with these sub-elements. Conclusion: null for all prescribed-by-law elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Also explicitly declined: "the Court does not consider it necessary to decide whether the interference was prescribed by law and pursued a legitimate aim having regard to its conclusions set out below, regarding the necessity of the interference." The Government had asserted aims of "protecting national security, health, morals and the rights of others, and of preventing disorder and crime," but the Court did not make findings on these. Conclusion: null; aims_found: [].

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the necessity test in detail. Key passages include:

- "the right to freedom of assembly, one of the foundations of a democratic society, is subject to a number of exceptions which must be narrowly interpreted and the necessity for any restrictions must be convincingly established."
- "peaceful participants may not be held responsible for reprehensible acts committed by others."
- "the domestic courts failed to provide concrete examples of such behaviour which, in the Court's opinion, could be characterised as incitement to violence."
- "the findings of the domestic courts regarding the applicant's alleged responsibility for the violence in question were drafted in very general and abstract terms, without any specific factual details which would convincingly point to any wrongdoing by the applicant."
- "the domestic courts failed to provide specific examples of any reprehensible acts attributable to the applicant... the applicant appears to have been sanctioned for being an active member of the opposition and for having availed himself of his right to freedom of peaceful assembly rather than committing any reprehensible acts."
- "the Court concludes that the domestic courts failed in their duty to provide reasons for their decisions and that the reasons adduced to justify the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly were not 'relevant and sufficient'."

Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society — i.e., the necessity requirement was NOT satisfied by the government).

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (the state interfered with the applicant's rights through prosecution and conviction). There is no mention of any positive obligation on the part of the Armenian government to facilitate or protect the assembly. Conclusion: null / not_discussed.
