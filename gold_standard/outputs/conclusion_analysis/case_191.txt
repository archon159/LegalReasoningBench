**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The opening paragraph states that "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies." This implies that: (i) assemblies existed, (ii) the applicants participated in them, and (iii) the assemblies were peaceful (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies fell outside Article 11 protection). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as "yes" — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "these assemblies" without questioning their existence.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as "yes" — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as "yes" — the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without any suggestion that the assemblies were violent or outside the protection of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court's conclusion refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly," confirming that interference is explicitly found. The conclusion passage reads: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" (plural) confirms the Court treats this as established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its leading case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, these sub-elements remain not_discussed, with null conclusions.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. Legitimate aim is not discussed, and no aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches a negative conclusion on necessity (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court references leading cases — Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia — as establishing the relevant principles, and also cites Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania [GC] for general principles on freedom of assembly and proportionality.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. There is no mention of a duty on the government to facilitate or protect the assembly. This element is not discussed.
