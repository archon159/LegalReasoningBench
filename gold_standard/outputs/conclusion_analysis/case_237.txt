**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is found in the final paragraph of Section B.3 (The Court's assessment):

'Having regard to the facts of the present cases and their clear similarity to those of Huseynli and Others (cited above, §§ 97-101) and Huseynov and Others (cited above, §§ 45-51) on all relevant and crucial points, the Court sees no particular circumstances that could compel it to deviate from its findings in those judgments, and finds that in the present cases the applicants' right to freedom of assembly was breached for the same reasons as those outlined in the above-mentioned judgments. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior judgments in Huseynli and Others and Huseynov and Others, finding the present cases sufficiently similar to those precedents.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful) in the reasoning section provided. However, the Court's framing of the complaint — 'their arrest and conviction had been measures used by the authorities to prevent them from participating in peaceful demonstrations' — implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The word 'peaceful demonstrations' implies the assembly was peaceful, the applicants were participants (or attempted participants), and that an assembly existed. These are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly discuss interference as a standalone element in the reasoning provided. However, the reference to 'arrest and conviction' as measures used to prevent participation in demonstrations implies that an interference with the right to freedom of assembly is taken as established. This is implied by the overall framing and by the Court's reference to the prior judgments where these issues were addressed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The sub-elements of 'prescribed by law' (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not explicitly discussed in the reasoning provided. The Court simply refers to its findings in Huseynli and Others and Huseynov and Others without spelling out the analysis. Given that the Court finds a violation of Article 11, and given that the violation in the referenced cases was found at the necessity/proportionality stage (based on the structure of those judgments as referenced), it is unclear whether 'prescribed by law' was found to be satisfied or not in those cases from the text provided here. The sub-elements are not discussed — not even impliedly — in the text before us.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Legitimate aim is not explicitly discussed in the reasoning provided. The Court makes no mention of any specific legitimate aim. As with prescribed by law, the Court simply defers to its findings in the referenced judgments.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Necessity is not explicitly discussed in the reasoning provided. The Court again defers to its findings in Huseynli and Others and Huseynov and Others. However, since the Court finds a violation of Article 11, and the violation must logically rest on a failure of one of the justification elements, necessity (or possibly prescribed by law) is implied as the element at which the interference failed. Given the structure of the referenced cases (which typically found violations at the necessity stage in similar Azerbaijani assembly cases), necessity is implied as not satisfied.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Positive obligation is not discussed at all in the reasoning provided — neither explicitly nor impliedly. The case is framed as one of direct interference by state authorities (arrest and conviction), not as a failure to protect assembly rights from third-party interference.
