**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final conclusion is stated in the last two paragraphs of the merits section:

'In view of the above, the Court considers that in the instant case the forceful intervention of the police officers was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder.'

'There has therefore been a breach of Article 11 of the Convention in the instant case.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

*Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether a 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 existed, but it is implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to 'Labour Day Celebrations,' 'demonstration,' 'peaceful assembly,' and 'gathering' without questioning whether these constitute an assembly. The interference section states: 'the applicants were not able to exercise their right to peaceful assembly.' This implies the existence of an assembly is taken for granted.

*Participation:* The Court implies that the applicants participated (or attempted to participate) in the assembly. It states: 'as a result of the police intervention, the applicants were not able to exercise their right to peaceful assembly.' This implies they were participants. Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element.

*Peaceful:* The Court addresses this implicitly and explicitly. It notes: 'there is no evidence to support this allegation [that members of an illegal organisation threw stones],' 'there is also nothing to suggest that the group waiting in front of the DISK headquarters presented a danger to public order or engaged in violent acts,' and 'There is also no information in the file that the police had encountered any violent or active physical resistance.' Furthermore: 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings.' The assembly is treated as peaceful throughout.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court notes that as a result of the police intervention, the applicants were not able to exercise their right to peaceful assembly (see paragraph 10 above), and was thus negatively affected. There has therefore been an interference with their rights under Article 11 of the Convention.' The Government contested this, but the Court found interference clearly established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed, but only briefly and affirmatively: 'it is noted that the interference in the present case had a legal basis, namely Sections 22 and 24 of the Meetings and Demonstration Marches Act, and was thus "prescribed by law" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.'

The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are NOT individually discussed. The Court simply concludes the interference was prescribed by law without breaking down the analysis into these sub-elements. Therefore, foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not discussed (the overall 'prescribed by law' conclusion is yes, but the sub-elements are not_discussed individually).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: 'As regards a legitimate aim, the Government submitted that the interference pursued, among others, the legitimate aim of preventing public disorder, and the Court finds no reason to differ.' The aim found is prevention of disorder (prevention_of_disorder).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the proportionality test and concludes the interference was NOT necessary:

- 'the Court considers that in the present case it is not called on to pronounce on the choice of the venue of the demonstrations... since in any case the police intervention took place in the early hours of 1 May 2008, even before the demonstration commenced. The Court should therefore determine whether the intervention of the security forces was proportionate to the aim pursued.'
- 'the police intervention commenced at 6.30 a.m., before the demonstrators started their march'
- 'there is no evidence to support this allegation [stones thrown by illegal organisation]'
- 'there is also nothing to suggest that the group waiting in front of the DISK headquarters presented a danger to public order or engaged in violent acts'
- 'the use of a gas bomb in hospital premises cannot be considered as necessary or proportionate in the circumstances of the present case'
- 'the forceful intervention of the police officers was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder'

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court mentions positive obligations in passing but does not make a finding of violation based on positive obligations. It states: 'although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities in the exercise of the rights protected, there may also be positive obligations to secure their effective enjoyment (see Djavit An, cited above, § 57, and Oya Ataman, cited above, § 36).' And: 'States must not only safeguard freedom of peaceful assembly, but must also refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon that right.' However, the Court's ultimate finding of violation is based on the negative obligation (disproportionate interference), not on a positive obligation. The positive obligation is mentioned but not the basis for the violation finding. The conclusion on positive obligation is therefore null (mentioned but not resolved as a separate finding).
