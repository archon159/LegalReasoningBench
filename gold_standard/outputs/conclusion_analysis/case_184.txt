**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated or tried to participate, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without suggesting the assembly fell outside Article 11 protection). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly reasoned.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies" without questioning whether an assembly existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and their "freedom of assembly" is directly engaged.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without any suggestion that the assemblies were non-peaceful or fell outside Article 11 protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used directly, and earlier the Court describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity, relying on its prior case-law. The sub-elements are therefore not_discussed, with null conclusions.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or assessed. This element is not discussed. However, since the Court proceeds to assess necessity (which logically presupposes a legitimate aim), one might argue it is implied — but the Court does not even implicitly identify an aim; it simply finds the interference not necessary. Given the complete absence of any reference to a legitimate aim, this is best classified as not_discussed with a null conclusion and no aims found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that necessity was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" (necessity was not established), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent.
