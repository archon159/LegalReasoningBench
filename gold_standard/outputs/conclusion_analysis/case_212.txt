**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The scope elements are not explicitly discussed in the text. However, they are implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "participants of public assemblies" and "the applicants' freedom of assembly," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated in it, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity, which logically presupposes that the assembly fell within the protective scope of Article 11, including the peacefulness requirement).

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies," implying assemblies existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied — the Court refers to the applicants as "participants of public assemblies" and discusses "the applicants' freedom of assembly."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the scope of Article 11 protection.

**(b) Interference**

The interference element is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in its conclusion, confirming that interference is found.

Quote: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'" — this explicitly presupposes and confirms that interference was found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

This element is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not address whether the interference was prescribed by law, foreseeable, accessible, or accompanied by sufficient safeguards. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim element is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the interference. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. However, since the Court discusses necessity (which logically presupposes a legitimate aim), one could argue legitimate aim is implied. That said, the Court does not name any aim, so it is best characterized as not_discussed (or at most implied without specifying any aim).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This element is explicitly discussed and is the basis for the violation finding. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the explicit conclusion on necessity, finding that the necessity requirement was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The positive obligation element is not discussed at all in this reasoning section. The Court's analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference), not positive obligations to facilitate or protect assemblies.
