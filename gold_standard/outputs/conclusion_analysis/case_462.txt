**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated (as "organisers or participants of public assemblies"), and the assembly must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). The opening paragraph also describes the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies the scope elements are met.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether an assembly existed.
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court applies Article 11 protection without questioning the peaceful nature of the assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the necessity analysis: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes "disproportionate measures taken against them... namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Interference is thus explicitly acknowledged, though not separately analyzed as a standalone step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity, relying on its prior case-law.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) without conducting a detailed proportionality analysis.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent.
