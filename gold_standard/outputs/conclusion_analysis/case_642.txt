**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two sentences of the merits assessment: 'Thus, a fair balance between the legitimate aim and the means for attaining it was not attained. Accordingly, the Court does not consider that disruption to the traffic in the direction of the airport was sufficient reason for a total ban on the demonstration. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness) as separate analytical steps. However, scope is implied by the Court's statement: 'The Court notes that the Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the ban on the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of their rights under that provision. It sees no reason to hold otherwise.' This implies that the assembly existed, that the applicants were participants/organisers, and that the assembly was peaceful (the Court later notes 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence' in a general principle, and the entire case is treated as a peaceful assembly). The peacefulness is also implied by the applicants' own framing ('peaceful gathering') and the Court's reference to 'peaceful gatherings' in the necessity analysis.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the Court treats the planned demonstration as an assembly without question.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as organisers of the demonstration.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court refers to 'peaceful gatherings' and the entire analysis proceeds on the assumption that the assembly was peaceful. The Court notes 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence' as a general principle applicable here.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: 'The Court notes that the Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the ban on the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of their rights under that provision. It sees no reason to hold otherwise.' The ban on organising the demonstration on the road leading to the airport is confirmed as an interference. Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed: 'The Court observes that the ban was based on section 8(1) of the Assembly Act (see paragraph 12 above) and considers that it was thus prescribed by law.' The Court accepts the prescribed-by-law requirement is satisfied. Conclusion: yes.

However, the Court does not separately discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. It simply states the ban was 'prescribed by law' without elaborating on these sub-criteria. The applicants had argued the authorities interpreted the Assembly Act in an 'overarching manner,' but the Court does not engage with this argument in detail — it simply accepts the legal basis. The sub-elements are therefore not discussed.

- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: Not discussed explicitly; the overall conclusion of 'prescribed by law' is accepted without sub-analysis.
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: Not discussed.
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: 'the Court is satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others.' Two aims are identified: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others (specifically other road users). Conclusion: yes.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the necessity test and finds it is not satisfied. Key passages:
- 'It remains to be ascertained whether the interference complained of was "necessary in a democratic society".' The Court then applies principles from Kudrevičius and Others.
- 'the Court finds that the Government have not demonstrated that the national authorities based their decisions on an acceptable assessment of the relevant facts.'
- 'The Court is therefore not convinced by the Government's explanation to the effect that the hard shoulder of the road could not have accommodated the demonstration without jeopardising access to the airport.'
- 'the Court observes that the site where the applicants had intended to hold their demonstration had a symbolic importance for the employees of the airport... it was the authorities' duty to reflect on possible alternative solutions and propose other arrangements to the organisers.'
- 'Instead of considering measures which could have allowed the assembly to proceed in a manner that did not cause undue disturbance to road traffic in the proximity of the airport, the authorities imposed a ban on it. They resorted to the most radical measure of denying the applicants the possibility of exercising their right to freedom of assembly.'
- 'Thus, a fair balance between the legitimate aim and the means for attaining it was not attained.'
Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly spells out a positive obligation in the necessity analysis: 'The Court considers that in the present case it was the authorities' duty to reflect on possible alternative solutions and propose other arrangements to the organisers (see Primov and Others, cited above, § 131). The domestic authorities nonetheless did not embark on an assessment of whether the employees of the airport had at their disposal alternative means of protecting their interest. Instead of considering measures which could have allowed the assembly to proceed in a manner that did not cause undue disturbance to road traffic in the proximity of the airport, the authorities imposed a ban on it.' This is framed as a duty (positive obligation) to seek alternative arrangements rather than simply banning the assembly. The Court finds this obligation was violated. Conclusion: yes (positive obligation exists and was violated).
