**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "In these circumstances, the Court finds a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of both cases complained of." This follows from the Court's analysis referencing the Gafgaz Mammadov judgment and the specific findings about the dispersal of demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court treats the existence of assemblies (demonstrations of 17 November 2012 and 26 January 2013) as given throughout the judgment. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate legal question. The Court refers to "the demonstration of 17 November 2012" and "the demonstration of 26 January 2013" without questioning whether these constitute assemblies within the meaning of Article 11.

*ii) Participation:* The applicant's participation is treated as established throughout. The Court refers to "his arrest and conviction following his participation in the demonstration" in both cases. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a legal sub-element.

*iii) Peaceful assembly:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies. In the first case, it notes (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov) "the lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration, which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner." In the second case, the Court refers to "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention." The peaceful character is thus explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied throughout the judgment. The Court discusses "the dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions" as the measures at issue, and notes these "could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies" and have "a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly." The existence of interference is not separately analyzed as a legal question but is clearly implied by the entire structure of the analysis.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" requirement, particularly foreseeability and safeguards against abuse.

*i) Foreseeable:* The Court explicitly notes "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov, § 55). The applicant also argued that "the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly did not comply with principles of foreseeability and precision." In the second case, the Court accepts that the dispersal was "lawful" (i.e., prescribed by law) because the applicant failed to substantiate that the assembly was spontaneous: "the Court is ready to accept that the dispersal of the demonstration of 26 January 2013 was lawful." So for the first case, foreseeability concerns are raised but the Court does not make a definitive finding on this sub-element alone (it proceeds to necessity). For the second case, the Court accepts lawfulness.

*ii) Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed. The Court does not separately address accessibility of the legal provisions.

*iii) Safeguards against abuse:* The Court explicitly notes concerns about "the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov, § 55), which relates to the adequacy of safeguards. This is explicitly discussed.

Overall on "prescribed by law": The Court raises serious concerns about foreseeability and safeguards in the first case but does not make a definitive finding that the interference was not prescribed by law — it proceeds to examine necessity. In the second case, it accepts lawfulness. The overall "prescribed by law" conclusion is thus mixed/inconclusive as a standalone finding, but the Court proceeds to necessity in both cases.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued that the dispersal was "necessary in the interests of national security, for the protection of public safety and the prevention of disorder or crime." The Court does not explicitly accept or reject these aims — it proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without making a definitive finding on legitimate aim. The aims are mentioned in the parties' submissions but the Court does not explicitly endorse them. This element is not_discussed as a standalone conclusion by the Court itself, though the Government's claimed aims are noted.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and decided. The Court states: "Turning to the question whether the dispersal of the demonstration of 26 January 2013 and the applicant's conviction were necessary in a democratic society... the Court concludes that the authorities in the present case have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration." The Court also notes: "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention." Furthermore: "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies... The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly." The Court finds necessity was NOT satisfied — the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
