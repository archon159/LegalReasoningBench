**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is found in the conclusion section:

"In view of the above, the Court finds that the refusals to authorise the public assemblies did not appear to meet the lawfulness requirement of Article 11 and were not necessary in a democratic society. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 existed, but this is implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers throughout to "peaceful public assemblies" that the applicants sought to organise, and the entire analysis proceeds on the assumption that the planned events were assemblies within the scope of Article 11. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained that the refusals by the domestic authorities to authorise their peaceful public assemblies or to authorise those assemblies at the planned locations violated their right to freedom of assembly." This implies the existence of assemblies.

**ii) Participation:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicants participated or tried to participate. It is implied that the applicants were organisers who sought to hold assemblies and were refused authorisation, which logically implies they attempted to exercise their right to assemble.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies: "Those refusals were not based on such fundamental grounds as allegations that the organisers and participants had violent intentions, wanted to incite violence, or wanted to otherwise reject the foundations of a democratic society." This is an explicit (if brief) confirmation that the assemblies were peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: "The Court considers that both the outright refusal and the refusals to authorise the assemblies at their planned locations constituted an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly (see The United Macedonian Organisation Ilinden and Ivanov v. Bulgaria, no. 44079/98, § 103, 20 October 2005, and Lashmankin and Others v. Russia, nos. 57818/09 and 14 others, § 405, 7 February 2017)."

Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court sets out the applicable principles at length and then applies them to the facts.

**i) Foreseeability:** Explicitly discussed. The Court examines whether the domestic law was sufficiently foreseeable and whether its practical application gave adequate protection. It states: "the manner in which the domestic authorities – the BCEA and the domestic courts – interpreted and applied in practice the domestic law provisions on the notification and authorisation of public assemblies did not give the applicants protection against arbitrary interference. That practical interpretation and application of the domestic law meant that the BCEA could exercise in practice an unfettered discretion to ban public assemblies." The Court also notes: "The above considerations, taken together, give strong indications that the refusals to authorise the public assemblies in all three applications failed to meet the 'prescribed by law' requirement under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention."

Conclusion: no (the foreseeability requirement was not met).

**ii) Accessibility:** The Court does not explicitly discuss accessibility of the law as a separate sub-element. The general principles section mentions that "[t]he law should be accessible to the persons concerned," but the application section focuses on foreseeability and safeguards rather than accessibility per se. Accessibility is not_discussed as a separate sub-element in the application to the facts.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The notion of 'quality of the law' requires, as a corollary of the foreseeability test, that the law be compatible with the rule of law. It thus implies that there must be adequate safeguards in domestic law against arbitrary interferences by public authorities." And in application: "the manner in which the domestic authorities – the BCEA and the domestic courts – interpreted and applied in practice the domestic law provisions on the notification and authorisation of public assemblies did not give the applicants protection against arbitrary interference. That practical interpretation and application of the domestic law meant that the BCEA could exercise in practice an unfettered discretion to ban public assemblies."

Conclusion: no (adequate safeguards were not present in practice).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed, but the Court leaves the question open: "The interference purportedly aimed at ensuring the interests of public safety and the protection of the rights of others. The Court leaves aside the question whether the motives summarised in paragraph 59 above concerned indeed public safety and the protection of the rights of others. Even assuming that there was a legitimate aim, the Court considers that the domestic authorities...failed to assess the necessity of the interference."

The Court does not definitively find a legitimate aim but assumes one arguendo. The aims mentioned are public safety and protection of the rights of others.

Conclusion: null (left open/assumed arguendo).
Aims found: public_safety, rights_of_others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds that the domestic authorities failed to adequately balance the competing interests:

"the domestic authorities (both the BCEA and the domestic courts) failed to assess the necessity of the interference in a democratic society for the achievement of such an aim, and to strike a fair balance between the competing interests at stake."

And: "Consequently, the Court considers that the domestic authorities did not adduce 'relevant and sufficient' reasons to justify the interference with the right to freedom of assembly of the applicants and failed to strike a fair balance between the competing interests at stake. The interference was therefore also disproportionate to any aim that could be legitimately pursued."

Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly discuss any positive obligation of the state to facilitate or protect the assemblies. The analysis is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). Positive obligations are not discussed.

Conclusion: null / not_discussed.
