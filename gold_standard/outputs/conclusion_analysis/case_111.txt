**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *Assembly exists*: implied (the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies")
- *Participation*: implied (the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies")
- *Peaceful*: implied (no suggestion of violence; the Court proceeds to assess the interference as one with "freedom of assembly" without excluding the peaceful character)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" confirms that interference is established, though it is not separately and formally analyzed — it is treated as given. This is explicit (the word "interferences" is used directly).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law in the leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it did not need to address prescribed by law. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and states it found "no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion." The necessity element is explicitly discussed and resolved in the negative (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary, hence a violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is not discussed.
