**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and its description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The Court would not have proceeded to assess interference and necessity without implicitly accepting that the assemblies existed, that the applicants participated, and that the assemblies were peaceful (since the protection of Article 11 does not extend to non-peaceful assemblies).

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether assemblies existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies fell outside Article 11's protection due to violence.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used directly, and the Court also describes in the opening paragraph the measures taken: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. Since the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis (finding a violation there), it does not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or assessed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law, particularly Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
