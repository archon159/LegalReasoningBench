**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and describes "the dispersal of these assemblies" — this implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without questioning whether the assembly was outside the scope of protection). The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov further implies that the assemblies were peaceful and within scope.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as "yes" — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "the dispersal of these assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied as "yes" — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as "yes" — the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the scope of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the passage: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The existence of interference is also described in the opening: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Interference is thus explicitly confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. These elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are therefore not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. Legitimate aim is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This element is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court also references its prior case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and the leading cases of Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not spell out any positive obligation for the government in this reasoning section. Positive obligations are not discussed.
