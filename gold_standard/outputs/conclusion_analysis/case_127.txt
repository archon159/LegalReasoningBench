**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Analysis of canonical Article 11 elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated (as "organisers and/or participants"), and the assembly must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These are implied by the overall framing of the case.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers and/or participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to apply Article 11 protection without any suggestion that the assemblies were non-peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court explicitly states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" confirms that interference is found. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, referencing leading cases and concluding the interference was not necessary in a democratic society. Since the Court does not address prescribed by law, these sub-elements are not_discussed, and no conclusion can be drawn.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that necessity was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The Court also references its proportionality case-law: "The Court refers to the principles established in its case-law regarding freedom of assembly... and proportionality of interference with it (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey... and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova...)."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
