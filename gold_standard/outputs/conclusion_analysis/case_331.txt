**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated explicitly in the section on the applicant's conviction under Articles 220 § 6 and 314 of the Criminal Code:

"In the light of the aforementioned considerations, the Court concludes that Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code was not 'foreseeable' in its application since it did not afford the applicant the legal protection against arbitrary interference with his right under Article 11 of the Convention (see Ahmet Yıldırım v. Turkey, no. 3111/10, § 67, ECHR 2012). Hence, the interference resulting from the application of Article 220 § 6 was not prescribed by law. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court also declined to separately examine the second set of criminal proceedings (under section 7(2) of Law no. 3713), stating: "In view of its finding of a violation of Article 11 of the Convention above, the Court does not consider it necessary to examine whether the criminal proceedings brought against the applicant under section 7(2) of Law no. 3713 constituted an interference with his right to freedom of assembly and, if so, whether they were justified."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses this in the admissibility section. The Government argued that the funeral and demonstration did not fall within the scope of Article 11, but the Court rejected this: "The Court therefore agrees with the applicant's assertion that the present case and that of Kartal v. Turkey (cited above) should be distinguished... In those circumstances, the Court rejects the Government's submission that Article 11 of the Convention is not applicable in the present case." The existence of assemblies (a funeral on 28 March 2006 and a demonstration on 5 March 2007) is explicitly confirmed as falling within the scope of Article 11. This is explicit.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court explicitly notes the applicant's participation: "He was sentenced to six years and three months in prison on the grounds that he had attended the funeral of four PKK militants, had walked in front of one of the coffins during the funeral and made a 'V' sign, and that he had applauded while other demonstrators chanted slogans in support of Abdullah Öcalan during a gathering at his university." The applicant's participation is not disputed and is treated as established. This is explicit (implied by the scope discussion and the merits analysis).

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly in the admissibility section. The Government argued that illegal slogans had been chanted and acts of violence committed, and that the applicant should be considered as having been engaged in acts of violence. The Court rejected this: "The Court observes that the indictment dated 8 May 2007 did not contain any charge against the applicant on account of alleged acts of violence... the applicant was not convicted of committing acts of violence in the judgments of 30 November 2007 and 28 April 2009." Furthermore, in the merits section, the Court refers to the applicant as "a peaceful demonstrator" and states that "the very essence of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly... was undermined when the applicant was held criminally liable under Articles 220 § 6 and 314 of the Criminal Code for the mere fact of attending a public meeting and expressing his views therein." This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference: "In the instant case, the Court considers that there has been an interference with the applicant's exercise of his right to freedom of assembly on account of his conviction for membership of an illegal organisation under Articles 220 § 6 and 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code based on his participation in a funeral and a demonstration." The Court also reiterates the general principle: "The term 'restrictions' in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an act of assembly and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly examines the "prescribed by law" requirement and concludes it is NOT satisfied.

**(i) Foreseeability**
The Court extensively discusses foreseeability and concludes it is not met: "the Court concludes that Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code was not 'foreseeable' in its application since it did not afford the applicant the legal protection against arbitrary interference with his right under Article 11 of the Convention... Hence, the interference resulting from the application of Article 220 § 6 was not prescribed by law."

Key passages include: "the text of Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code tied the status of membership of an illegal organisation to the mere fact of a person having acted 'on behalf' of that organisation, without the prosecution having to prove the material elements of actual membership"; "the array of acts that potentially constitute a basis for the application of a severe criminal sanction in the form of imprisonment, under Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code, are so vast that the wording of the provision, including its extensive interpretation by the domestic courts, do not afford a sufficient measure of protection against arbitrary interferences by the public authorities." This is explicit, conclusion: no.

**(ii) Accessibility**
The Court explicitly addresses accessibility and finds it satisfied: "The Court considers that there is no doubt that Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code was accessible." This is explicit, conclusion: yes.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse**
The Court discusses safeguards as part of the foreseeability analysis, finding them insufficient. The Court states: "a rule is 'foreseeable' when it affords a measure of protection against arbitrary interferences by the public authorities and against the extensive application of a restriction to any party's detriment" and finds that "the wording of the provision, including its extensive interpretation by the domestic courts, do not afford a sufficient measure of protection against arbitrary interferences by the public authorities." The Court also notes: "on account of his conviction, for acts which fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention, there remained no distinction between the applicant, a peaceful demonstrator, and an individual who had committed offences within the structure of the PKK. Such extensive interpretation of a legal norm cannot be justified when it has the effect of equating mere exercise of fundamental freedoms with membership of an illegal organisation in the absence of any concrete evidence of such membership." This is discussed explicitly as part of the foreseeability/prescribed by law analysis, conclusion: no (insufficient safeguards).

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court does not reach a definitive conclusion on legitimate aim. The Government submitted that the interference pursued legitimate aims of "protecting national security, territorial integrity, public safety and public order as well as the prevention of crime." However, since the Court found the interference was not prescribed by law, it did not need to (and did not) make a finding on whether a legitimate aim was actually pursued. The legitimate aim is mentioned in the parties' submissions but not resolved by the Court. The Court's analysis stops at the "prescribed by law" stage. This is not_discussed by the Court itself (only raised by the parties), conclusion: null, aims_found: [] (since the Court made no finding).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
The Court does not formally reach the necessity test, having found the interference was not prescribed by law. However, the Court does make observations that touch on proportionality within the prescribed by law analysis: "the applicant was given a prison sentence of six years and three months under Articles 220 § 6 and 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code and was imprisoned for a period of four years and eight months in total... when demonstrators, such as the applicant, face the charge of membership of an illegal armed organisation, they risk an additional sentence of between five and ten years in prison, a sanction which is strikingly severe and grossly disproportionate to their conduct." These observations are made in the context of the prescribed by law/foreseeability analysis rather than as a standalone necessity assessment. The Court explicitly noted in the Gülcü comparison that in the present case "the question of lawfulness calls for a separate examination," implying it would not proceed to necessity. Necessity is not formally discussed as a separate element, conclusion: null.

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (the State interfered with the applicant's rights through criminal conviction). This is not_discussed, conclusion: null.
