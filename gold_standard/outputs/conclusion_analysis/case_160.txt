**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final paragraph of the assessment states: "The Court concludes that the ban on the religious assembly planned by the applicant was not 'necessary in a democratic society'. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention interpreted in the light of Article 9."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court explicitly identifies the applicant's planned event as a "religious assembly" and an "assembly" throughout. In the interference section: "The applicant attempted to organise a religious assembly in the town park." This is explicit. Conclusion: yes.

*ii) Participation:* The Court notes the applicant applied for authorisation to organise the assembly, and the refusal constituted an interference with "the applicant's rights under Article 11." This is implied rather than separately analyzed as a sub-element — the Court treats the applicant as the organiser and thus a participant/organiser without separately discussing this sub-element. Conclusion: yes, implied.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly discusses the peaceful character of the assembly: "The Court further notes the indisputably peaceful character of the religious assembly planned by the applicant. There was no evidence that any of the participants would incite or resort to violence." Conclusion: yes, explicit.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed in the section "A. Whether there has been an interference": "The refusal of authorisation to organise an assembly constituted an interference with the applicant's rights under Article 11 interpreted in the light of Article 9." Conclusion: yes, explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court accepts prescribed by law in a single sentence: "The Court accepts that the interference was 'prescribed by law' and that it pursued 'a legitimate aim' within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Articles 9 and 11, that of preventing disorder." The Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. It simply accepts the condition without elaboration. The overall conclusion is yes (prescribed by law), but the sub-elements are not individually discussed.

*Foreseeable:* not_discussed (no separate analysis). Conclusion: null.
*Accessible:* not_discussed. Conclusion: null.
*Safeguards:* not_discussed. Conclusion: null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Also accepted in the same sentence: "The Court accepts that the interference was 'prescribed by law' and that it pursued 'a legitimate aim' within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Articles 9 and 11, that of preventing disorder." The aim identified is prevention of disorder. Conclusion: yes, explicit. Aims found: prevention_of_disorder. (The Government also raised protection of rights of others — the Court addresses this at the end but does not accept it as a legitimate aim justifying the ban; the Court only formally accepts "preventing disorder" as the legitimate aim.)

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Extensively and explicitly discussed. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages include:
- "It remains to be determined whether it was 'necessary in a democratic society'."
- "The Court is not convinced by that argument [that the minority religion could be banned to avoid discontent of majority]."
- "the mere fact that the Evangelical Christian religion was practised by a minority of the town residents was not capable of justifying an interference with the rights of followers of that religion."
- "Instead of considering measures which could have allowed the applicant's religious assembly to proceed peacefully, the authorities imposed a ban on it. They resorted to the most radical measure... Such a comprehensive ban cannot be considered justified."
- "The Court concludes that the ban on the religious assembly planned by the applicant was not 'necessary in a democratic society'."
Conclusion: no (not necessary), explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court mentions positive obligations in the general principles section: "although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities with the exercise of the rights protected, there may in addition be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these rights (see Wilson, National Union of Journalists and Others v. the United Kingdom...)." Furthermore, in the application section, the Court states: "It is thus the duty of Contracting States to take reasonable and appropriate measures to enable lawful demonstrations to proceed peacefully (see Plattform 'Ärzte für das Leben' v. Austria...)." However, the Court does not separately conclude that the government violated a positive obligation — the violation is framed as a negative interference (the ban). The positive obligation is mentioned as a general principle and as context for why the authorities should have taken protective measures rather than banning the assembly, but the Court's ultimate finding is framed in terms of the ban being unjustified (negative obligation), not a failure of positive obligation. Conclusion: discussed (explicit mention), but the Court does not separately find a violation of a positive obligation — the positive obligation framing supports the necessity analysis rather than standing as an independent finding. I will mark this as explicit with conclusion null (since the Court mentions it but does not make a separate finding of violation of positive obligation).
