**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated or tried to participate, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity, which would be inapplicable if the assembly were not peaceful). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" and "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the description of applicants as "organisers or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied by the Court's proceeding to assess the interference and necessity without any suggestion that the assemblies were violent or non-peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The existence of interference is also described in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The Court uses the word "interferences" explicitly, confirming this element is established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment, relying on its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, these elements remain not discussed.

- **prescribed_by_law**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or assessed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and states it found "no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
