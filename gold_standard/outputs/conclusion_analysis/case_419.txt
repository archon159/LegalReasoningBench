**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Analysis of canonical doctrine elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and its description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The existence of an assembly is implied by the phrase "public assemblies"; participation is implied by describing the applicants as "organisers or participants"; and the peaceful nature is implied by the fact that the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without any suggestion that the assemblies fell outside Article 11 protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze whether there was an interference in a dedicated section, but it is clearly implied. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in its necessity analysis, confirming that interference is treated as established (implied).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss the "prescribed by law" requirement at all — neither foreseeability, accessibility, nor safeguards against abuse are mentioned. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, suggesting it either assumed this element was satisfied or found it unnecessary to address given the necessity finding. This element is not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. No aims from Article 11(2) are mentioned. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is negative (i.e., the necessity requirement was NOT met), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and the general principles from Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania, Oya Ataman v. Turkey, and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
