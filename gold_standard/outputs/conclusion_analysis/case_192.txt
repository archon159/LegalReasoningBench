**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and describes "the dispersal of these assemblies" — this implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without questioning whether the assembly was outside the scope of protection). The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov further implies that the assemblies were peaceful and within scope.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the scope of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and earlier the text describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. Interference is thus explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor do the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) receive any explicit treatment. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, this element is not discussed. It is not even clearly implied, as the Court's reasoning does not logically require a finding on this element before reaching necessity.

- **prescribed_by_law**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court references its proportionality case-law (Oya Ataman, Hyde Park) and leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) to support this conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
