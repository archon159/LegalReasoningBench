**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final paragraph of section B.3 (The Court's assessment):

"Having regard to the facts of the present cases and their clear similarity to those of the Gafgaz Mammadov case on all relevant and crucial points, the Court sees no particular circumstances that could compel it to deviate from its findings in that judgment, and finds that in the present cases the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was breached for the same reasons as those outlined above."

And the operative conclusion: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that demonstrations took place. It explicitly refers to "the demonstration of 2 April 2011" and "the demonstrations" throughout, and the entire analysis proceeds on the basis that assemblies occurred. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a doctrinal sub-element.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes the applicant's arrest and conviction for participating in the demonstration, and refers to "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration" (quoting from Gafgaz Mammadov, §§ 63-64). Participation is implied as established.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly discusses this sub-element: "The Court notes from the outset that none of the official records or the domestic court decisions submitted by the parties suggests that the demonstration of 2 April 2011 had been violent. The Court therefore accepts the applicant's assertion that that demonstration had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner up until his arrest." It also references "a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner" (from Gafgaz Mammadov, § 61).

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. It refers to "the dispersal of the demonstrations by the police and his arrest and conviction for an administrative offence" as the subject of the complaint, and the entire analysis proceeds on the basis that these acts constituted interference. The Court notes "a lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention" (Gafgaz Mammadov, §§ 63-64), and finds the right was "breached." Interference is implied as established by the fact that the Court proceeds to examine justification.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element. It notes "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov, § 55). It also notes "doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely Article 310.1 of the CAO, relied on by the authorities to arrest and convict the participant of an unauthorised demonstration" (Gafgaz Mammadov, §§ 56 and 62).

**(i) Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed — "serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies." The applicant also argued that "the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly did not comply with the principles of foreseeability and precision." The Court's conclusion is negative (not sufficiently foreseeable).

**(ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element. The applicant's submissions focus on foreseeability and precision, and the Court's analysis does not separately address accessibility. Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Explicitly discussed — "serious concerns about... the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed" (Gafgaz Mammadov, § 55). The conclusion is negative (insufficient safeguards).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government submitted that "the dispersal of the demonstrations had pursued the aim of protecting the rights and freedoms of others and preventing disorder." The Court does not explicitly accept or reject this claim in its own assessment — it proceeds to find a violation without clearly resolving whether a legitimate aim was established. The Court's analysis focuses on the lack of sufficient reasons and the prescribed-by-law issues, without making a definitive finding on legitimate aim. The aims mentioned by the Government are "rights of others" and "prevention of disorder," but the Court does not endorse them. Given the Court's approach of referencing Gafgaz Mammadov and finding a violation "for the same reasons," legitimate aim appears not to have been conclusively established by the Court itself. The discussion is present but inconclusive — the Court does not explicitly find a legitimate aim was or was not served.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses this element by referencing "a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner" (Gafgaz Mammadov, § 61), and "a failure by the authorities to take into consideration the fact that the demonstration had been notified" (Gafgaz Mammadov, § 60), and "a lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11" (Gafgaz Mammadov, §§ 63-64). The conclusion is that the interference was not necessary — the right was "breached."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (unjustified interference by the state). Not discussed.
