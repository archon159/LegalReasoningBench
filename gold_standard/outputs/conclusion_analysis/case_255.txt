**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The key reasoning paragraph leading to this conclusion reads: "The foregoing considerations are sufficient to enable the Court to conclude that the interference which constituted the dispersal of a peaceful gathering in a private property was not 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court explicitly finds that Article 11 is applicable, noting in the Admissibility section: "the right to freedom of assembly as protected under Article 11 of the Convention may cover both private and public meetings, including an assembly of an essentially social character... and finds that this provision is applicable in the present case." This is an explicit finding that the gathering at the private café constitutes an assembly within the meaning of Article 11.

*ii) Participation:* The Court implies that the applicant participated in the assembly. The interference is described as the "dispersal by the police of the gathering of 14 June 2008" and the applicant's right to freedom of assembly is treated as having been interfered with. The Court does note uncertainty about whether he attended as a journalist or as a participant, but resolves this by examining the case under Article 11 (rather than Article 10), implying his participation in the assembly is accepted.

*iii) Peaceful assembly:* The Court explicitly characterizes the gathering as peaceful in the merits section: "the interference which constituted the dispersal of a peaceful gathering in a private property was not 'prescribed by law'." This is an explicit finding that the assembly was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court notes that it is undisputed between the parties, and the Court agrees, that there was an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly on account of the dispersal by the police of the gathering of 14 June 2008." Conclusion: yes, there was interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed. The Court examines whether the interference had a legal basis in domestic law. It notes: "the Government failed to refer to any domestic law or provisions which could constitute the legal basis for the dispersal of a gathering held in places which are in private ownership." It further notes that the Law on Freedom of Assembly (Articles 7 and 14) provides the legal basis for police intervention in assemblies, but "Article 4.1 of the same Law clearly provided that the right to hold gatherings in places which are in private ownership is not regulated by this Law." The Court concludes: "the interference which constituted the dispersal of a peaceful gathering in a private property was not 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention."

The Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards in a structured way. Rather, it focuses on the absence of any applicable legal basis at all — the law explicitly excluded private gatherings from its scope. This is essentially a finding that the interference lacked any legal basis, which subsumes all three sub-elements. Since the Court finds no legal basis exists at all, foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not separately addressed. They are implied to be unsatisfied (since there is no law at all to be foreseeable, accessible, or to contain safeguards), but the Court does not explicitly discuss them as separate sub-elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. The Court explicitly states: "Having reached that conclusion, the Court does not need to satisfy itself that the other requirements of Article 11 § 2 (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) have been complied with." No legitimate aim is identified or assessed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Not discussed. As stated above, the Court explicitly declines to examine this element: "the Court does not need to satisfy itself that the other requirements of Article 11 § 2 (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) have been complied with."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The third-party intervener (Article 19) raised positive obligations, submitting that "the Government had failed to comply with their positive obligations to protect the exercise of the right to freedom of expression." However, the Court itself does not explicitly address positive obligations in its own assessment. The Court's analysis focuses entirely on the negative obligation (the unjustified interference). The positive obligation argument is mentioned in the parties' submissions section but is not taken up by the Court in its own assessment. Therefore, positive obligations are not discussed by the Court itself.
