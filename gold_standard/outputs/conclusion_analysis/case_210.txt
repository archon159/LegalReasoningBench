**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention, interpreted in the light of Article 9."

This conclusion is reached after the Court finds that the interference was not prescribed by law and not necessary in a democratic society.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. It states: "The Court reiterates that the right to freedom of assembly covers both private meetings and meetings in public places, and can be exercised by individual participants and by the persons organising the event." The planned public religious events (meetings for the promotion of Vaishnavism) are treated as assemblies falling within the scope of Article 11. This is explicit.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court implies that Mr Frolov, as the organiser who filed the notifications, was attempting to participate in (and organise) the assembly. The Court notes: "the Moscow authorities rejected Mr Frolov's notifications of a public religious event." This is implied rather than separately discussed as a distinct sub-element.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful character of the planned events: "The Court notes the indisputably peaceful character of the planned religious events. Participants intended to assemble in support of their religion, Vaishnavism, and a particular lifestyle associated with it which in their view offered health benefits. There was no reason to presume a risk of any disturbance of public order or breach of peace on their part."

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: "The Court has previously found that the refusal of authorisation to hold a religious service in the public space constituted interference with the applicant's rights under Article 11 of the Convention, interpreted in the light of Article 9 (see Barankevich, cited above, § 20). That finding applies in the circumstances of the present case in which the Moscow authorities rejected Mr Frolov's notifications of a public religious event. The interference will constitute a violation of that provision unless it can be shown that it has been 'prescribed by law', has pursued at least one legitimate aim under paragraph 2, and has been 'necessary in a democratic society' for the achievement of that aim."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element and finds the interference was NOT prescribed by law on two grounds:

**(i) Foreseeable:** The Court explicitly finds the interference unforeseeable on two separate grounds. First: "The Russian courts gave no reasons whatsoever for their finding that the promotion of Vaishnavism and a healthy lifestyle fell outside the scope of that broad definition. In these circumstances, the Court finds that the rejection of Mr Frolov's notifications on that ground was unforeseeable and not 'prescribed by law'." Second: "confronted with the domestic courts' failure to specify the nature of the alleged incompatibility of the planned event with the concept of a 'meeting', the Court finds that this ground for rejecting the event notifications was unforeseeable and the interference did not meet the 'prescribed by law' requirement."

**(ii) Accessible:** The Court does not explicitly discuss accessibility of the legal provisions. The statutory basis is described (Religions Act and Public Events Act), but accessibility as a distinct sub-element is not addressed. Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards:** The Court implicitly touches on the absence of safeguards when it notes that "The Act does not specify how the purpose of the event should be assessed or provide for the authorities' discretion in determining which purposes are permissible and which are not," suggesting a lack of adequate safeguards against arbitrary application. However, this is framed in the context of foreseeability rather than as a standalone safeguards analysis. It is implied at best.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court discusses legitimate aim but does not make a clean finding that the interference served a legitimate aim. The Government argued the interference pursued "a legitimate aim" for "the protection of public order and the rights of others." The Court addresses the "incompatibility with religious beliefs of others" argument under necessity rather than legitimate aim, stating: "the incompatibility with religious beliefs of others as such, as alleged by the Government in the present case, does not pass the test of being 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court also notes that "the right to freedom of assembly may be restricted for the prevention of disorder and the protection of the rights of others which are both legitimate aims under the Convention." However, the Court does not make an explicit finding that a legitimate aim was established — it proceeds to find the interference not prescribed by law and not necessary, without definitively concluding on legitimate aim. The aims referenced (prevention of disorder, rights of others) are mentioned but not conclusively found to be served. Given the structure of the judgment, the legitimate aim question is discussed but left somewhat open (the Court moves to necessity without a clean "yes" on legitimate aim). I will treat it as discussed but with no conclusive affirmative finding, noting the aims referenced were prevention of disorder and rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses necessity and finds the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages: "the incompatibility with religious beliefs of others as such, as alleged by the Government in the present case, does not pass the test of being 'necessary in a democratic society'." And: "a restriction on the legitimate exercise of the right to convince other people of the benefits, spiritual or health-related, associated with Vaishnavism was not necessary in a democratic society." The Court also applies heightened scrutiny to content-based restrictions: "their objections related to the religious nature of planned events and accordingly amounted to content-based restrictions on freedom of assembly which should be subjected to the most serious scrutiny by the Court."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference with the right to freedom of assembly. Not discussed.
