**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the scope sub-elements (whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without questioning scope). These conclusions are implied rather than explicit.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: The opening paragraph states the applicants were "organisers or participants of public assemblies," implying an assembly existed. This is implied, not explicitly analyzed.
- **scope_participation**: The same passage — "organisers or participants of public assemblies" — implies participation. Implied, not explicit.
- **scope_peaceful**: Not explicitly discussed, but implied by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis without questioning whether the assemblies were peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'," explicitly using the word "interferences." This confirms that interference is explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor do its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) receive any explicit treatment. The Court jumps directly to necessity. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without specifying which legitimate aim the interference was said to serve. Not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is negative (the interference was NOT necessary), which is what leads to the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. Not discussed.
