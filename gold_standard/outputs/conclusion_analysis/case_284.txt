**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied. The Court states that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred, which logically presupposes that (i) there were assemblies, (ii) the applicants participated (as "organisers or participants of public assemblies"), and (iii) the assemblies were peaceful (since the protection of Article 11 only extends to peaceful assemblies, and the Court proceeds to find a violation without questioning this). The opening paragraph also describes the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," implying the existence of assemblies and participation. No explicit discussion of peacefulness is present, but it is implied by the Court's willingness to apply Article 11 protection.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied (yes) — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether assemblies existed.
- scope_participation: implied (yes) — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- scope_peaceful: implied (yes) — the Court applies Article 11 without questioning peacefulness, implying the assemblies were peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The Court thus explicitly acknowledges that there were interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These sub-elements are not_discussed. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its leading case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) to find a violation on necessity grounds. Since the Court finds a violation at the necessity stage, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These elements are not discussed and no conclusion can be drawn.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. The legitimate aim element is not_discussed, and no aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This element is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the central finding of the judgment, and the Court concludes that necessity was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" (the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not_discussed.
