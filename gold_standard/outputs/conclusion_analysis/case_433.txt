**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the Court's assessment: 'In those circumstances the Court finds a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The reasoning leading to this conclusion is found throughout section B.3, particularly: 'the Court concludes that the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations' and 'The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention.' Additionally: 'The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicants' arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies... undoubtedly had a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that assemblies took place — it discusses the demonstrations of 12 January, 26 January, 10 March and 29 December 2013 as actual assemblies, referring to them as 'demonstrations' and 'assemblies' throughout. For example: 'The Court observes that the organisers did not submit a prior notice about the demonstration of 29 December 2013 to the BCEA.' The existence of assemblies is not explicitly debated but is clearly implied by the entire analysis.

**(ii) Participation:** The applicants' participation is implied throughout. The Court refers to 'the applicants' arrests and convictions' and notes that 'the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11.' This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate sub-element.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies. The applicants submitted 'that the demonstrations had been intended to be peaceful and had been held in a peaceful manner.' The Court accepts this implicitly by proceeding to analyze the interference and necessity, and explicitly states: 'the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention.' This is explicit as to the peaceful character.

**(b) Interference**

The Court treats the interference as established and implicit throughout. The dispersal of demonstrations, arrests, and convictions for administrative offences are described as the subject of the complaints: 'the dispersal of the demonstrations by the police and their arrest and conviction for an administrative offence had been in breach of their freedom of assembly.' The interference is not separately analyzed as a distinct step but is clearly implied as given.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the lawfulness of the interference, though it does not resolve it definitively for all demonstrations. For the demonstration of 29 December 2013: 'In those circumstances the Court has doubts whether the dispersal of the assembly of 29 December 2013 was prescribed by law. However, given that a more conspicuous problem arises with respect to the necessity of the interference, the Court considers that it is not appropriate to limit its examination under Article 11 of the Convention only to the lawfulness of the interference.' For the demonstrations of 12 January, 26 January and 10 March 2013: 'the Court is ready to accept that the dispersal of the demonstrations on 12 January, 26 January and 10 March 2013 was lawful.' The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually discussed.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element. The Court's analysis of lawfulness focuses on whether the spontaneous assembly exception applied, not on the foreseeability of the legal norm itself. Not discussed as a standalone sub-element.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued that 'the dispersal of the demonstrations had been necessary in the interests of national security, for the protection of public safety and the prevention of disorder or crime.' The Court does not explicitly accept or reject these aims — it proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without making a formal finding on legitimate aim. The aims are mentioned in the parties' submissions but the Court does not separately conclude on this element, moving instead to necessity. This is therefore implied (the Court proceeds as if a legitimate aim may exist) but not explicitly resolved.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central and explicitly discussed element. The Court states: 'Turning to the question of whether the dispersal of the demonstrations of 12 January, 26 January, 10 March and 29 December 2013, and the applicants' convictions were necessary in a democratic society, the Court notes that the issues raised by the present complaints are essentially the same as those examined in Gafgaz Mammadov.' It concludes: 'the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations' and 'The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court also finds a chilling effect: 'The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of the sanctions that could potentially be applied against the participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly had a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly.' The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), leading to the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
