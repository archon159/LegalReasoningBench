**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraphs of section B.2.(d):

'In the light of the foregoing, the Court finds that the applicant's criminal convictions for membership of the PKK, dissemination of propaganda in support of the PKK and resistance to the police as well as the imposition upon him of prison sentences and his detention between 21 July 2008 and 25 July 2010, were not "necessary in a democratic society". There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In section B.2.(a), the Court notes: 'the demonstration of 14 July 2008 was organised by the DTP to protest about the conditions of detention of Abdullah Öcalan.' The existence of an assembly (a demonstration) is explicitly confirmed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation: 'the applicant claimed that when he first joined the demonstrators, he started walking and chanting slogans with them.' The Court also notes that 'the applicant was arrested, detained on remand and subsequently convicted on the ground of having attended a demonstration and thrown stones at the security forces during that demonstration.' Participation is explicitly confirmed.

**iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly: 'nothing in the case file suggests that this demonstration was not intended to be peaceful or that the organisers had violent intentions. The Court further observes that the applicant claimed that when he first joined the demonstrators, he started walking and chanting slogans with them. Thus, he had the intention of showing support for Mr Öcalan, but not of behaving violently when he started demonstrating... there is nothing in the domestic courts' decisions showing that the applicant had violent intentions when he joined the demonstration. What is more, the charges against the applicant did not concern infliction of any bodily harm on anyone. The Court therefore accepts that during the events of 14 July 2008 the applicant enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court thus finds the assembly was peaceful (at least at the outset), and the applicant falls within the scope of Article 11 protection. This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference in section B.2.(a). It notes that it is 'not disputed between the parties that there was an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly.' The Court then concludes: 'the applicant's criminal convictions for membership of the PKK, dissemination of propaganda in support of the PKK and resistance to the police contained in the aforementioned judgments, as well as the imposition upon him of prison sentences and his detention between 21 July 2008 and 25 July 2010, constituted interference with his right to freedom of assembly as guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention.' Conclusion: yes, interference found. Discussed: explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element in section B.2.(b). It sets out the general standard: 'The law should be accessible to the persons concerned and formulated with sufficient precision to enable them – if need be, with appropriate advice – to foresee, to a degree that is reasonable in the circumstances, the consequences which a given action may entail.' The Court then examines the specific provisions.

**i) Foreseeable:** The Court discusses foreseeability explicitly. It examines whether 'the domestic courts' interpretation of the offence of membership of an illegal organisation could reasonably be foreseen by the applicant at the material time.' The Court describes the interpretation used by the domestic courts and notes it is consistent with the Plenary Court of Cassation decision of 4 March 2008. However, the Court ultimately declines to reach a final conclusion on foreseeability: 'the Court considers that it is not required to reach a final conclusion on the lawfulness issue in so far as it relates to the applicant's conviction under Article 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code.' So foreseeability is discussed but left unresolved (null conclusion).

**ii) Accessible:** The Court explicitly addresses accessibility: 'there is no doubt that the aforementioned provisions of Laws nos. 5237, 3713 and 2911 were accessible. As to the decision of 4 March 2008, the Court observes that while this decision was not published in the Official Gazette, it was available on the Internet. Thus, the Court does not find it necessary to pursue further the issue of the accessibility of domestic law.' Accessibility is discussed explicitly but the Court does not make a definitive finding (it finds no need to pursue it further), suggesting it is not a problem — implied yes, but not formally concluded.

**iii) Safeguards:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the legal provisions contain sufficient safeguards against abuse as a separate sub-element. However, in the necessity section, the Court does discuss the lack of procedural safeguards in the domestic courts' reasoning (obligation to provide reasons). This is not the same as the 'prescribed by law' safeguards sub-element. The safeguards sub-element under 'prescribed by law' is not discussed.

Overall on 'prescribed by law': The Court explicitly discusses this element but declines to reach a final conclusion, stating it will examine the matter under necessity instead. Conclusion: null. Discussed: explicit.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim in section B.2.(c): 'The Court is of the opinion that, in the present case, the national authorities may be considered to have pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.' Conclusion: yes. Aims found: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, rights of others. Discussed: explicit.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity in section B.2.(d). Key findings include:
- The domestic courts failed to provide reasons for convicting the applicant of PKK membership and propaganda dissemination.
- The applicant was a minor and his age was not sufficiently taken into account.
- The penalties were extremely severe and disproportionate.
- Even for the stone-throwing (acts of violence), the penalties imposed by the assize court were disproportionate given the applicant's age.

The Court concludes: 'the applicant's criminal convictions for membership of the PKK, dissemination of propaganda in support of the PKK and resistance to the police as well as the imposition upon him of prison sentences and his detention between 21 July 2008 and 25 July 2010, were not "necessary in a democratic society".' Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary). Discussed: explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Government mentioned positive obligations in their submissions ('the national authorities had a positive obligation to take reasonable and appropriate measures to protect people and public order during public demonstrations'), but the Court itself does not spell out or analyze a positive obligation on the part of the State in its own assessment. The Court's analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (whether the interference was justified). Positive obligation: not discussed by the Court in its own assessment. Conclusion: null. Discussed: not_discussed.
