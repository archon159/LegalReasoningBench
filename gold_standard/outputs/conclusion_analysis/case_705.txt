**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's explicit finding of an "interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" logically implies that: (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" — quoted from the opening paragraph), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since Article 11 only protects peaceful assemblies, and the Court proceeds to assess the interference without questioning this). These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

The opening paragraph does state: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This confirms participation and the existence of assemblies, though not as a formal doctrinal analysis.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming that an interference was found. Additionally, the opening paragraph describes the measures (arrest, dispersal, conviction for administrative offences) that constitute the interference. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed. Given that the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis and finds a violation there, it appears the Court did not need to address prescribed by law. These are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing whether the interference served a legitimate aim. This element is not discussed, and no specific aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation. This is explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is not discussed.
