**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of section B.2(b): "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The reasoning leading to this conclusion is found in section B.2(b): "Consequently, the interference did not meet the requirement of lawfulness. That being so, the Court is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether a qualifying assembly existed, but it is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court repeatedly refers to "the demonstration of 2 April 2011" and "opposition protests" as the context for the interference. The entire analysis presupposes that a peaceful assembly was planned and that the applicants were prevented from attending it. This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a threshold question.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court implies that the applicants participated (or intended to participate) in assemblies. In section B.2(a), the Court states: "the administrative proceedings against the applicants and their ensuing detention were measures aimed at preventing them from participating in the demonstration of 2 April 2011 and punishing them for having participated in opposition protests in general." This is implied as a given rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate threshold question.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court does not explicitly analyze whether the assembly was peaceful as a threshold question, but it is implied throughout. The Court refers to "the right to freedom of peaceful assembly" and describes the planned demonstration as a "peaceful demonstration" (noting that "the organisers had notified the BCEA of their intention to hold a peaceful demonstration"). This is implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses interference in section B.2(a), titled "Whether there was interference." The Court concludes: "The Court considers that those measures amounted to an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of peaceful assembly."

The Court first sets out the legal standard: "interference with the exercise of freedom of peaceful assembly does not need to amount to an outright ban, legal or de facto, but can consist in various other measures taken by the authorities. The term 'restrictions' in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an assembly and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards."

The Court then analyzes the factual basis at length, noting the political context in Azerbaijan in 2011, the applicants' opposition affiliations, the timing of arrests (two days before the demonstration), the dubious grounds for arrest, and the standardized and vague charges. It concludes: "The material before the Court allows it to draw strong, clear and concordant inferences to the effect that the administrative proceedings against the applicants and their ensuing detention were measures aimed at preventing them from participating in the demonstration of 2 April 2011 and punishing them for having participated in opposition protests in general."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" requirement in section B.2(b), titled "Whether the interference was justified." The Court states: "The legal basis for those measures was Articles 296 and 310.1 of the CAO, which prescribes an administrative penalty for 'minor hooliganism' and for 'failure to comply with a lawful order of a police officer' respectively. Thus, the measures in question were imposed relying on legal provisions which had no connection with the intended purpose of those measures. The Court cannot but agree with the applicants that the interference with their freedom of peaceful assembly on such a legal basis could only be characterised as arbitrary and unlawful."

The Court concludes: "Consequently, the interference did not meet the requirement of lawfulness."

The Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. Instead, it finds the measures unlawful on the basis that the legal provisions used had no connection with the actual purpose of the measures (i.e., the provisions were used as a pretext). This is a finding of arbitrariness rather than a classic foreseeability/accessibility/safeguards analysis. The overall "prescribed by law" conclusion is explicitly discussed and found to be "no" (not met). The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states that it does not need to examine legitimate aim: "That being so, the Court is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society." Accordingly, legitimate aim is not discussed and no aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Similarly, the Court explicitly states it is not required to determine whether the interference was necessary in a democratic society, as quoted above. This element is not discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of a negative obligation (the state must refrain from interfering). There is a brief reference to the general principle that "States must not only safeguard the right to assemble peacefully but must also refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon that right," but this is cited as part of the interference analysis rather than as a standalone positive obligation analysis. No positive obligation conclusion is reached.
