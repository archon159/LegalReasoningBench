**Question 1: Has Article 11 been violated?**

No. The Court explicitly concludes that there is no violation of Article 11. The relevant passage states: "The Court finds that largely identical considerations arise under this provision as were examined above under Article 10 of the Convention. For the same reasons, it also finds no failure to protect the applicants' freedom of assembly and, accordingly, no violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**Question 2: Analysis of canonical elements**

The Article 11 reasoning section is extremely brief. The Court does not independently work through the canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine. Instead, it cross-references its Article 10 analysis and applies the same conclusions. Without the Article 10 reasoning being provided here, we can only analyze what is present in this excerpt.

**(a) Scope (assembly exists, participation, peaceful):**
None of these sub-elements are explicitly discussed in the Article 11 section. The Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" implies that there was an assembly and that the applicants participated in it (otherwise there would be nothing to protect), but this is only implied, not explicitly addressed. The peaceful nature of the assembly is not discussed at all in this section.

**(b) Interference:**
The Court refers to "no failure to protect the applicants' freedom of assembly," which frames the case as a positive obligation matter rather than a direct interference matter. Interference is not explicitly discussed as a standalone element.

**(c) Prescribed by law (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards):**
None of these sub-elements are discussed in the Article 11 section. The cross-reference to Article 10 suggests they may have been addressed there, but they are not discussed here.

**(d) Legitimate aim:**
Not discussed in the Article 11 section.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:**
Not discussed in the Article 11 section.

**(f) Positive obligation:**
This is the one element that is explicitly (if briefly) addressed. The Court states it "finds no failure to protect the applicants' freedom of assembly," which is the language of positive obligations — the duty to protect rather than merely refrain from interfering. The conclusion is that the positive obligation was NOT violated (i.e., the government did not fail in its duty to protect). This is explicitly discussed, with the conclusion being "no" (no failure/no violation).

**Summary:**
The Court finds no violation of Article 11. The only element explicitly discussed in this section is the positive obligation, where the Court concludes there was no failure. All other elements are either implied (scope, in that the Court references "the applicants' freedom of assembly") or not discussed at all in this section, with the Court instead cross-referencing its Article 10 analysis.
