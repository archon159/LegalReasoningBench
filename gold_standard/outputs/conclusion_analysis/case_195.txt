**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated (as "organisers or participants of public assemblies"), and the assembly must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). The introductory paragraph does describe the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies the existence of assemblies and participation. Peacefulness is implied by the fact that Article 11 protection is extended.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph and to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" being interfered with.
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court proceeds to apply Article 11 protection without any suggestion that the assemblies were violent.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Interference is thus explicitly confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, referencing leading cases. None of the three sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are discussed. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without reaching prescribed by law, these elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is named. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately justify the interference), leading to the violation. The Court also references its proportionality case-law (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is not discussed.
