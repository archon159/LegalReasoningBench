**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has NOT been violated. The final paragraph states: "Consequently, there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The penultimate paragraph provides the reasoning: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that an assembly existed by referring to "the applicant and other persons clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating" and by proceeding to analyze the case under Article 11. The Government of the respondent State argued the demonstration was "outside the scope of Article 11" due to its violent character, but the Court does not explicitly rule on whether an assembly existed as a threshold matter — it proceeds directly to interference. The assembly's existence is implied by the Court's analysis.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court states: "The applicant and other persons clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Kassianos school in Nicosia. The demonstration was dispersed and some of the demonstrators, including the applicant, were arrested." This implies the applicant participated in the assembly, though it is not discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(iii) Peaceful character:** The Government argued the demonstration was "clearly outside the scope of Article 11" due to its violent character. The Court does not explicitly rule on whether the assembly was peaceful for scope purposes. Instead, it proceeds to find an interference and then addresses the violence in the context of legitimate aim and necessity. The Court notes: "the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order" and "the intervention of the Turkish and/or Turkish-Cypriot forces was not due to the political nature of the demonstration but was provoked by its violent character." The Court appears to treat the violent character not as excluding the assembly from scope but as justifying the interference — this is an implied finding that the assembly (or at least the applicant's participation) falls within scope, with the violence addressed at the justification stage rather than the scope stage.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: "Under these circumstances, the Court considers that there has been an interference with the applicant's right of assembly (see Protopapa, cited above, § 104)." Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed: "This interference had a legal basis, namely sections 70 and 71 of the Cypriot Criminal Code (see paragraphs 35-36 above) and section 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law (see paragraph 39 above), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention. In this respect, the Court recalls its finding that when, as in the Foka case, an act of the 'TRNC' authorities was in compliance with laws in force within the territory of northern Cyprus, it should in principle be regarded as having a legal basis in domestic law for the purposes of the Convention."

The Court concludes the interference was prescribed by law. However, the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are NOT individually discussed. The Court simply identifies the legal basis and declares the requirement satisfied without breaking it down into sub-elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: "The Government submitted that the interference pursued legitimate aims, including the protection of national security and/or public safety and the prevention of disorder and crime." The Court then proceeds to analyze the violent nature of the demonstration and the violation of borders, effectively accepting these aims. The Court states: "interferences with the right guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention are in principle justified for the prevention of disorder or crime and for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others where, as in the instant case, demonstrators engage in acts of violence." The aims found include: national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, and prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "There remain the questions whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society." It then analyzes the violent character of the demonstration, the breach of UN buffer zone, the violation of 'TRNC' borders, and the wide margin of appreciation: "In these conditions and having regard to the wide margin of appreciation left to the States in this sphere (see Plattform 'Ärzte für das Leben', cited above, § 34), the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2." Conclusion: the interference was necessary (i.e., not disproportionate), so the necessity requirement is satisfied from the government's perspective — no violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court briefly mentions the positive obligation dimension: "It is clear from this case-law that the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey, no. 74552/01, § 35, 5 December 2006). However, they cannot guarantee this absolutely and they have a wide discretion in the choice of the means to be used." However, this is mentioned in passing as part of the general principles, not as a separate claim being adjudicated. The Court does not find a violation of any positive obligation. The positive obligation is mentioned but not the subject of a distinct conclusion.
