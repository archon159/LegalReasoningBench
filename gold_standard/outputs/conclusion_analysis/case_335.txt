**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of Section B ('Whether the interference was justified'):

'In the light of the foregoing, the Court considers that the intervention of the police officers in the demonstration and the violence they used towards the applicant was disproportionate and not necessary for preventing disorder or crime, within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention. It also considers that the brutality of the dispersal had an inevitable dissuasive effect on people's willingness to demonstrate.'

Followed by: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is clearly implied throughout the reasoning. The text repeatedly refers to 'the demonstration,' 'the demonstrators,' and 'peaceful gatherings.' The entire analysis presupposes that an assembly took place. For example: 'the intervention of the police officers in the demonstration' and 'a demonstration in a public place may cause some disruption to ordinary life.' This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicant participated in the assembly, but this is implied throughout. The Court refers to 'the demonstration in which the applicant had taken part' (in the Government's argument section) and the entire analysis proceeds on the basis that the applicant was a participant. This is implied.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court does not open with an explicit finding that the assembly was peaceful, but it strongly implies this throughout the necessity analysis. The Court states: 'the applicant and many other demonstrators did not act violently, contrary to the allegations of the Government' and 'the demonstrators neither presented a danger to public order nor engaged in acts of violence requiring such a heavy-handed intervention' and 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings.' These statements imply a finding that the assembly was peaceful. This is implied rather than explicitly framed as a scope question.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed in Section A ('Whether there was an interference with the applicant's exercise of her freedom of peaceful assembly'). The Court states: 'The Court considers that the intervention of the police officers, as well as the ill-treatment to which the applicant was subjected, constituted an interference with her rights under Article 11 of the Convention (see, mutatis mutandis, Nurettin Aldemir and Others v. Turkey...).' Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed in Section B. The Court states: 'it is noted that the interference in the present case had a legal basis, namely the Law on Meetings and Demonstration Marches (see paragraphs 29-31 above), and was thus "prescribed by law" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.' The overall conclusion is yes, prescribed by law.

However, the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse are NOT individually discussed. The Court simply accepts the legal basis without examining these sub-requirements. The Government's argument that the interference was 'prescribed by law' is accepted without further analysis of the quality of the law. Therefore:
- Foreseeable: not discussed
- Accessible: not discussed
- Safeguards: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed in Section B. The Court states: 'As regards a legitimate aim, the Government submitted that the interference pursued, among others, the legitimate aim of prevention of disorder and crime, and the Court finds no reason to differ.' The aims found are prevention of disorder and prevention of crime. The Government also mentioned 'maintaining public order' which maps to public safety/prevention of disorder. Conclusion: yes, legitimate aim established.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed in Section B under the heading 'Whether the interference was justified.' The Court examines whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society' and concludes it was not. Key passages:
- 'having regard to the absence of any criminal proceedings against the applicant, as well as the lack of any information to show that proceedings were initiated against any other demonstrator for an alleged breach of that Law, the Court observes that the applicant and many other demonstrators did not act violently'
- 'the demonstrators neither presented a danger to public order nor engaged in acts of violence requiring such a heavy-handed intervention'
- 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings'
- 'the intervention of the police officers in the demonstration and the violence they used towards the applicant was disproportionate and not necessary for preventing disorder or crime'
Conclusion: no, the interference was not necessary in a democratic society (i.e., the necessity test was not satisfied by the government).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The applicant's submissions reference a positive obligation: 'the State was under an obligation to protect this right, rather than prevent its citizens from exercising it.' The Court does not explicitly adopt a positive obligation framing in its own analysis. The Court's analysis is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). There is a passing reference to tolerance toward peaceful gatherings, but this is framed as part of the necessity analysis rather than as a standalone positive obligation. Positive obligation is not discussed by the Court itself.
