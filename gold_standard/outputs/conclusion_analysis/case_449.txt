**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final conclusion is stated in the last paragraph of section (ii) 'Necessary in a democratic society': 'Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' This is preceded by the summary: 'In sum, even assuming that the dispersal of the assembly and the applicant's prosecution, detention and conviction complied with domestic law and pursued one of the legitimate aims enumerated in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention – presumably, prevention of disorder and crime – the measures in question were not necessary in a democratic society.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court describes the assembly at Freedom Square: 'the present case concerns primarily the events culminating in the police operation of the early morning of 1 March 2008... the camp was part of a much bigger assembly which had been going on at Freedom Square since 20 February 2008, attracting thousands of people.' The Court also notes: 'large crowds, including opposition leaders and supporters, gathered from 20 February 2008 at Freedom Square on a daily basis to take part in a political debate on a matter of serious public concern.' Conclusion: yes.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court notes the applicant's participation: 'he had been an active participant in the rallies and had even on one occasion given a speech' (from applicant's submissions, accepted by the Court). The Court also notes: 'it therefore appears that the applicant was prosecuted and detained for simply having actively participated in, and possibly organised, the assembly at Freedom Square.' The Court further states: 'the applicant was arrested – apparently shortly after the dispersal of the assembly.' Conclusion: yes.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court states: 'In sum, there is not sufficient and convincing evidence to conclude that the organisers and the participants of the assembly at Freedom Square, including the applicant, had violent intentions and that the assembly in question was not peaceful.' And later: 'The Court has already held that the assembly at Freedom Square was peaceful and nothing suggests that it was not intended to be so.' The Court examined the Government's allegations about weapons and violent intentions and rejected them for lack of convincing evidence. Conclusion: yes.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'the police operation in question effectively resulted in a dispersal of the assembly at Freedom Square and therefore interfered with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly. Furthermore, the punitive measures taken afterwards included the applicant's prosecution and detention for a number of acts allegedly committed during the assembly at Freedom Square.' And: 'The Court concludes that there has been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly on account of both the dispersal of the demonstration and the applicant's prosecution, detention and conviction.' Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this issue: 'The Court, however, does not consider it necessary to decide these issues having regard to its conclusions set out below, regarding the necessity of the interference.' The applicant had argued the proceedings were not prescribed by law because they were fabricated; the Government did not make particular submissions on lawfulness. The Court skips to necessity. Conclusion: null (not decided). The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are therefore also not discussed.

**i) Foreseeable:** not discussed. Conclusion: null.
**ii) Accessible:** not discussed. Conclusion: null.
**iii) Safeguards:** not discussed. Conclusion: null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly mentioned but not decided. The Court states: 'even assuming that the dispersal of the assembly and the applicant's prosecution, detention and conviction complied with domestic law and pursued one of the legitimate aims enumerated in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention – presumably, prevention of disorder and crime – the measures in question were not necessary in a democratic society.' The Government had submitted the aim was 'preventing disorder and crime.' The Court assumes this without deciding. Conclusion: null (assumed but not decided). Aims referenced: prevention of disorder and prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the full necessity test. Key passages include:
- 'the right to freedom of assembly, one of the foundations of a democratic society, is subject to a number of exceptions which must be narrowly interpreted and the necessity for any restrictions must be convincingly established.'
- On the dispersal: 'The Court therefore concludes that the dispersal of the assembly at Freedom Square without sufficient justification and apparently without warnings to disperse and with unjustified and excessive use of force, was a disproportionate measure which went beyond what it was reasonable to expect from the authorities when curtailing freedom of assembly.'
- On punitive measures: 'The Court considers that the dispersal of the peaceful assembly and subsequent rounding-up and detention of its activists or other peaceful participants without any evidence that they had personally committed any reprehensible acts, as happened in the applicant's case, cannot be regarded as a measure "necessary in a democratic society".'
- On the courts' reasoning: 'it cannot be said that the reasons adduced by the domestic courts to justify the interference were genuinely "relevant and sufficient", which stripped the applicant of the procedural protection that he enjoyed by virtue of his rights under Article 11.'
- Final conclusion: 'the measures in question were not necessary in a democratic society.' Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not explicitly discussed as a positive obligation framework. The Court does not frame any part of its analysis in terms of a positive obligation on the state to protect or facilitate the assembly. The analysis is entirely framed as a negative obligation (unjustified interference). Conclusion: not discussed.
