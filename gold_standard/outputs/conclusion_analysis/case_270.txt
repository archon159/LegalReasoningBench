**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the conclusion section (d): 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court also summarizes its reasoning in the same section: 'the authorities failed to act with due tolerance and good faith as regards the applicant's right to freedom of assembly, did not adduce sufficient and relevant reasons justifying the interference, and imposed a sanction which was disproportionate in the circumstances.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether a 'demonstration' qualifies as an 'assembly' under Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the judgment — the Court proceeds directly to analyze interference with 'freedom of peaceful assembly,' treating the demonstration of 19 June 2011 as an assembly without question. The Court refers to 'the demonstration in issue' and the applicant's 'right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention' without any doubt about whether an assembly existed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation. In section (a): 'the applicant who participated in the demonstration was arrested and convicted.' Further: 'the impugned "behaviour" of the applicant actually consisted of his participation in the demonstration.' And: 'the facts of the case disclose interference directly related to the applicant's exercise of his right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention.' This is explicitly discussed.

**iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. In section (c): 'the authorities dispersed the demonstration shortly after it began, despite the fact that it had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner up to that point.' Also: 'the applicant in fact was arrested and convicted for his participation in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration.' The Court also notes: 'where irregular demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, the Court has required that the public authorities show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings.' The assembly is explicitly found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference in section (a) 'Whether there was interference.' It concludes: 'The Court concludes that there has been interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly on account of both the dispersal of the demonstration and the applicant's arrest and conviction.' The Court also notes that the Government's argument that the applicant was punished for disobeying a police order rather than for participating in the demonstration was rejected: 'the impugned "behaviour" of the applicant actually consisted of his participation in the demonstration.' Interference is explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses lawfulness in section (b) 'Whether the interference was lawful and pursued a legitimate aim.' However, it does not reach a definitive conclusion on this element, instead noting 'serious concerns' and 'doubts' before moving on to necessity. The Court states: 'given that a more conspicuous problem arises with respect to the necessity of the interference, the Court considers that it is not appropriate to limit its examination under Article 11 of the Convention to the lawfulness of the interference only.'

**i) Foreseeable:** The Court explicitly raises foreseeability concerns: 'the Court has serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed.' The Court notes the discrepancy between the Constitution (requiring only notification) and the Law on Freedom of Assembly (which gave broad powers to prohibit or stop assemblies), and references international reports noting that the notification system had been replaced in practice by an authorisation system. However, no definitive conclusion is reached — the Court leaves the question open.

**ii) Accessible:** The Court does not explicitly discuss accessibility of the law. This sub-element is not discussed.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse:** The Court implicitly raises this concern when it notes 'the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed' and the broad powers given to the local executive authority. However, it does not explicitly frame this as a 'safeguards' analysis. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed, and no definitive conclusion is reached.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not reach a definitive conclusion on legitimate aim. In section (b), it states it will examine necessity 'which in the specific circumstances will also take into consideration the issue of whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim.' In section (c), the Court references the Government's assertion about 'the necessity of the dispersal' in the context of 'public safety and preventing disorder or crime' (from the parties' submissions), but the Court does not explicitly endorse or reject these as legitimate aims. The Court's necessity analysis proceeds without formally confirming a legitimate aim. The Government had argued the aims of 'protecting public safety and preventing disorder or crime.' The Court's analysis is inconclusive on this point — it neither confirms nor denies a legitimate aim, folding the question into the necessity analysis. Given the Court's approach of examining necessity without formally confirming a legitimate aim, the aims_found list reflects what the Government asserted (public_safety, prevention_of_disorder, prevention_of_crime) but the Court did not formally endorse them.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity in section (c) 'Whether the interference was necessary in a democratic society.' The Court concludes that the interference was NOT necessary. Key passages:

- 'the authorities have not explained why, instead of taking measures to minimise the disruption to traffic and implementing other safety measures, they decided to refuse "authorisation" of the demonstration and subsequently to disperse it.'
- 'the authorities dispersed the demonstration shortly after it began, despite the fact that it had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner up to that point. This already calls into question the Government's assertion about the necessity of the dispersal.'
- 'the authorities have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration.'
- 'the authorities did not adduce sufficient and relevant reasons justifying the applicant's arrest and conviction. Moreover, the sanction imposed on him was unwarranted by the circumstances of the case and disproportionate within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention.'
- The Court also notes the lack of balancing: 'The authorities made no effort to balance the applicant's right to participate in the demonstration against any damage this could cause to other public or private interests.'

Necessity is explicitly discussed and found to be absent (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly frame any part of its analysis in terms of a 'positive obligation' on the government. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (i.e., unjustified interference). Positive obligations are not discussed.
