**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court explicitly concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key paragraph states: "The Court finds that there has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention and dismisses the Government's objection as to the exhaustion of domestic remedies in respect of the first applicant."

Additionally, the Court references its findings in Gafgaz Mammadov: "Having regard to the facts of the present case and the clear similarity between these facts and those in the case of Gafgaz Mammadov on all relevant and crucial points, the Court sees no particular circumstances that could compel it to deviate from its findings in that judgment, and finds that, in the present case, the first applicant's right to freedom of assembly was breached for the same reasons as those outlined in that judgment."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. It refers to "the demonstration of 2 April 2011" and the first applicant's "administrative arrest and conviction for failing to stop participating in an unauthorised demonstration." The existence of an assembly is explicitly treated as given.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly addresses this sub-element, noting the complexity arising from the applicant's denial of intent to participate. The Court states: "Furthermore, the first applicant's administrative arrest and conviction for failing to stop participating in an unauthorised demonstration, under Article 310.1 (failure to comply with a lawful order of a police officer) of the CAO, was directed at activities falling within the scope of freedom of assembly, and he was sanctioned for engaging in such activities, despite denying that he had intended to take part in the assembly in his appeal. The Court considers that, in such circumstances, the first applicant's conviction must be regarded as constituting an interference with the exercise of his right to freedom of assembly. To hold otherwise would be tantamount to requiring him to acknowledge the acts of which he stood accused." The Court thus implicitly accepts participation (or at least that the conviction was directed at assembly-related activities) as established.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly discusses this: "The Court notes that the case file, including the domestic court decisions submitted by the parties, does not suggest that the demonstration of 2 April 2011 was violent. The Court therefore accepts the first applicant's assertion that the demonstration had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner up until his arrest."

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "The Court considers that, in such circumstances, the first applicant's conviction must be regarded as constituting an interference with the exercise of his right to freedom of assembly." The Government had argued there was no interference because the applicant denied intending to participate, but the Court rejected this argument. The Court also notes the chilling effect: "The first applicant's arrest and the administrative proceedings against him could not but have had the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law. The Court refers to the Gafgaz Mammadov judgment for its full reasoning ("§§ 50-68"), and the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not addressed in the text provided. These elements are not_discussed in the reasoning excerpt provided.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim in the text provided. It defers to Gafgaz Mammadov for the full analysis. No specific legitimate aim is identified in the reasoning excerpt.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court does not explicitly discuss necessity in the text provided, again deferring to Gafgaz Mammadov. However, the Court does note the chilling effect of the measures: "Those measures undoubtedly have a chilling effect, which deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate." This is consistent with a finding that the interference was not necessary in a democratic society, but the full necessity analysis is incorporated by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov rather than conducted explicitly here. Given the violation finding and the reference to Gafgaz Mammadov §§ 50-68, necessity is implied as having been found not satisfied.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in the reasoning provided. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (interference) case.
