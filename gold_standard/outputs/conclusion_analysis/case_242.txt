**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "participants and organisers of public assemblies," which logically presupposes that (i) assemblies existed, (ii) the applicants participated in them, and (iii) the assemblies were peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly reasoned.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" and "the applicants' freedom of assembly."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the description of the applicants as "participants and organisers of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied by the application of Article 11 protection and the finding of a violation; no suggestion of violence is made.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the introductory paragraph describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants and organisers of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This confirms that interference is explicitly discussed and found to exist.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it did not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are therefore not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and the principles in Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania, Oya Ataman v. Turkey, and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova. The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this reasoning. This element is entirely absent.
