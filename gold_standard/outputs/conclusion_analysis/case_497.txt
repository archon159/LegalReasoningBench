**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section: 'In the light of the foregoing, the Court considers that the intervention of the police officers in the demonstration, coupled with the subsequent prosecution of the applicants for their participation in a peaceful demonstration, was disproportionate and not necessary for preventing disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the four applicants.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly define or analyze whether an 'assembly' existed, but it is implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to 'the meeting,' 'the demonstration,' and 'a peaceful demonstration' repeatedly, treating the existence of an assembly as a given. For example: 'the intervention of the police officers in the demonstration' and 'their participation in a peaceful demonstration.' This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court implies that the applicants participated in the assembly. It states: 'the police officers arrested four of the applicants, together with a number of other people taking part in the demonstration, on the ground that they had breached the Meetings and Demonstration Marches Act.' The applicants' participation is treated as established fact, implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a doctrinal element.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. It states: 'the decision adopted by the Malatya Criminal Court of First Instance also shows that the group neither presented a danger to public order nor engaged in acts of violence.' It further states: 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings.' And: 'the Court is concerned by the fact that the four applicants were prosecuted – albeit subsequently acquitted – on account of the mere fact of their participation in a peaceful demonstration.' The assembly is explicitly found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and affirms interference: 'The Court considers that the intervention of the police officers, which led to the subsequent arrests of the four applicants for participating in the meeting, as well as the ill-treatment to which they were subjected, constituted an interference with their rights under Article 11 of the Convention.' This is explicit. Notably, for one applicant (Ms İspir), the Court found no interference: 'It follows that there has been no interference with Ms İspir's rights under Article 11 of the Convention.'

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this element: 'it is noted that the interference in the present case had a legal basis, namely sections 23 and 24 of the Meetings and Demonstration Marches Act, and was thus "prescribed by law" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.' The Court finds the prescribed-by-law requirement satisfied. However, the Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court simply states the interference had a legal basis without analyzing foreseeability.

**ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.

**iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: 'As regards a legitimate aim, the Government submitted that the interference pursued, among others, the legitimate aim of preventing public disorder, and the Court finds no reason to differ.' The aim found is prevention of disorder (public disorder). The Government also referenced public safety in its submissions, and the Court quoted Article 11 § 2 as including 'public safety' and 'prevention of disorder,' but the Court's own finding focuses on 'preventing public disorder.'

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It states: 'Turning to the question of whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society"...' It then finds: 'the applicants and the other demonstrators did not act in breach of this law, contrary to the allegations of the Government'; 'the group neither presented a danger to public order nor engaged in acts of violence'; 'the Court is concerned by the fact that the four applicants were prosecuted – albeit subsequently acquitted – on account of the mere fact of their participation in a peaceful demonstration'; 'a peaceful demonstration should not, in principle, be made subject to the threat of a penal sanction'; and ultimately: 'the intervention of the police officers in the demonstration, coupled with the subsequent prosecution of the applicants for their participation in a peaceful demonstration, was disproportionate and not necessary for preventing disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court concludes necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly frame any part of its analysis in terms of a positive obligation on the state. The analysis is conducted entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). Positive obligations are not discussed.
