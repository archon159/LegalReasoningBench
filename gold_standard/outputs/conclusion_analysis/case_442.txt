**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *Assembly exists*: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *Participation*: Implied by the same passage — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- *Peaceful*: Implied — the Court proceeds to find a violation under Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies were non-peaceful, and the leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the nature of the interference: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies, and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis by reference to leading cases. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. This element is not discussed, and no aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is negative (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and states it found "no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is not discussed.
