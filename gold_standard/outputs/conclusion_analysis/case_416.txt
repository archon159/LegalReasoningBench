**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is drawn from the final paragraphs of the merits section:

'In view of the above considerations and bearing in mind the Court's above findings under Article 6 of the Convention, the Court concludes that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly, taken together with her right to freedom of expression, in the present case was disproportionate.'

'There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention, assessed in the light of its Article 10.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly treats the events as a 'demonstration' throughout. It notes: 'the demonstration involved only three persons.' The existence of an assembly is treated as given and not contested. This is implied by the entire analysis rather than explicitly stated as a separate finding.

**(ii) Participation:** The applicant's participation is implied throughout — she is described as having held the demonstration, held a banner, and been arrested in connection with it. The Court states: 'the applicant had refused (i) to stop this "non-authorised" demonstration.' Participation is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate element.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly refers to the assembly as peaceful in multiple places: 'the immediate dispersal of the peaceful demonstration (which is presumed to be lawful for the reasons stated above)' and 'although a demonstration in a public place may cause some disruption to ordinary life... it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings.' The peaceful nature is explicitly affirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: 'the Court considers that the relevant facts disclose an interference which was sufficiently linked to the applicant's exercise of her right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention, considered in conjunction with her freedom of expression under Article 10 of the Convention.' This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to rule on this: 'The parties disagreed as to whether the interference was prescribed by law and served a legitimate aim. However, the Court decides to dispense with ruling on the issue of lawfulness because, in any event, the interference fell short of being necessary in a democratic society, for the reasons set out below.' The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are therefore not discussed. The overall prescribed-by-law question is explicitly raised but left unresolved (null).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court also explicitly declines to rule on legitimate aim: 'The parties disagreed as to whether the interference was prescribed by law and served a legitimate aim. However, the Court decides to dispense with ruling on the issue of lawfulness...' The Government had argued the aims of public safety, prevention of disorder, and protection of the rights of others (specifically judges' reputation and honour). However, the Court does not make a finding on whether a legitimate aim was actually served. The Court does note: 'the Court cannot deal with the Government's argument suggesting that the demonstration had to be dispersed in order to put an end to unsubstantiated accusations in respect of judges and in order to protect their reputation and honour. This justification was never mentioned in the domestic proceedings and did not justify the interference with the applicant's rights.' This suggests the Court is skeptical of the aims advanced, but it does not formally conclude on legitimate aim. The conclusion is null.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed at length. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary:

- 'the Court concludes that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly, taken together with her right to freedom of expression, in the present case was disproportionate.'

Key reasoning includes:
- The domestic decisions contained contradictory submissions about the nature of the offence.
- The Government's arguments about non-compliance with notification requirements 'cannot weigh in the Court's proportionality analysis' because the domestic courts made no clear findings on this.
- 'there may be circumstances in which the formal unlawfulness of a peaceful public assembly is not sufficient to justify its dispersal.'
- 'the demonstration involved only three persons... it is difficult to conceive that such an event could have generated the gathering of a significant crowd warranting specific (for instance, security) measures.'
- The proceedings against the other two participants were discontinued.
- The Government's argument about protecting judges' reputation 'was never mentioned in the domestic proceedings and did not justify the interference.'
- 'the national courts in the administrative offence procedure in the present case did not assess the applicant's situation, including the penalty to be imposed, taking into account the relevant principles under Articles 10 and 11 of the Convention.'
- 'It cannot be said that the national authorities adduced reasons which could be accepted as "relevant and sufficient".'

This element is explicitly discussed and the conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary — answering 'no' to whether the government adequately balanced).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (interference by the state). This element is not discussed.
