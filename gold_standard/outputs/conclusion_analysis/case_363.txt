**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits assessment states: "The Court finds therefore that there has been a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether a 'rally' constitutes an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the entire analysis — the Court proceeds directly to examine the ban on the rally under Article 11 without questioning whether a rally qualifies as an assembly. The admissibility section references "the right to freedom of assembly under Article 11" in the context of the rally, implying the assembly element is satisfied.

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation/organisational role. In the admissibility section: "the right to freedom of assembly under Article 11 of the Convention can be exercised not only by the participants in a gathering but also by those who organise it, whether they be individuals or legal persons (see Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania [GC], no. 37553/05, § 91, ECHR 2015, with further references). Although when notifying the mayor about the rally planned for 30 September 2006 and when seeking judicial review of his decision to ban it the applicant purported to act on behalf of the unregistered Macedonian Initiative Committee, he was one of the rally's organisers... He can therefore claim to be a victim with respect to the decision to ban the rally."

In the merits section, the Court reaffirms: "The Court has already found that the applicant, who was one of the rally's organisers, was personally affected by the mayor's decision to ban it... The applicant was not proposing to hold a 'solo demonstration' but to take part in a gathering with others (contrast Novikova and Others v. Russia, nos. 25501/07 and 4 others, § 91, 26 April 2016)."

*iii) Peaceful assembly:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assembly was peaceful. However, this is implied — the Court proceeds to examine the ban under Article 11 without any suggestion that the assembly was not peaceful. The Government's justification for the ban was based on public-order concerns and the coincidence with a municipal event, not on any claim that the rally itself would be violent.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference. In the merits section: "the Court considers that the ban amounted to a 'restriction' of the applicant's right to peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention. The fact that it was only the applicant who complained to the Court in that respect does not mean that that right is not in issue."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to examine this element: "There is no need to examine whether the restriction was 'prescribed by law' or pursued one or more of the aims set out in Article 11 § 2 because, even assuming that it was and did, it was in any event not 'necessary in a democratic society'..."

Accordingly, the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not discussed. The Court assumes without deciding that the restriction was prescribed by law.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly declines to examine this element, as quoted above: "There is no need to examine whether the restriction was 'prescribed by law' or pursued one or more of the aims set out in Article 11 § 2 because, even assuming that it was and did, it was in any event not 'necessary in a democratic society'..."

The Government's submissions referenced public-order considerations and the smooth running of a municipal event, which could relate to 'public safety' or 'prevention of disorder' or 'rights of others', but the Court does not adopt any of these as established legitimate aims. No aims are formally found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and decided. The Court finds the restriction was NOT necessary in a democratic society: "even assuming that it was and did, it was in any event not 'necessary in a democratic society' for the following reasons."

The Court notes: "The mayor and the court which upheld his decision justified the ban by reference to the holding of a municipal event at the same time and place and by the need to protect the participants in that event from being exposed to controversial statements on historical issues seen as sensitive... In four materially identical cases such grounds have been found to be insufficient for the purposes of Article 11 § 2."

Further: "The domestic authorities provided no details regarding the logistical or security difficulties which two parallel events might have posed and, more importantly, the Government have not explained why justifications considered insufficient in previous cases should suffice in the instant one. In addition, it is noteworthy that on a previous occasion when the authorities did not ban a rally organised by Ilinden, they allowed a counter-demonstration to proceed on the same day."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference (a ban), and no positive obligation analysis is undertaken.
