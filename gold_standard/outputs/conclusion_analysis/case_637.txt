**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is found in the final paragraphs of Section B.2 (The Court's assessment):

'Having regard to the facts of the present case and their clear similarity to those of the Gafgaz Mammadov case on all relevant and crucial points, the Court sees no particular circumstances that could compel it to deviate from its findings in that judgment, and finds that in the present case the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was breached for the same reasons as those outlined above.'

And conclusively: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly confirms that an assembly existed by referring to 'the demonstration' throughout. More explicitly, the Court references 'the dispersal of the demonstration' and notes it was 'intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner.' The assembly's existence is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate doctrinal step.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes 'a lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention.' This confirms the applicant participated in the assembly. This is explicitly discussed.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly states 'a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner.' The peaceful nature of the assembly is explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. It refers to 'the dispersal of the demonstration by the police and his arrest and conviction for an administrative offence' as the subject of the complaint, and the entire analysis proceeds on the basis that these acts constituted interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly. This is implied by the structure of the analysis, though the dispersal and arrest/conviction are explicitly mentioned as the acts at issue.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the 'prescribed by law' element by referencing the Gafgaz Mammadov analysis: 'the Court noted the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being arbitrarily banned or dispersed.' This addresses foreseeability and safeguards against abuse.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed — 'serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies.' The conclusion is negative (not sufficiently foreseeable).

**(ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed. The Government submitted that 'the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly had been precise and foreseeable,' but the Court does not separately address accessibility. Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Explicitly discussed — 'the possibility of public assemblies being arbitrarily banned or dispersed' and 'doubts about the credibility of the formal grounds, namely Article 310.1 of the CAO, relied on by the authorities to arrest and convict the applicant.' The conclusion is negative (insufficient safeguards).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued that 'the dispersal of the demonstration had pursued the aim of protecting the rights and freedoms of others and preventing disorder, and had been in the interests of national security.' The Court does not explicitly accept or reject these aims — it proceeds to find a violation without clearly resolving whether a legitimate aim was established. The aims are mentioned in the parties' submissions but the Court does not make a definitive finding on this element, instead focusing on the lack of sufficient reasons and the prescribed-by-law concerns. The legitimate aim element is mentioned but not conclusively resolved by the Court itself.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses necessity by referencing 'a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner' and 'a failure by the authorities to take into consideration the fact that notice of the demonstration had been given.' The conclusion is negative — the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). Not discussed.
