**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" being interfered with, and describes the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies." This implies that an assembly existed, that the applicants participated (or tried to), and that the assemblies were within the scope of Article 11 protection. The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov — all involving peaceful assemblies — further implies that the assemblies were peaceful. None of these sub-elements are explicitly discussed as separate analytical steps.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the phrase "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and the reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the same phrase — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied by the overall framing and the reference to leading cases involving peaceful assemblies; no suggestion of violence is made.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" (plural) confirms that the Court treats the arrests, dispersals, and convictions as interferences with Article 11 rights. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or examined. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not_discussed, and no aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and states it has "not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion." The necessity element is explicitly discussed and resolved against the government (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary, hence a violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state. This element is not_discussed.
