**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passages are:

- "Accordingly, the Court considers that in the instant case the police intervention against the demonstration was disproportionate and was not necessary for preventing disorder and protecting public safety within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."
- "There has accordingly been a violation of that provision."

These appear in the final two paragraphs of the Article 11 assessment section.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court implicitly accepts that an assembly existed. It describes "the group in question concerned around 30-35 people... carrying banners and candles and... wanted to read out a press statement regarding Israel's actions against Palestine... at Tünnel square." The Court proceeds to analyze the demonstration under Article 11 without questioning whether an assembly existed, so this is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

*ii) Participation:* The Court states "the police intervention and the subsequent arrest of the applicant constituted, in itself, an interference with the applicant's rights under Article 11 of the Convention." This implies the applicant participated in the assembly. Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element, but implied.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses this: "where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings." The Court applies this principle to the instant case, implying it found the assembly to be peaceful. The Government did not contest the peaceful nature of the demonstration. This is implied rather than explicitly stated as a finding.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: "The Court considers that the police intervention and the subsequent arrest of the applicant constituted, in itself, an interference with the applicant's rights under Article 11 of the Convention (see Saya and Others v. Turkey, no. 4327/02, § 39, 7 October 2008)."

Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this: "the Court is satisfied that the interference in the present case had a legal basis, namely Article 24 of the Assemblies and Marches Act (Law no. 2911), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention."

The Court does not separately discuss foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards as sub-elements. It simply states satisfaction that the interference had a legal basis. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not discussed explicitly or implicitly.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: "the Court is satisfied that the interference in the present case... pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting public safety."

Aims found: prevention of disorder and public safety.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court:
- Notes the Government "failed to elaborate on the exact grounds why the demonstration organised by the Human Rights Association was unlawful."
- Acknowledges the demonstration was unlawful due to absence of prior notification, but states "an unlawful situation does not justify an infringement of freedom of assembly."
- Observes the group was small (30-35 people), carrying banners and candles, wanting to read a press statement — posing no evident danger to public order.
- Notes the dispersal was "quite prompt" and the applicant did not have "sufficient time - together with his fellow demonstrators - to manifest his views."
- Concludes: "the police intervention against the demonstration was disproportionate and was not necessary for preventing disorder and protecting public safety within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

Conclusion: necessity not satisfied (no).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of a negative obligation (unjustified interference). Not discussed.
