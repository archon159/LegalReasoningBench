**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm both the substantive finding (not necessary in a democratic society) and the formal conclusion (breach of Article 11).

**2. Analysis of canonical doctrine elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for Article 11 to be engaged at all. The reference to the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" in the opening paragraph also implies that an assembly existed and that the applicants participated. The peaceful nature is implied by the fact that the Court proceeds to assess the interference under Article 11 without excluding the assembly from protection. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: The opening paragraph states "organisers or participants of public assemblies," implying assemblies existed. Implied, conclusion: yes.
- **scope_participation**: The opening paragraph refers to the applicants as "organisers or participants," implying participation. Implied, conclusion: yes.
- **scope_peaceful**: Not explicitly discussed, but implied by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis and find a violation. Implied, conclusion: yes.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the penultimate paragraph: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Interference is therefore explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. Since the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis and finds a violation there, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim pursued by the interference. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment. No legitimate aim is identified. Not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" (the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. Not discussed.
