**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of the assessment section:

'Having regard to the above considerations, the Court concludes that the Georgian authorities failed to act with due tolerance and good faith as regards the applicants' right to freedom of assembly, did not adduce sufficient and relevant reasons justifying the interference, and imposed a sanction which was disproportionate in the circumstances. It follows that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court explicitly notes that 'the applicants held a demonstration in front of the Tbilisi Court of Appeal' and earlier states that 'The Court reiterates that the right to freedom of assembly covers both private meetings and meetings on public thoroughfares, as well as static meetings and public processions.' The existence of an assembly (a demonstration/picket) is explicitly discussed and confirmed.

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly notes that 'the demonstration, in which just five persons participated, was dispersed a few minutes after it had started.' The applicants' participation in the assembly is explicitly confirmed.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly in multiple places: 'given the absence of any violent behaviour by the applicants during the authorised picket' and 'the applicants addressed the Tbilisi Court of Appeal without being violent or rejecting democratic principles in their speech.' The assembly is explicitly found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: 'Their prosecution, as conceded by the Government, amounted to an interference with the exercise of the freedom of peaceful assembly. It must therefore be determined whether the interference complained of was "prescribed by law", prompted by one or more of the legitimate aims set out in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, and "necessary in a democratic society".' The Government itself conceded interference, and the Court confirms it. Conclusion: yes, interference found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the lawfulness requirement: 'As regards the requirement of "lawfulness", the Court reiterates that both section 76(3) § 1 (f) of the Courts Act and Article 208 § 6(1) of the CCP...expressly and concomitantly stated that actions allegedly constituting a breach of public order and contempt of court could be prevented by court bailiffs and punished by the President of the relevant court if committed inside court-houses.' The Court also notes the Constitutional Court of Georgia characterised the contempt of court definition as vague.

*i) Foreseeability:* Explicitly discussed: 'the Court...has very serious doubts that the applicants could reasonably have foreseen that their demonstration outside the building of the Tbilisi Court of Appeal, in its yard, could ever have attracted liability under the above-mentioned wording of the domestic provisions.' The Court expresses serious doubts about foreseeability, suggesting a negative conclusion, but does not make a definitive finding because it proceeds to examine necessity instead: 'However, having regard to a more conspicuous problem arising with respect to the necessity of the interference, the Court does not deem it appropriate to limit its finding under Article 11 of the Convention to the lawfulness of the interference only.' So foreseeability is explicitly discussed but left unresolved (conclusion: null, as the Court declines to make a definitive finding).

*ii) Accessibility:* The Government argued the provisions 'fully satisfied the relevant requirements of accessibility, foreseeability and precision.' The Court does not separately address accessibility in its own assessment — it focuses on foreseeability and then moves to necessity. Accessibility is not explicitly discussed by the Court in its own assessment (not_discussed, conclusion: null).

*iii) Safeguards against abuse:* Not explicitly discussed by the Court in its assessment. The Court does note the vagueness of the contempt of court definition (citing the Constitutional Court of Georgia), which could be read as implying insufficient safeguards, but this is not explicitly framed as a safeguards analysis. Conclusion: null, not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: 'The Court can accept the Government's argument that the interference in question pursued a legitimate aim: the prevention of disorder and the protection of the authority of the judiciary.' The Court accepts two aims: prevention of disorder and protection of the authority of the judiciary (which maps to 'rights of others' or more precisely the authority of the judiciary as a sub-category). In the ECHR Article 11 § 2 framework, 'maintaining the authority and impartiality of the judiciary' is listed as a legitimate aim (borrowed from Article 10 § 2, applied here via the Article 10 lens). The Court accepts the legitimate aim. Conclusion: yes.

Aims found: prevention of disorder, and protection of the authority of the judiciary. The latter is not a listed aim in Article 11 § 2 per se, but the Court applies it via Article 10 principles. For the purposes of the schema, the closest listed aims are 'prevention_of_disorder' and arguably 'rights_of_others' (as the authority of the judiciary protects the rights of others to a fair trial). I will list 'prevention_of_disorder' and 'rights_of_others'.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court examines: (1) the small size of the demonstration (five persons) and its very short duration; (2) the content of the slogans — mostly critical value judgments on a matter of public concern, not contempt of court; (3) the only offensive phrase ('bastard') was directed at the Minister of Interior, not a member of the judiciary; (4) the decision of the President of the Tbilisi Court of Appeal lacked relevant and sufficient reasons; (5) the penalty of 30 days' deprivation of liberty was the most severe applicable and disproportionate given the absence of violent behaviour.

The Court concludes: 'the Georgian authorities failed to act with due tolerance and good faith as regards the applicants' right to freedom of assembly, did not adduce sufficient and relevant reasons justifying the interference, and imposed a sanction which was disproportionate in the circumstances.' Necessity: not satisfied. Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly frame any part of its analysis in terms of a positive obligation on the state to facilitate or protect the assembly. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). Positive obligation: not discussed. Conclusion: null.
