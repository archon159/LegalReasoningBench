**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "Bearing in mind the above circumstances, the Court concludes that the interference did not correspond to a pressing social need and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the planned protest demonstration constitutes an "assembly" within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied by the Court's entire analysis — the Court proceeds directly to discuss interference with the "right to freedom of assembly," which logically presupposes that the planned event qualifies as an assembly. The Court refers to "Hyde Park's application to hold a demonstration" and "a peaceful rally in support of freedom of speech," implying the existence of an assembly.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Not explicitly discussed. It is implied, however, since the applicants are described as having submitted an application to hold the demonstration, meaning they were the organizers/participants. The Court's entire analysis presupposes their participation.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
This is explicitly discussed: "Moreover, there was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities or other groups meeting in the park on the day in question. Rather their intention was to hold a peaceful rally in support of freedom of speech." This confirms the peaceful nature of the assembly.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: "It is common ground between the parties, and the Court agrees, that the decision to reject Hyde Park's application to hold a demonstration on 14 October 2005 amounted to 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11." The Government also accepted that there had been an interference. Conclusion: yes, there was interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the lawfulness issue but deliberately leaves it unresolved as a standalone question, merging it with the necessity analysis: "In so far as the lawfulness of the interference is concerned, the Court notes that under section 14 of the Assemblies Act the Chișinău Municipality was obliged to give reasons in writing for rejecting Hyde Park's application... According to section 12(6) of the Assemblies Act, an application could be rejected only if the Municipality was in possession of evidence that the provisions of sections 6 and 7 would be breached with serious consequences for society. The Municipality's decision appears not to have relied on any of the reasons provided for in sections 6 and 7 of the Assemblies Act. This in itself might be a sufficient basis for the conclusion that the impugned measures were not 'prescribed by law'. However, in the present case, the Court considers that the issue of practical compliance with the law is indissociable from the question as to whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society'. It will therefore examine this issue below."

The Court thus explicitly discusses the prescribed-by-law element but does not reach a definitive conclusion on it, folding it into the necessity analysis. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually addressed.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element. The Court's concern is about whether the Municipality's reasons were consistent with the Assemblies Act, which touches on foreseeability (the law sets out specific grounds for rejection), but this is not framed in terms of the standard foreseeability test. Conclusion: null.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed. Conclusion: null.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Not discussed as a distinct sub-element. Conclusion: null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed but left unresolved: "The parties also disagreed as to whether the interference served a legitimate aim. The Court, for the reasons set out below, does not consider it necessary to decide this point either." The Court declines to determine whether a legitimate aim was pursued. No specific aim is identified or confirmed. Conclusion: null; aims_found: [].

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court recalls the general principles about democracy, pluralism, and proportionality, then applies them to the facts: "Turning to the circumstances of the present case, the Court observes that the Municipality rejected Hyde Park's application to hold a protest demonstration planned for 14 October 2005 on the grounds that other events were scheduled to take place in the Stefan cel Mare Park... For the Court, the Municipality's reasons cannot be considered relevant and sufficient within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention. There was no suggestion that the park in which the assembly was to take place was too small to accommodate all the various events planned there. Moreover, there was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities or other groups meeting in the park on the day in question. Rather their intention was to hold a peaceful rally in support of freedom of speech. Therefore, the Court can only conclude that the Municipality's refusal to authorise the demonstration did not respond to a pressing social need."

The Court also disregards the reasons given by the courts in subsequent judicial proceedings because those courts lacked authority under the Assemblies Act to authorize or reject assemblies, and their decisions came after the planned date of the demonstration.

Conclusion: The interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society (i.e., the necessity test was not satisfied by the government). The answer to "did government adequately balance?" is no.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by a public authority). Conclusion: not discussed.
