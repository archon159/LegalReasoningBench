**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's explicit finding of an "interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" logically implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated (or tried to), and the assembly must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants or organisers of public assemblies")
- scope_participation: implied yes (the applicants are described as "participants or organisers of public assemblies")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court proceeds to assess the interference under Article 11, which presupposes a peaceful assembly)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly confirmed, though the Court does not engage in a detailed separate analysis of this element — it is treated as established by reference to the leading cases.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment, relying on its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). Since the violation is found on necessity grounds, the prescribed-by-law element is left unaddressed (it may be assumed to be satisfied given the Court proceeds to necessity, but this is not stated).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing this element. The aims_found list is therefore empty.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion. The necessity element is therefore explicitly discussed and decided in the negative (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary, hence a violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely as one of unjustified interference (negative obligation), not a failure to protect assembly rights. This element is not discussed at all.
