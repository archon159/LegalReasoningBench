**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of Section III.B.2 (The Court's assessment):

'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

The Court also explains its reasoning in the preceding paragraphs of the same section, noting: 'the Court sees no particular circumstances that could compel it to deviate from its findings in those judgments, and finds that in the present cases the applicants' right to freedom of assembly was breached for the same reasons as those outlined in the above-mentioned judgments.' And: 'The applicants' arrest and the administrative proceedings against them could only have had the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies. Those measures undoubtedly have a chilling effect, which deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness) in the reasoning section reproduced. However, the entire analysis presupposes that there were demonstrations (assemblies), that the applicants participated (or tried to participate) in them, and that the assemblies were peaceful. This is implied by the Court's framing of the complaint ('their arrest and conviction had been measures used by the authorities to punish them for participating in demonstrations and/or to prevent them from doing so') and by the finding of a violation of the right to freedom of assembly. The reference to 'political rallies' and 'demonstrations' implies the existence of assemblies. The peaceful nature is implied by the fact that the Court treats the right as applicable without any suggestion that the assemblies fell outside the protection of Article 11.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied — the Court refers to 'demonstrations' and 'political rallies' throughout, treating the existence of assemblies as given.
- **scope_participation**: Implied — the complaint itself is framed as the applicants 'participating in demonstrations,' and the Court accepts this framing.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court applies Article 11 without any discussion of violence or non-peaceful conduct, implying the assemblies were peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. In the merits section: 'The applicants' arrest and the administrative proceedings against them could only have had the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies. Those measures undoubtedly have a chilling effect, which deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations.' The arrest and administrative proceedings are identified as the interfering acts. The conclusion is affirmative (yes, there was interference).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court proceeds directly to find a violation by reference to prior judgments, without separately analyzing the 'prescribed by law' requirement in the reproduced text. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No legitimate aim is identified or assessed in the reproduced reasoning. The Court jumps to the conclusion of a violation by reference to prior case law, without separately addressing legitimate aim.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the necessity test in the traditional sense (proportionality balancing). However, the finding that the arrest and administrative proceedings had a 'chilling effect' and discouraged participation in political debate is functionally equivalent to a finding that the measures were not necessary in a democratic society. This is implied by the overall conclusion of a violation and the reference to prior judgments where this analysis was presumably conducted. The Court's statement about the 'chilling effect' implies a finding that the measures were disproportionate and not necessary.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in the reproduced reasoning. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference by the state (arrest and administrative proceedings), not as a failure to protect the applicants' assembly rights. Positive obligations are not discussed.

**Summary of findings:**

- Scope (overall): implied yes
- Assembly exists: implied yes
- Participation: implied yes
- Peaceful: implied yes
- Interference: explicitly yes ('The applicants' arrest and the administrative proceedings against them could only have had the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies.')
- Prescribed by law: not discussed
- Prescribed by law — foreseeable: not discussed
- Prescribed by law — accessible: not discussed
- Prescribed by law — safeguards: not discussed
- Legitimate aim: not discussed
- Necessity: implied no (the chilling effect finding implies the measures were not necessary)
- Positive obligation: not discussed
