**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is found in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

- 'Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the measures applied to the applicants as peaceful participants in the public assembly did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society.'
- 'These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention.'

These passages together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court explicitly addresses this by reference to its earlier finding: 'The Court refers to its finding that the gathering in question fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Navalnyy v. Russia [GC], nos. 29580/12 and 4 others, §§ 110-12, 15 November 2018).' This is an explicit finding that an assembly existed and fell within the scope of Article 11.

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly addresses participation: 'It considers that in the present case the applicants' intentions and conduct were also covered by the notion of peaceful assembly, including those of Ms Polyakova who claimed that she was acting as an observer for a group of human rights defender organisations.' This confirms that all applicants, including the observer, were participating in the assembly.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature: 'It considers that in the present case the applicants' intentions and conduct were also covered by the notion of peaceful assembly...' and later refers to 'the measures applied to the applicants as peaceful participants in the public assembly.' The assembly is explicitly found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: 'It finds that the applicants' arrests, transfer to the police station and the ensuing sanctions constituted an interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 § 2.' This is an explicit and affirmative finding of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The Government referenced the legal basis (Articles 19.3 § 1 and 20.2 § 5 of the CAO and sections 5 and 7 of the Public Events Act), but the Court does not engage with this element — it proceeds directly to necessity. The prescribed-by-law sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are therefore not discussed. Given that the Court jumps to necessity and finds a violation there, the prescribed-by-law question is left unresolved/not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. It does not identify any of the listed aims. The Government's submissions focused on the lack of notification procedure compliance, which could relate to prevention of disorder or public safety, but the Court does not engage with this analysis. The legitimate aim element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly addresses necessity: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the measures applied to the applicants as peaceful participants in the public assembly did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society.' This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was NOT met (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation. The Court also references proportionality principles from its case-law (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova, Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania) and leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by state authorities). Positive obligations are not mentioned or implied.
