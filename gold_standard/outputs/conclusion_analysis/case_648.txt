**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section: "In view of the above, the Court concludes that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The finding is grounded in two sub-conclusions: (i) "the forceful intervention of the police officers was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention," and (ii) "the sanction imposed on some of the applicants, namely one year and three months' imprisonment, cannot be considered proportionate in the circumstances of the case."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court implicitly accepts that an assembly existed. It describes "the applicants participated in a gathering on 31 July 2000 in a park in order to protest against F-type prisons regime and the violent clashes that had occurred in Bergama Prison." The Court proceeds to analyze the case under Article 11 without questioning whether an assembly existed, so this is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element.

*ii) Participation:* The Court states "the applicants participated in a gathering" and that "the subsequent conviction of the applicants for participating in the meeting constituted, in itself, an interference with the applicants' rights under Article 11." Participation is implied by the interference analysis rather than separately discussed as a threshold question.

*iii) Peaceful assembly:* The Court explicitly addresses this: "It appears that the protestors, including the applicants, wished to draw attention to a sensitive subject concerning the conditions of detention in prisons and that the gathering was initially peaceful." It also states: "where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings." The Court acknowledges that some violence occurred later but attributes this to the police intervention escalating tensions. The assembly is treated as peaceful for Article 11 purposes.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court considers that the police intervention and the subsequent conviction of the applicants for participating in the meeting constituted, in itself, an interference with the applicants' rights under Article 11." The Government had argued there was no interference, but the Court rejected this.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed, but only at the overall level. The Court states: "it is noted that the interference in the present case had a legal basis, namely section 22 of the Assemblies and Marches Act (Law no. 2911), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." The Court does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or adequacy of safeguards as distinct sub-questions. It simply concludes the prescribed-by-law requirement is met by reference to the statutory basis.

*i) Foreseeable:* Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element. The overall "prescribed by law" conclusion is yes, but foreseeability is not separately analyzed.

*ii) Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element.

*iii) Safeguards:* Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "As regards a legitimate aim, the Government submitted that the interference pursued the legitimate aim of preventing public disorder and the Court finds no reason to differ." The aim found is prevention of disorder (and the quoted Article 11 § 2 text also references public safety).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the necessity test and concludes the interference was not necessary. Key passages: "the forceful intervention of the police officers was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court also notes: "there is no evidence to suggest that the group in question initially presented a serious danger to public order" and "the authorities intervened swiftly with considerable force in order to disperse them, thereby causing tensions to rise, followed by clashes." The Court also finds the criminal sanction disproportionate: "the sanction imposed on some of the applicants, namely one year and three months' imprisonment, cannot be considered proportionate in the circumstances of the case."

**(f) Positive obligation**

Explicitly mentioned but not the basis for the violation finding. The Court states: "although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities in the exercise of the rights protected, there may also be positive obligations to secure their effective enjoyment (see Djavit An, cited above, § 57, and Oya Ataman, cited above, § 36)." However, the Court does not separately conclude that a positive obligation was violated; the violation is found on the basis of disproportionate interference (negative obligation). The positive obligation is mentioned in passing as part of the general principles, so it is discussed but no separate conclusion is reached on it.
