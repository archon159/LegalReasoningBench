**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm both the substantive finding (not necessary in a democratic society) and the formal conclusion (breach of Article 11).

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies" and its finding of an "interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The Court would not have proceeded to assess interference and necessity without implicitly accepting that the assemblies existed, that the applicants participated (as organisers and/or participants), and that the assemblies were peaceful (since the Court applies Article 11 protection and references leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov, all of which involved peaceful assemblies).

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" without questioning their existence.
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers and/or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court proceeds under Article 11 and references cases involving peaceful assemblies; no suggestion of violence.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The introductory paragraph also describes the measures: "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit discussion of interference, though the Court does not separately analyse whether these acts constitute interference — it treats it as given, consistent with the leading cases cited.

Conclusion: yes, explicitly discussed (by direct reference to "the interferences").

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. None of the three sub-elements are discussed.

- **prescribed_by_law**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment. No specific aim is identified or examined. The aims_found list is therefore empty.

Conclusion: not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the explicit basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to a different conclusion.

Conclusion: no (the interference was NOT necessary), explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). No positive obligation is spelled out or found violated.

Conclusion: not discussed.
