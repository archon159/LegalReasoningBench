**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of Section IV.C.3: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court reached this conclusion after finding that the interference was not prescribed by law (manifestly arbitrary), making it unnecessary to examine legitimate aim and necessity.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly references the assembly at Freedom Square on 22 February 2008. In Section C.1, the Court notes: 'the applicant's complaints under Articles 10 and 11 are mainly based on the allegation that his prosecution and conviction were a measure to prevent him from participating in demonstrations and to punish him for having done so.' The existence of the assembly is treated as an established fact throughout the judgment, though not formally analyzed as a separate sub-element. It is implied by the entire framing of the case.

**(ii) Participation:** The applicant's participation is explicitly referenced multiple times. In Section C.2: 'the applicant was a high-ranking public official who was fired from his post after publicly expressing his support for the protest movement... On the day following his speech at Freedom Square critical of the authorities...' and 'a charge was brought against the applicant under Article 300 of the CC of "usurpation of power" which... can be understood to have concerned the applicant's involvement in the protest movement led by Mr Ter-Petrosyan, including the support shown by the applicant to that movement through his participation in the assembly at Freedom Square and the speech he had given there.' Participation is treated as established fact, implied rather than formally analyzed.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court does not explicitly analyze whether the assembly was peaceful as a separate sub-element. However, the applicant states in his submissions: 'his speech at Freedom Square had not incited violence, public disorder or hatred.' The Court's willingness to apply Article 11 protection implies it accepted the assembly was peaceful, but this is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses interference in Section C.2. It states: 'The Court reiterates that an interference with the freedom of peaceful assembly does not need to amount to an outright ban, legal or de facto, but can consist in various other measures taken by the authorities. The term "restrictions" in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an assembly, such as a prior ban, dispersal of the rally or the arrest of participants, and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards, including penalties imposed for having taken part in a rally.' After extensive analysis of the facts, the Court concludes: 'The Court is, therefore, prepared to assume that the entirety of the facts on which the applicant's prosecution and conviction were based can be regarded, on arguable grounds, as an instance of an "interference" with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly.' The conclusion is affirmative (yes, there was interference), though expressed with some caution ('prepared to assume').

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this in Section C.3: 'The first step in the Court's examination is to determine whether the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly was "prescribed by law".' The Court concludes: 'the impugned interference with the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly could only be characterised as manifestly arbitrary and, consequently, unlawful for the purposes of Article 11 of the Convention.' And: 'The Court therefore concludes that the interference in question did not meet the Convention requirement of lawfulness.'

The Court does not separately analyze the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) in the traditional sense. Instead, it focuses on the arbitrary nature of the prosecution as the basis for finding the interference not prescribed by law. The finding of arbitrariness subsumes all three sub-elements — the Court does not find the legal provision itself deficient, but rather that the prosecution was a disguised/pretextual use of criminal law. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually discussed; the overall conclusion is that the interference was not prescribed by law due to its arbitrary character. These sub-elements are therefore not discussed (the overall 'prescribed by law' conclusion is explicit, but the individual sub-elements are not_discussed).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states it will not examine legitimate aim: 'That being so, it is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was proportionate to the aim pursued.' No legitimate aim is found or discussed. The conclusion is null (not reached).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly states it will not examine necessity, as it already found the interference not prescribed by law: 'it is not required to determine whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and, if so, whether it was proportionate to the aim pursued.' Necessity is not discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by the state through prosecution and conviction). Positive obligations are not mentioned.
