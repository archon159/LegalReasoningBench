**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

'Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society".'

And: 'These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention.'

No specific paragraph numbers are provided in the text as reproduced, but these conclusions appear in the final substantive paragraphs of the Article 11 section.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

- **(i) Assembly exists:** Not explicitly discussed. However, the Court refers to 'participants of public assemblies' and 'dispersal of these assemblies,' which implies that an assembly existed. This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed.
- **(ii) Participation:** Not explicitly discussed. The applicants are described as 'participants of public assemblies,' implying their participation is taken as given. This is implied.
- **(iii) Peaceful:** Not explicitly discussed or implied. The Court does not address whether the assemblies were peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society".' The existence of interference is also implied by the description of 'disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by conviction for administrative offence.' The Court treats interference as established (implied by the necessity analysis), though it does not conduct a separate explicit analysis of whether interference occurred — it is taken as given from the facts described.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Not discussed at all — neither explicitly nor impliedly. The Court jumps directly to necessity without addressing whether the interference was prescribed by law.

- **(i) Foreseeable:** Not discussed.
- **(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.
- **(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. The Court does not identify or analyze any legitimate aim pursued by the interference.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only element explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society".' The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The Court does not spell out any positive obligation for the government in this reasoning.

**Summary of approach:** This is a repetitive-case judgment (a so-called 'clone' or 'repetitive' case) where the Court relies on its findings in leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and states it has found no new facts or arguments to depart from those conclusions. As a result, the Court does not conduct a full step-by-step analysis of each canonical element, but instead proceeds directly to the necessity conclusion and the violation finding. Scope and interference are implied from the factual description; prescribed by law, legitimate aim, and positive obligation are not discussed.
