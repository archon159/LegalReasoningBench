**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the Court's assessment under section B. Merits > 3. Whether the interference was justified > (b) The Court's assessment:

"Having regard to the foregoing considerations, the Court finds that the applicants' conviction for the criminal offence was not a necessary and proportionate measure in order to achieve the legitimate aims pursued. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In the interference section, the Court notes: "the applicants were convicted of an offence in connection to their actions during an assembly which did not involve any violence." Further, in the necessity assessment: "in May 2003 the Lithuanian authorities issued the farmers with permits to hold peaceful assemblies in selected areas" and "on 21 May 2003 the farmers' peaceful demonstration dispersed and resulted in major disruptions of traffic on three main roads." The existence of an assembly is explicitly confirmed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses participation. In the interference section: "the applicants' conviction for their participation in the gathering at issue amounted to an interference with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The applicants' roles as organizers and participants are discussed throughout. This is explicitly addressed.

**iii) Peaceful nature:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. In the interference section: "the applicants were convicted of an offence in connection to their actions during an assembly which did not involve any violence." In the necessity section: "the element of violence was clearly absent in the instant case." The Court also notes: "the farmers who held a demonstration on Panevėžys-Pasvalys-Riga highway not only allowed through passenger vehicles and vehicles that carried dangerous substances, but also that vehicles which carried goods and cars had been allowed to go through ten at a time on each side of the road." The peaceful nature is explicitly confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference. In section B. Merits > 2(b): "the Court finds that the applicants' conviction for their participation in the gathering at issue amounted to an interference with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court notes that while the applicants were permitted to exercise their right to peaceful assembly, their criminal conviction constituted the interference. This is explicitly discussed and concluded affirmatively.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not definitively resolve the "prescribed by law" question. Instead, it assumes it for the sake of argument: "even assuming that such interference was therefore 'prescribed by law' and in pursuit of legitimate aims... the Court considers that it was not proportionate." The Court does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards sub-elements. The applicants raised arguments about the lack of clarity of "serious breach of public order" under Article 283 § 1 of the Criminal Code, and the Government responded by citing Galstyan and arguing sufficient precision. However, the Court bypasses this question by assuming it in the Government's favor and proceeding directly to proportionality.

**i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly resolved — assumed in Government's favor.
**ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.
**iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court also assumes legitimate aim without definitively resolving it: "even assuming that such interference was therefore 'prescribed by law' and in pursuit of legitimate aims, namely the 'prevention of disorder' and 'the protection of the rights and freedoms of others' so as to satisfy the requirements of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention in that respect, for the reasons described below, the Court considers that it was not proportionate." The aims assumed (but not definitively found) are prevention of disorder and protection of the rights and freedoms of others. This is explicitly discussed but not conclusively determined — the Court assumes it arguendo.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary (i.e., not proportionate). Key passages:

- "the right to freedom of assembly is a fundamental right in a democratic society and, like the right to freedom of expression, is one of the foundations of such a society. Thus, it should not be interpreted restrictively."
- "any demonstration in a public place inevitably causes a certain level of disruption to ordinary life, including disruption of traffic, and that it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings if the freedom of assembly guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention is not to be deprived of all substance."
- "the element of violence was clearly absent in the instant case."
- The Court compares the applicants' treatment with that of farmer A.D., who received only an administrative fine of LTL 40 (approximately EUR 12) for similar conduct, while the applicants faced criminal conviction and custodial sentences.
- "the Court finds that the applicants' conviction for the criminal offence was not a necessary and proportionate measure in order to achieve the legitimate aims pursued."

The necessity element is explicitly discussed and concluded negatively (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court briefly mentions the positive obligation framework in passing: "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to lawful demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey)." However, this is cited as general context rather than as a distinct positive obligation analysis. The Court does not separately analyze whether the government failed a positive obligation — the case is framed as a negative obligation case (unjustified interference). The positive obligation is mentioned but not the focus of the Court's reasoning, and no conclusion is drawn specifically on this element.
