**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of the Court's assessment:

- "Having regard to these factors, and notwithstanding the margin of appreciation afforded to the State in this area, the Court considers that the Austrian authorities failed to strike a fair balance between the competing interests."
- "Consequently, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 exists, but it is implied throughout the entire reasoning. The Court refers to "the prohibition of an assembly he had intended to hold" and treats the planned gathering as falling within Article 11 without question. The assembly is treated as a counter-demonstration/protest meeting. This is implied by the Court's direct engagement with the interference and necessity questions.

**ii) Participation:** The applicant was the organiser of the assembly he intended to hold. The Court notes "the applicant, being a member of parliament, essentially wished to protest against the gathering of Comradeship IV." His participation/organisation is implied throughout but not separately discussed as a distinct sub-element.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the intended assembly: "They envisaged peaceful and silent means of expressing their opinion, namely the carrying of commemorative messages, and had explicitly ruled out the use of chanting or banners. Thus, the intended assembly in itself could not have hurt the feelings of cemetery-goers." This confirms the assembly was peaceful. The Court also notes that "it was not alleged that any violent incidents had occurred on previous occasions." The right invoked is explicitly called "the right to freedom of peaceful assembly" throughout.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly states: "It is common ground that the prohibition in issue constituted an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly which was prescribed by law, namely by section 6 of the Assembly Act, and served legitimate aims recognised in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." The interference is thus explicitly confirmed and undisputed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly states that the interference "was prescribed by law, namely by section 6 of the Assembly Act." This is treated as common ground between the parties. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not separately discussed. The Government submitted that "the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was prescribed by law, namely by section 6 of the Assembly Act," and the Court accepted this without further analysis. The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are not discussed individually — the Court simply accepts the 'prescribed by law' requirement as satisfied without breaking it down.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states: "It is common ground that the prohibition in issue constituted an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly which was prescribed by law, namely by section 6 of the Assembly Act, and served legitimate aims recognised in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, namely the 'prevention of disorder' and 'the protection of the rights and freedoms of others'." The aims found are: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights and freedoms of others. The Government had also mentioned public order and protection of Article 9 rights (freedom of religion) of cemetery-goers, which falls under 'rights of others.'

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central and most extensively discussed element. The Court explicitly states: "The parties' submissions concentrate on the question whether the interference was also 'necessary in a democratic society' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2. The Court reiterates that the notion of necessity implies that the interference corresponds to a pressing social need and, in particular, that it is proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued."

The Court finds that the prohibition was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages include:
- "the unconditional prohibition of a counter-demonstration is a very far-reaching measure which would require particular justification"
- "the Court notes a number of factors which indicate that the prohibition in issue was disproportionate to the aim pursued"
- "the assembly was in no way directed against the cemetery-goers' beliefs or the manifestation of them"
- "the applicant expected only a small number of participants. They envisaged peaceful and silent means of expressing their opinion"
- "it was not alleged that any violent incidents had occurred on previous occasions"
- "the Court is not convinced by the Government's argument that allowing both meetings while taking preventive measures... was not a viable alternative"
- "the domestic authorities imposed an unconditional prohibition on the applicant's assembly... they gave too little weight to the applicant's interest in holding the intended assembly... while giving too much weight to the interest of cemetery-goers"
- "the Austrian authorities failed to strike a fair balance between the competing interests"

The conclusion on necessity is: NO, the interference was not necessary in a democratic society (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intervention with the legitimate aim).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly discusses positive obligations under Article 11: "As regards the right to freedom of peaceful assembly as guaranteed by Article 11, the Court reiterates that it comprises negative and positive obligations on the part of the Contracting State... States may be required under Article 11 to take positive measures in order to protect a lawful demonstration against counter-demonstrations (see Plattform 'Ärzte für das Leben', cited above, § 34)."

The Constitutional Court had relied on a positive obligation under Article 9 to justify the prohibition. The ECtHR addresses this indirectly by finding that the prohibition was disproportionate and that allowing both meetings with police presence was a viable alternative. The Court states: "the Court is not convinced by the Government's argument that allowing both meetings while taking preventive measures, such as ensuring police presence in order to keep the two assemblies apart, was not a viable alternative which would have preserved the applicant's right to freedom of assembly while at the same time offering a sufficient degree of protection as regards the rights of the cemetery's visitors."

The positive obligation is discussed explicitly in the abstract (the State may need to protect demonstrations), but the Court's conclusion is that the State did not need to prohibit the assembly to fulfil its positive obligations — rather, less restrictive measures (police presence) would have sufficed. The positive obligation discussion does not lead to a separate finding of violation; the violation is found under the necessity/proportionality analysis. The conclusion on positive obligation is nuanced: the Court acknowledges positive obligations exist but finds they did not require the prohibition imposed here — so the government's invocation of positive obligations to justify the ban was not accepted.
