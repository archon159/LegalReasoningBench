**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has NOT been violated. The final paragraph states: "Consequently, there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention." This is preceded by the holding: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that an assembly existed by referring to "the applicant and others clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in the Ayios Kassianos area of Nicosia" and by proceeding to analyze Article 11. The Government of Cyprus also referred to it as "a lawful demonstration." This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court states "the applicant and others clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating" and that "some of the demonstrators, including the applicant, were arrested." The applicant's participation is implied by the Court's analysis, not separately discussed as a sub-element.

**(iii) Peaceful character:** The Government argued the demonstration was "clearly outside the scope of Article 11" due to its violent character. The Court does not explicitly rule on whether the assembly was peaceful for scoping purposes, but it does note the violent character of the demonstration in the context of necessity/proportionality. The Court states: "the intervention of the Turkish and/or Turkish-Cypriot forces was not due to the political nature of the demonstration but was provoked by its violent character and by the violation of the 'TRNC' borders by some of the demonstrators." The Court also references the Commission's finding that "the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent." However, the Court does not exclude the assembly from the scope of Article 11 on grounds of violence — it proceeds to analyze interference, prescribed by law, legitimate aim, and necessity. The peaceful character sub-element is thus discussed implicitly (the Court proceeds despite the violent character, suggesting it does not exclude the assembly from scope, but the violence is addressed under necessity).

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: "The Court notes that the applicant and others clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in the Ayios Kassianos area of Nicosia. The demonstration was dispersed and some of the demonstrators, including the applicant, were arrested. Under these circumstances, the Court considers that there has been an interference with the applicant's right of assembly (see Protopapa, cited above, § 104)." Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed: "This interference had a legal basis, namely sections 70 and 71 of the Cypriot Criminal Code (see paragraphs 28-29 above) and section 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law (see paragraph 32 above), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention. In this respect, the Court recalls its finding that when, as in the Foka case, an act of the 'TRNC' authorities was in compliance with laws in force within the territory of northern Cyprus, it should in principle be regarded as having a legal basis in domestic law for the purposes of the Convention." Conclusion: yes, prescribed by law.

The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are NOT explicitly discussed. The Court simply concludes the interference was "prescribed by law" without separately analyzing these three sub-elements. They are not_discussed as separate sub-elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: "The Government submitted that the interference pursued legitimate aims, including the protection of national security and/or public safety and the prevention of disorder and crime." The Court then proceeds to analyze the facts in a way that implicitly accepts these aims, noting the violent character of the demonstration and the violation of borders. The Court states: "interferences with the right guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention are in principle justified for the prevention of disorder or crime and for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others where, as in the instant case, demonstrators engage in acts of violence." The Court does not separately and explicitly conclude "yes, a legitimate aim was pursued" but proceeds directly to necessity, implying acceptance of the legitimate aims. The aims referenced are national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, and prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed at length. The Court references fundamental principles, the duty of authorities to take appropriate measures, the wide margin of appreciation, and the violent character of the demonstration. Key passage: "In these conditions and having regard to the wide margin of appreciation left to the States in this sphere (see Plattform 'Ärzte für das Leben', cited above, § 34), the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2." Conclusion: the interference was necessary (not disproportionate), so no violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court mentions the positive obligation in passing: "It is clear from this case-law that the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey, no. 74552/01, § 35, 5 December 2006). However, they cannot guarantee this absolutely and they have a wide discretion in the choice of the means to be used." This is mentioned as a general principle but the Court does not analyze whether the defendant government violated a positive obligation — the case is framed as a negative obligation (interference) case. The positive obligation is mentioned but not applied as a separate analytical framework. Conclusion: mentioned but not concluded upon as a separate obligation; the Court does not find a violation of any positive obligation.
