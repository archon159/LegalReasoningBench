**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final paragraph of the section:

"These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant."

Additionally, the penultimate paragraph states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements and were moreover not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope (assembly exists, participation, peaceful)**

The scope elements are not explicitly discussed in this reasoning section. However, they are implied by the Court's explicit discussion of interference and the merits. The Court refers to "the applicants complained principally of the restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events" and discusses "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly," which logically presupposes that there were assemblies, that the applicants participated (or attempted to), and that the assemblies were peaceful. These are implied rather than explicitly addressed.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements." The word "interferences" is used directly, confirming that the Court found governmental interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly. The conclusion is affirmative (yes, there was interference).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" / "quality of law" requirement is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements." This is a direct finding that the prescribed-by-law requirement was NOT satisfied. The Court does not separately and explicitly discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse in this excerpt — it simply refers to the leading case of Lashmankin and Others v. Russia and adopts its reasoning by reference. The sub-elements are therefore implied (since the overall "quality of law" finding is made, the sub-elements are logically engaged), but not individually spelled out in this text. The overall conclusion on prescribed by law is negative (the legal provisions did not meet the quality of law requirements).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Legitimate aim is not explicitly discussed in this reasoning section. The Court jumps from finding that the "quality of law" requirement was not met directly to finding a violation, without separately addressing whether a legitimate aim was pursued. This is not_discussed in terms of explicit treatment, and given the Court found the prescribed-by-law test failed, it did not need to reach legitimate aim. No aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Necessity is explicitly discussed. The Court states the interferences were "moreover not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is a direct finding that the necessity requirement was NOT satisfied. The conclusion is negative (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

Positive obligations are not discussed anywhere in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely as one of negative obligations (restrictions imposed by authorities). This element is not_discussed.
