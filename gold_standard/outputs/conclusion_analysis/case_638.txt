**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has NOT been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "There has accordingly been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention read in the light of Article 10." This conclusion follows from the Court's finding that both the removal of the applicants from the acting Rector's office and the imposition of administrative fines were proportionate to the legitimate aims pursued and necessary in a democratic society.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses whether the events constitute an assembly within the meaning of Article 11. In the section on interference, the Court notes: "The events which developed subsequent to their unauthorised entry to the Rector's office do not represent, in the Court's view, a standard situation of a 'peaceful assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention." However, the Court ultimately concludes that the protest, viewed as a whole, falls within the scope of Article 11: "The Court thus does not consider that the applicants' protest on 3 July 2006, viewed as a whole, was of such a nature and degree as to exclude them from the scope of protection under Article 11 of the Convention, read in the light of Article 10." The assembly element is thus explicitly discussed and answered affirmatively.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court does not separately discuss participation as a distinct sub-element. However, it is implied throughout the analysis — the applicants are described as having gathered in the lecture hall, moved to the Rector's office, and been removed by police. The Court states: "The Court, hence, concludes that the applicants were entitled in the course of their protest at the University to invoke the guarantees of Article 11 of the Convention." Participation is implied rather than explicitly addressed as a separate question.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. It notes: "Article 11 of the Convention only protects the right to 'peaceful assembly', a notion which does not cover a demonstration where the organisers and participants have violent intentions." Applying this to the facts: "The Court notes that the applicants were not held responsible for using violence. While the events at issue happened in a situation of tension, the applicants' conduct was not established to have been of a violent nature." The Court thus concludes the assembly was sufficiently peaceful to fall within Article 11's protection, though it acknowledges the conduct was not at the "core" of the freedom.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and finds interference. It states: "The Court, hence, concludes that the applicants were entitled in the course of their protest at the University to invoke the guarantees of Article 11 of the Convention. It further notes that the applicants were removed by police from the office of the acting Rector of the University. Subsequently, they were charged and found responsible for several administrative offences in connection with what had happened in the University. It thus concludes that their removal and administrative responsibility constituted an interference with their right to freedom of assembly (see Açık and Others, cited above, § 40)." The Government had argued there was no interference, but the Court rejected this.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the lawfulness requirement, though it does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards as distinct sub-elements.

**Overall prescribed by law:** The Court finds the interference was prescribed by law. Regarding the removal: "The Court accepts that the impugned interference had a basis in domestic law. It notes that the police acted at the request of the acting Rector of the University... It therefore finds that the requirement of lawfulness is satisfied." Regarding the administrative sanctions: "the Court considers that the interference by way of imposing administrative sanctions in the current case was prescribed by law."

**i) Foreseeability:** The Court touches on foreseeability in the context of Article 166 of the CAO: "the Court is of the opinion that the interpretation of this provision given by the domestic courts in the present case was not arbitrary, and that the applicants could have foreseen, to a degree reasonable in the circumstances, that their actions entailing disruption to the functioning of the University, could have been deemed to amount to a minor breach of public order attracting the application of Article 166 of the CAO." This is an explicit discussion of foreseeability, concluding it was satisfied.

**ii) Accessibility:** The Court does not explicitly discuss accessibility of the legal provisions. It is implied by the finding that the interference was prescribed by law.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse:** The Court does not explicitly discuss safeguards against abuse. This sub-element is not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim for both instances of interference.

Regarding the removal: "the Court accepts the Government's argument that given the circumstances of the current case, the impugned interference pursued the legitimate aims of preventing public disorder and protecting the rights of others."

Regarding the administrative sanctions: "the legitimate aim of the interference was the prevention of disruption to public order and to the interests of others."

The aims found are: prevention of disorder (public disorder) and protection of the rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity/proportionality for both instances of interference.

Regarding the removal: "the Court considers that the removal of the applicants from the acting Rector's office in order to achieve the legitimate aims pursued was not disproportionate." The Court notes the applicants were allowed to protest for hours, no physical force was used, police negotiated for over an hour, and after removal they were allowed to continue protesting on University premises.

Regarding the administrative sanctions: "the Court concludes that the interference with the applicants' rights under Article 11 of the Convention read through the prism of Article 10 was proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued and necessary in a democratic society." The Court notes the fines were only GEL 100, no arrests were made, and the applicants had been allowed to protest for months.

The Court concludes necessity was satisfied in both instances — i.e., the interference was NOT disproportionate, meaning the government DID adequately balance the intervention with the legitimate aim. The conclusion on necessity is therefore "yes" (the interference was necessary/proportionate), supporting the finding of no violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the government under Article 11 in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference with the right to assembly). This element is not discussed.
