**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *Assembly exists*: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *Participation*: Implied — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- *Peaceful*: Implied — the Court proceeds to find a violation under Article 11 without excluding the applicants from its protection, which would have been required had the assemblies been non-peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming this element is established.

Conclusion: yes, explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. These sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim pursued by the interference. No aims from Article 11(2) are mentioned. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the interference was not justified), leading to the violation.

Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary), explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.

**Summary of implied elements**: The scope elements (assembly exists, participation, peaceful) and the interference element are implied by the Court's framing of the case and its reference to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly." The prescribed-by-law and legitimate aim elements are not discussed at all — the Court proceeds directly to necessity, relying on its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) to dispose of the case efficiently under a repetitive-case procedure.
