**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of section B.2(c):

"In view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicant, the Court concludes that his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and protecting the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society."

Followed by: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Frumkin, cited above, §§ 99 and 137, and Yaroslav Belousov, cited above, §§ 168-71)." The existence of an assembly is confirmed by reference to prior case law.

**ii) Participation:** Explicitly discussed. The Court addresses the applicant's personal participation: "As regards the applicant personally, it does not transpire from the description of his offences that he was among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character. Accordingly, he enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention." This confirms the applicant participated in the assembly and was covered by Article 11.

**iii) Peaceful character:** Explicitly discussed. The Court notes that the applicant was not among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression, and that "he enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court also notes later that "the applicant's overall conduct degenerated from peaceful to violent, it was sporadic in nature and appeared to be a spontaneous reaction to the force used by the police against the protestors, the majority of whom were peaceful." The assembly is treated as initially peaceful and the applicant as a peaceful participant for purposes of Article 11 protection.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court also finds that the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for acts he committed in the course of the dispersal of the demonstration constituted an interference with the exercise of the freedom of assembly."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed (overall conclusion). The Court states: "The parties made essentially the same submissions on the lawfulness of the interference as in Yaroslav Belousov (cited above, § 173). The Court sees no reason to reach a different conclusion in this case (ibid., §§ 174-75). It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful..." The Court concludes the interference was prescribed by law, but does so by reference to the Yaroslav Belousov judgment without separately elaborating on foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards sub-elements in this text. The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are not individually discussed in the excerpted text — the Court simply adopts the prior case's conclusion.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful, and pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and protecting the rights and freedoms of others." The aims found are: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court examines in detail whether the criminal conviction and sentence were necessary. Key passages include:
- "the Court notes that he was found guilty of two criminal offences..."
- "the domestic courts did not examine the applicant's allegation that the police had contributed to the onset of violence and had used excessive force."
- "the criminal file did not disclose any pre-existing intention by the applicant to participate in violent acts"
- "the Court does not consider that the risks referred to by the Government...had any personal relation to him"
- "the reasons advanced by the Government could not justify a three-year prison sentence for participation in mass disorder under Article 212 of the Criminal Code"
- "there was no 'pressing social need' to give the applicant a sentence of that duration"
- "the applicant's criminal conviction, and especially the severity of his sentence, must have had the effect of discouraging him and other opposition supporters...from attending demonstrations"
- Final conclusion: "his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims...and...was therefore not necessary in a democratic society."

The necessity test is answered negatively — the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly addresses the positive obligation issue in the Admissibility section, but declines to assess it as a separate issue: "the Court is not required to assess the authorities' alleged failure to discharge their positive obligation in respect of the conduct of the demonstration at Bolotnaya Square as a separate issue under Article 11 of the Convention." However, the Court does consider the authorities' role in the onset of violence as part of the necessity analysis, noting that domestic courts failed to examine the applicant's allegation that police contributed to the onset of violence. The positive obligation is discussed but the Court explicitly declines to rule on it as a standalone issue, treating it as subsumed within the necessity/interference analysis. No separate violation of a positive obligation is found.
