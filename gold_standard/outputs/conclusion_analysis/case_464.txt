**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final conclusion is stated in the last two paragraphs of section (c): 'In view of these findings, the Court concludes that the interference with the applicant's right to peaceful assembly could not be justified under the requirements of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention (see paragraph 75 above). There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In the facts section, it establishes: 'From about 6 p.m. to 6.30 p.m. the applicant took part in an authorised and peaceful public demonstration at Triumfalnaya Square.' This is treated as a well-established fact. The assembly is explicitly identified as an authorised political rally/demonstration.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation. It finds as established fact that 'the applicant took part in an authorised and peaceful public demonstration at Triumfalnaya Square' and that 'During the meeting the applicant addressed the participants with a speech expressing strong political views.' His participation is not in dispute.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. It describes it as 'an authorised and peaceful public demonstration.' Furthermore, the Court's factual findings establish that the applicant did not engage in any violent or disruptive conduct — he merely shouted that Article 31 of the Constitution guaranteed freedom of assembly and did not resist arrest. The assembly itself is described as peaceful throughout.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: 'The Court considers that the applicant's arrest and detention constituted an interference with his right to peaceful assembly, as did the ensuing administrative charges brought against him. The Court observes, moreover, that the Government did not dispute the existence of the interference with the right to peaceful assembly in the present case.' This is explicit and uncontested.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court addresses the lawfulness question but does so in a merged analysis with necessity. It states: 'It considers that in this case the questions of lawfulness and of the existence of a legitimate aim are indissociable from the question whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society", and it considers it unnecessary to examine them separately.' Nevertheless, the Court does make substantive findings on lawfulness: 'The Court notes that the legal basis for the applicant's arrest and the subsequent administrative charges brought against him was Article 19.3 of the Code of Administrative Offences... However, the reference to this provision was contested by the applicant on the grounds that the underlying events had not, in fact, taken place, and the Court has upheld this view... The Court therefore concludes that the arrest and the ensuing administrative liability were imposed on the applicant without any connection with the intended purpose of the legal provision for disobeying lawful orders of the police. The interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly on such a legal basis could only be characterised as arbitrary and unlawful.'

The Court's finding of unlawfulness is based on the arbitrary application of the legal provision rather than on the provision's lack of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards per se. The Court does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards as sub-elements. The conclusion is that the interference was NOT prescribed by law (it was arbitrary and unlawful), but this is reached through the factual finding that the legal basis was misapplied, not through a textual analysis of the provision's qualities.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element. The Court's finding of unlawfulness is based on arbitrary application, not on the text of the law being unforeseeable. Implied 'no' conclusion follows from the overall finding of unlawfulness/arbitrariness.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed as a separate sub-element, though the arbitrary application finding implicitly suggests inadequate safeguards against abuse.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court merges the legitimate aim analysis with necessity and lawfulness: 'It considers that in this case the questions of lawfulness and of the existence of a legitimate aim are indissociable from the question whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society", and it considers it unnecessary to examine them separately.' The Government had claimed the aim of 'maintaining public order.' The Court does not separately confirm or deny a legitimate aim, but given its finding that the interference was arbitrary and unjustified, it implicitly finds no legitimate aim was actually served. The aims are not separately confirmed. The Government's submission referenced 'maintaining public order' (which maps to prevention of disorder/public safety), but the Court does not endorse this.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses necessity, merged with lawfulness and legitimate aim. It finds: 'the applicant's arrest and the charges against him had not been justified by a pressing social need.' It also notes the chilling effect: 'The administrative proceedings against the applicant and his ensuing detention had the effect of discouraging him from participating in protest rallies or indeed from engaging actively in opposition politics. Undoubtedly, those measures had a serious potential also to deter other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations and, more generally, from participating in open political debate.' The Court concludes the interference was not necessary.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference case. No positive obligation is spelled out or analyzed.
