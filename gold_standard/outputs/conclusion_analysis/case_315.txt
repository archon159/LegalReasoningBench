**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final paragraphs of the reasoning state: "Bearing in mind the above circumstances, the Court concludes that the interference did not correspond to a pressing social need and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." This conclusion is then repeated: "Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied by the entire framing of the case. The Court refers to "the decision to reject Hyde Park's application to hold a demonstration between 1 and 31 August 2006" and treats the matter as falling within Article 11 without question. The existence of an assembly (a planned protest demonstration) is implied.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicants participated or attempted to participate. However, it is implied — the applicants are described as having applied to hold the demonstration, and the rejection of that application is treated as an interference with their right. Participation (or attempted participation) is implied.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court does not explicitly address whether the assembly was peaceful as a threshold matter. However, it notes: "there was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities." This implies the assembly was peaceful, though the statement is made in the context of the necessity analysis rather than as a scope determination.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "It is common ground between the parties, and the Court agrees, that the decision to reject Hyde Park's application to hold a demonstration between 1 and 31 August 2006 amounted to 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11." The Government also accepted that there had been an interference. Conclusion: yes, explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element but does not reach a definitive conclusion on it, instead merging it with the necessity analysis: "In so far as the lawfulness of the interference is concerned, the Court notes that under section 14 of the Assemblies Act the Chişinău Municipality was obliged to give reasons in writing for rejecting Hyde Park's application... According to section 12(6) of the Assemblies Act, an application could be rejected only if the Municipality was in possession of evidence that the provisions of sections 6 and 7 would be breached with serious consequences for society. The Municipality's decision appears not to have been based on any of the reasons provided for in sections 6 and 7 of the Assemblies Act. This in itself might be a sufficient basis for the conclusion that the impugned measures were not 'prescribed by law'. However, in the present case, the Court considers that the issue of compliance with the law is indissociable from the question as to whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society'. It will therefore examine this issue below."

The Court thus explicitly raises the prescribed-by-law question but declines to resolve it separately, folding it into the necessity analysis. The overall conclusion on prescribed by law is therefore left unresolved (null) as a standalone finding.

**(i) Foreseeable:** The Court does not separately discuss foreseeability. The discussion of the Assemblies Act and its requirements touches on whether the Municipality acted within the legal framework, but foreseeability as a sub-element is not explicitly addressed. Not discussed as a distinct sub-element.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed as a distinct sub-element, though the Court's reference to the Assemblies Act's requirements (sections 6, 7, 12(6), 14) implicitly touches on the existence of legal safeguards.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states it does not need to decide this point: "The parties also disagreed as to whether the interference served a legitimate aim. The Court, for the reasons set out below, does not consider it necessary to decide this point either." No legitimate aim is identified. Conclusion: null (not decided), explicitly discussed but left unresolved.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court recalls the hallmarks of a democratic society (pluralism, tolerance, broadmindedness), the proportionality standard, and then applies it: "the Municipality rejected the application to hold the protest demonstration planned for 1-31 August 2006 on the ground that, in its opinion, one day of protest was sufficient... the Municipality's reasons cannot be considered relevant and sufficient within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention... there was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities. Therefore, the Court can only conclude that the Municipality's refusal to authorise the demonstration did not respond to a pressing social need." Conclusion: the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society (i.e., the necessity requirement was not satisfied by the government), explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the state. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference case. Not discussed.
