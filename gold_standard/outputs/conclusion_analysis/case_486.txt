**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without questioning the peaceful nature of the assemblies). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze whether there was an interference in a dedicated section, but it clearly implies that there was one. The opening paragraph states that the applicants complained of "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in its necessity analysis, confirming that interference is treated as established. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate analytical step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss the "prescribed by law" requirement at all — neither the foreseeability, accessibility, nor safeguards sub-elements. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, referencing leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) where similar issues were found to violate Article 11. No conclusion on "prescribed by law" can be drawn from this text.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov), finding no fact or argument capable of leading to a different conclusion. The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent.
