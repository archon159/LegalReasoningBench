**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final conclusion is stated in the last two paragraphs of the merits section:

"In the light of the foregoing, the Court considers that the prosecution of the applicants and the imposition of administrative fines for their participation in a peaceful demonstration were disproportionate and not necessary for maintaining public order within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

And: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In the section on interference, it states: "all the applicants participated in the meetings" and describes the gatherings as demonstrations aimed at drawing attention to the transfer of SSK hospitals to the Ministry of Health. In the necessity section: "the applicants gathered to demonstrate in an area that had been prohibited by the relevant authorities" and "The participants held a peaceful demonstration." The existence of an assembly is explicitly confirmed.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes participation: "all the applicants participated in the meetings and two of the three applicants read out the press statements." This is explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly in the necessity section: "The participants held a peaceful demonstration and did not cause any disruptions in the entrance of the hospitals; they also allowed patients to enter the hospitals. Moreover, there is no evidence to suggest that the demonstrators either presented a danger to public order or engaged in acts of violence." Also: "the three applicants were prosecuted and subsequently sentenced to pay administrative fines on account of the mere fact of their participation in a peaceful demonstration." Explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference: "the Court considers that the applicants the imposition of fines on the applicants constituted an interference with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly." It also notes the chilling effect: "the prosecution and conviction of the applicants could have had a chilling effect and discouraged them from taking part in other similar meetings." Explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the 'prescribed by law' requirement but declines to reach a final conclusion on it, choosing instead to resolve the case on necessity grounds. The Court states: "the Court considers that it is not required to reach a final conclusion on the lawfulness issue." The Court does set out the standard: the law must be "accessible to the person concerned, who must moreover be able to foresee its consequences" and "afford adequate legal protection against arbitrariness."

**(i) Foreseeable:** The Court raises the foreseeability requirement explicitly: "The law must thus be adequately accessible and foreseeable, that is, formulated with sufficient precision to enable the individual – if need be with appropriate advice – to regulate his conduct." However, no final conclusion is reached. The applicants argued the circulars were not published in the Official Gazette. Discussed explicitly but left unresolved (null).

**(ii) Accessible:** The Court raises accessibility explicitly alongside foreseeability: "The law must thus be adequately accessible and foreseeable." The applicants argued the circulars had not been published in the Official Gazette. Discussed explicitly but left unresolved (null).

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse:** The Court mentions the requirement that the law "afford adequate legal protection against arbitrariness and accordingly indicate with sufficient clarity the scope of discretion conferred on the competent authorities and the manner of its exercise" (citing Malone and Rotaru). This touches on safeguards. However, no final conclusion is reached. Discussed explicitly but left unresolved (null).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "As concerns the issue of whether the impugned measure had a legitimate aim, the Government submitted that the interference pursued the legitimate aim of maintaining public order, public safety and public health. The applicants maintained their allegations. The Court accepts that the measure pursued the legitimate aims cited by the Government." The aims accepted are public order (prevention of disorder), public safety, and public health (protection of health). Explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court extensively discusses necessity. Key passages include:
- "The proportionality principle demands that a balance be struck between the requirements of the purposes listed in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention and those of freedom of peaceful assembly."
- "The Court is concerned by the fact that the three applicants were prosecuted and subsequently sentenced to pay administrative fines on account of the mere fact of their participation in a peaceful demonstration."
- "The Court, however, notes that it was not established in the reasoning of the domestic courts' judgments whether the domestic courts had engaged with the proportionality of the interference and the balancing of rights of the applicants on account of freedom of assembly. The Court, therefore, takes the view that the reasons indicated by the domestic courts were not relevant or sufficient, and that they were not proportionate to the legitimate aims pursued."
- "the prosecution of the applicants and the imposition of administrative fines for their participation in a peaceful demonstration were disproportionate and not necessary for maintaining public order within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11."

The Court concludes that necessity was NOT satisfied. Explicitly discussed, conclusion: no (not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly frame any positive obligation in this case. It does mention in the general principles section that "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to lawful demonstrations in order to ensure that they are conducted peacefully" (citing Oya Ataman), but this is cited as general background and not as the basis for a positive obligation finding against the respondent state. The case is resolved entirely on the negative obligation (interference) side. Not discussed as a distinct element.
