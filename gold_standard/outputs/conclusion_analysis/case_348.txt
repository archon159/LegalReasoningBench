**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers and/or participants of public assemblies" and describes "the dispersal of these assemblies" — this implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was within the scope of Article 11 (i.e., peaceful enough to attract protection). The Court's willingness to proceed to the interference and necessity analysis logically presupposes that scope is satisfied. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the description of applicants as "organisers and/or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity, which logically requires the assembly to have been peaceful. No suggestion of violence is made.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies, and their conviction for administrative offences."

Furthermore, the Court explicitly uses the word "interferences" in the necessity analysis: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This confirms that interference is explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). These sub-elements are not discussed, even by implication, in the text provided.

- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not_discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not_discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not_discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), leading to the violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent from the text.
