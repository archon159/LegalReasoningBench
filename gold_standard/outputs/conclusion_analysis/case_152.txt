**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court finds a violation of Article 11 in two separate assessments:

(a) Regarding the first applicant in his first application (arrest/conviction before the 11 March 2011 demonstration): "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." (Section B.3.(a), final paragraph)

(b) Regarding the second applicant and the first applicant in his second application (dispersal of demonstrations and arrests/convictions): "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." (Section B.3.(b), final paragraph)

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied throughout. The entire analysis presupposes that demonstrations took place (11 March 2011, 20 October 2012, 17 November 2012). The Court refers to "the demonstration of 11 March 2011," "the dispersal of the demonstrations," and "opposition protests" throughout. This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a doctrinal step.

*Participation:* The Court implies participation. For the first applicant in the first application, it notes he "had participated in various protests held by the opposition" and "had promoted public participation in the demonstration of 11 March 2011." For the second applicant and first applicant in the second application, the complaints concern "the dispersal of the demonstrations in which they had participated." Participation is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a doctrinal step.

*Peaceful:* For the second applicant and first applicant in the second application, the Court explicitly discusses the peaceful nature of the assemblies, noting (by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov) "there had been a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration, which the organisers had intended to be peaceful and which had been conducted in a peaceful manner." The applicants also "argued that the authorities had not taken into consideration the fact that all three demonstrations had been intended to be peaceful and had been held in a peaceful manner." This is explicitly discussed for the second set of complaints. For the first applicant's first application, the peaceful nature is implied (the arrest occurred before the demonstration, and the Court treats it as an assembly-related interference).

**(b) Interference**

For the first applicant in his first application: The Court explicitly finds interference. It states: "the proceedings against the applicant in the present case and the ensuing administrative detention were arbitrary and unlawful measures aimed at punishing the applicant for his political activity, including his previous participation in opposition protests, and preventing him from participating in or promoting such protests." The Government had argued "there had been no interference with the applicant's rights under Article 11 of the Convention since he had been arrested and convicted for an administrative offence in circumstances unrelated to any public assembly" — but the Court rejects this by finding a violation. The Court also notes: "The applicant's arrest and administrative detention could not but have had the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies."

For the second applicant and first applicant in the second application: Interference is explicitly implied by the finding that "both applicants' right to freedom of assembly was breached" due to "the dispersal of the demonstration and his arrest and conviction" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

For the second applicant and first applicant in the second application, the Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" element by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov: "there were serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being arbitrarily banned or dispersed." This addresses foreseeability and safeguards against abuse. The Court also notes "doubts about the credibility of the formal grounds, namely Article 310.1 of the CAO, relied on by the authorities." The applicants themselves argued that "the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly did not comply with the principles of foreseeability and precision" and that "the system of prior authorisation... allowed for arbitrary interference with freedom of assembly."

Foreseeability: Explicitly discussed — "serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies" — conclusion is negative (not foreseeable/precise enough).

Accessibility: Not explicitly discussed.

Safeguards: Explicitly discussed — "the possibility of public assemblies being arbitrarily banned or dispersed" and "no acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration had itself been protected by Article 11" — conclusion is negative (insufficient safeguards).

For the first applicant in his first application, the "prescribed by law" element is not explicitly discussed as a separate doctrinal step. The Court focuses on the arbitrary and unlawful nature of the proceedings, which implies the interference was not lawful, but does not analyze the legal basis in doctrinal terms.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

For the second applicant and first applicant in the second application: The Government argued the dispersal "pursued the aim of protecting the rights of others as the square in the centre of Baku where the demonstrations had been held had been one of the main leisure spaces for citizens." The Court does not explicitly accept this aim; rather, it finds a violation by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov without separately endorsing any legitimate aim. The legitimate aim is mentioned in the parties' submissions but not resolved affirmatively by the Court.

For the first applicant in his first application: No legitimate aim is discussed.

Overall, legitimate aim is mentioned (rights of others) in submissions but not explicitly accepted or resolved by the Court as a doctrinal finding.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

For the second applicant and first applicant in the second application: The Court explicitly addresses necessity by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov: "there had been a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration" and "there had been no acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration had itself been protected by Article 11." The conclusion is that the interference was not necessary.

For the first applicant in his first application: The Court addresses proportionality/necessity implicitly by finding the measures were "arbitrary and unlawful" and had a "chilling effect" — but does not use the formal necessity framework explicitly.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (interference with the right). Positive obligations are not discussed.

**Summary of findings across both sets of complaints:**

The case involves two distinct sets of complaints analyzed separately. For the structured results, I will treat the overall case:
- Scope (assembly exists): implied yes
- Scope (participation): implied yes
- Scope (peaceful): explicitly discussed, yes
- Interference: explicitly discussed, yes
- Prescribed by law (overall): explicitly discussed (for second set), conclusion no (concerns about foreseeability/safeguards)
- Prescribed by law foreseeable: explicitly discussed, no
- Prescribed by law accessible: not discussed
- Prescribed by law safeguards: explicitly discussed, no
- Legitimate aim: mentioned in submissions, not resolved by Court (null)
- Necessity: explicitly discussed (for second set), no
- Positive obligation: not discussed
