**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated in it, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity, which would be unnecessary if the assembly were not peaceful). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly stated.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: The text states the applicants were "participants of public assemblies" — implied affirmative.
- **scope_participation**: The text states the applicants were "participants of public assemblies" — implied affirmative.
- **scope_peaceful**: Not explicitly discussed, but implied by the Court's proceeding to analyze the interference and necessity without excluding the assembly from protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The text states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the opening paragraph describes the measures: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." The Court thus explicitly finds that there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. Legitimate aim is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is negative (the interference was NOT necessary), which supports the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed at all.
