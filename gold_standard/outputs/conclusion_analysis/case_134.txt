**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly," which logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied — the Court refers to "public assemblies" without questioning their existence.
- **scope_participation**: Implied — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court applies Article 11 protection without any suggestion that the assemblies were non-peaceful; the leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The nature of the interference is also described at the outset: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its leading case findings. These sub-elements are not discussed, even impliedly, in the text provided.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and the general principles from Kudrevičius, Oya Ataman, and Hyde Park.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent.
