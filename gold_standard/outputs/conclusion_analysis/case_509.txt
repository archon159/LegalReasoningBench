**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The scope is not explicitly discussed in the reasoning text. However, it is implied by the Court's analysis. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and discusses "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly." This implies that: (i) an assembly existed (the Court refers to "public assemblies"); (ii) the applicants participated (they are described as "organisers or participants"); and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (the Court proceeds to analyze the interference under Article 11, which only protects peaceful assemblies, and finds a violation — had the assembly been non-peaceful, Article 11 protection would not apply). These sub-elements are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied — the Court refers to "public assemblies" without explicitly analyzing whether an assembly existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court proceeds to find a violation of Article 11, which logically requires the assembly to have been peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The earlier passage also refers to "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly acknowledged, though the Court does not engage in a detailed separate analysis of this element — it is treated as established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" requirement is not discussed at all — neither explicitly nor by clear implication. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not_discussed, and no conclusion can be drawn from the text.

- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not_discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not_discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not_discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim element is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the government. However, by proceeding to the necessity analysis, one could argue that a legitimate aim is implied (since necessity analysis presupposes a legitimate aim), but the Court does not spell this out. Given the brevity of the reasoning and the reliance on leading cases, the legitimate aim is at best implied. No specific aims are identified in the text.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court also references its case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and the leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The positive obligation dimension of Article 11 is not discussed in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (the state interfering with the applicants' freedom of assembly). No positive obligation is spelled out or found violated.
