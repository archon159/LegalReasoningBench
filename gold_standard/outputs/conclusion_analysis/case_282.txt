**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and describes "the dispersal of these assemblies" — this implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without questioning whether the assembly was outside the scope of protection). The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov further implies that the assemblies were peaceful and within scope.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as "yes" — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "the dispersal of these assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied as "yes" — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as "yes" — the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the scope of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze whether there was an interference in a dedicated passage, but it is clearly implied. The text states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" presupposes that an interference was found. Additionally, the opening paragraph describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of, which are treated as interferences throughout. This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, either explicitly or impliedly. The reasoning jumps directly to necessity. None of the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are addressed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The reasoning proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law, specifically the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, and by stating it found "no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. There is no mention of a duty on the government to facilitate or protect the assembly. This element is not discussed.
