**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the section 'As regards the applicant's conviction under Articles 220 § 6 and 314 of the Criminal Code – (ii) Whether the interference was justified': 'Hence, the interference resulting from the application of Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code in the present case was not prescribed by law. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that an assembly existed. The demonstration of 26 October 2008 is treated throughout as a public gathering/demonstration falling within the scope of Article 11. The Government's admissibility objection that the demonstration did not fall within Article 11's scope was dismissed: 'The Court notes that it has already examined and dismissed identical objections submitted by the Government.' The Court also states: 'the applicant's criminal conviction under Articles 220 § 6 and 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code was indisputably directed at activities falling within the scope of freedom of assembly.' This is explicit.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses whether the applicant participated. It notes the applicant's denial but concludes: 'the applicant's criminal conviction under Articles 220 § 6 and 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code was indisputably directed at activities falling within the scope of freedom of assembly, and that he was sanctioned for participating in the demonstration held on 26 October 2008.' The Court also notes that in later submissions the applicant 'contended that he had attended the demonstration of 26 October 2008.' The Court proceeds on the basis of the facts as established by the national courts, which found he participated. This is explicit.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court does not explicitly address whether the assembly was peaceful in a separate analysis. However, the Court's reasoning in the merits section implicitly treats the assembly as peaceful (or at least the applicant's participation as peaceful), noting that 'the very essence of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly... had been undermined when Mr Işıkırık had been held criminally liable under Articles 220 § 6 and 314 of the Criminal Code for the mere fact of attending a public meeting and expressing his views therein.' The Court draws a distinction between a 'peaceful demonstrator' and 'an individual who had committed offences within the structure of the PKK,' implying the assembly/participation was peaceful. This is implied rather than explicitly decided.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and finds interference: 'the Court concludes that the applicant's criminal conviction for membership of the PKK as well as the imposition of a prison sentence upon him, constituted an interference with the exercise of his right to freedom of assembly as guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention.' The Government's ratione materiae objection (that there was no interference because the applicant denied participation) was dismissed. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this element and finds the interference was NOT prescribed by law. Specifically, it finds the foreseeability requirement was not met: 'the interference resulting from the application of Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code in the present case was not prescribed by law.' The Court relies on its earlier Işıkırık judgment, which found that 'the wording of the provision, including its extensive interpretation by the domestic courts, did not afford a sufficient measure of protection against arbitrary interferences by the public authorities' and that 'Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code had not been "foreseeable" in its application since it did not afford the applicant the legal protection against arbitrary interference with his right under Article 11 of the Convention.'

**(i) Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed and found not satisfied. The Court quotes Işıkırık: 'Article 220 § 6 of the Criminal Code had not been "foreseeable" in its application since it did not afford the applicant the legal protection against arbitrary interference with his right under Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court applies this to the present case.

**(ii) Accessible:** The Government argued the provisions 'were accessible and foreseeable.' The Court does not separately address accessibility; it focuses on foreseeability/safeguards. Accessibility is not_discussed as a separate sub-element by the Court (the Government raised it but the Court did not address it separately).

**(iii) Safeguards:** The Court implicitly addresses safeguards through the foreseeability analysis, noting the provision 'did not afford a sufficient measure of protection against arbitrary interferences by the public authorities.' This is implied within the foreseeability/prescribed by law analysis.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued the interference pursued legitimate aims: 'protecting national security, territorial integrity, public safety and public order, as well as the prevention of crime.' However, the Court does not explicitly address or decide on legitimate aim. Having found the interference was not prescribed by law, the Court does not proceed to examine legitimate aim. This element is not_discussed by the Court in its assessment (the Government raised it but the Court did not rule on it).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court does not reach the necessity test, having found the interference was not prescribed by law. However, within the prescribed by law analysis (drawing on Işıkırık), the Court does discuss proportionality-adjacent concerns: the 'chilling effect' of Article 220 § 6, the deterrence of public participation, and the equating of peaceful demonstration with membership of an illegal organisation. These observations are embedded in the prescribed by law analysis rather than a separate necessity analysis. Necessity is not_discussed as a standalone element.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (unjustified interference by the State through criminal conviction). This element is not_discussed.
