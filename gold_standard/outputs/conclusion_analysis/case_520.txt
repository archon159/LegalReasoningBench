**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether an assembly existed, whether the applicants participated, and whether it was peaceful). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The introductory paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." This implies that (i) assemblies existed, (ii) the applicants participated in them, and (iii) the assemblies were peaceful (since the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without excluding the applicants from its protection). The Court's reference to leading cases such as Frumkin v. Russia and Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia — which involved peaceful assemblies — further implies that the assemblies here were peaceful. None of these sub-elements are explicitly discussed as such.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a standalone element. The introductory paragraph describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." The Court then states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" confirms that interference is treated as established, though it is not separately analyzed. It is implied by the Court's direct move to the necessity analysis.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" requirement and its three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse) are not discussed at all — neither explicitly nor by implication. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis without addressing whether the interference had a legal basis. This element is not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim requirement is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the Russian authorities. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly addressed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the explicit conclusion on the necessity element, and it is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and the principle of proportionality cited from Oya Ataman v. Turkey and Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova. The conclusion is that necessity is NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is not_discussed.
