**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The key passage is in the final section on necessity:

"It follows that the interference with the applicants' rights cannot be said to have been 'necessary in a democratic society' and was thus in breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

This conclusion is reached after the Court finds that the domestic courts (specifically the Supreme Court) failed to carry out the balancing test required under Article 11 § 2: "By reaching such a conclusion and not exercising the balancing test required under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, the Supreme Court failed to give relevant and sufficient reasons for the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly, and thus failed to convincingly establish the necessity for such restrictions, which must be interpreted narrowly."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses whether there was an assembly. The gathering on 5 July 2011 is treated throughout as an assembly. The Court notes: "The Court is prepared to agree with the Government that impeding the scheduled eviction of the Schijnheilig squat was the foreseeable result of intentional action by the organisers and participants, including the applicants." The existence of a gathering/assembly is not disputed — it is explicitly treated as such throughout the judgment. This is explicit.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicants' participation. It notes: "the applicants – who must be presumed to have had peaceful intentions in the absence of sufficient and convincing evidence to the contrary – personally set off smoke bombs, threw objects or kicked out in the direction of the police, or otherwise resorted or incited to violence" (negating violent participation). The applicants' participation in the gathering is explicitly confirmed: "the Court finds that the conduct during the gathering for which they were held responsible was not of such a nature and degree as to remove their participation in it from the scope of protection of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention." This is explicit.

**iii) Peaceful:** This is the central contested issue and is explicitly and extensively discussed. The Government argued the assembly was not peaceful; the applicants argued it was. The Court applies a three-part test: "(i) whether the assembly was intended to be peaceful or whether the organisers had violent intentions; (ii) whether the applicant demonstrated violent intentions when joining the assembly; and (iii) whether the applicant inflicted bodily harm on anyone." The Court concludes: "the Court finds that the applicants are entitled to invoke the guarantees of Article 11, which is therefore applicable ratione materiae in the present case" — meaning the assembly was sufficiently peaceful for the applicants. Key passage: "Since it does not appear from the materials in the case file that the applicants...personally set off smoke bombs, threw objects or kicked out in the direction of the police, or otherwise resorted or incited to violence, the Court finds that the conduct during the gathering for which they were held responsible was not of such a nature and degree as to remove their participation in it from the scope of protection of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention." This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed and confirmed: "the Court finds that the applicants are entitled to invoke the guarantees of Article 11, which is therefore applicable ratione materiae in the present case, and that their arrest, prosecution and conviction amounted to an interference with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court also notes in the general principles section: "an interference may consist in, inter alia, dispersal of a gathering or the arrest of participants, and penalties imposed for having taken part in a gathering." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly raises this issue but declines to rule on it: "The Court notes that the parties disagreed as to whether the interference was prescribed by law (see paragraphs 41 and 44 above). However, the Court decides to dispense with ruling on the issue of lawfulness because, in any event, the interference cannot be said to have been 'necessary in a democratic society', for the reasons set out below."

Thus, the overall 'prescribed by law' question is explicitly raised but left unresolved (no conclusion). The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed. The conclusion is null for all three sub-elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed and accepted: "The Court further accepts that the interference may be regarded as having pursued the 'prevention of disorder or crime' and the 'protection of the rights and freedoms of others' within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Article 11." This is explicit. The aims found are: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, and protection of the rights and freedoms of others. (The Government also mentioned public safety, but the Court's own acceptance specifically names 'prevention of disorder or crime' and 'protection of the rights and freedoms of others'.)

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed and found not satisfied (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary). The Court finds: "By reaching such a conclusion and not exercising the balancing test required under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, the Supreme Court failed to give relevant and sufficient reasons for the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly, and thus failed to convincingly establish the necessity for such restrictions." And: "the Court finds that the requirements under Article 11 of the Convention were not met because the analysis of applicability of that provision – and, consequently, the assessment of the justification of the interference – were not carried out at the domestic level in a manner consistent with the Convention and the Court's case-law." Final conclusion: "It follows that the interference with the applicants' rights cannot be said to have been 'necessary in a democratic society' and was thus in breach of Article 11 of the Convention." This is explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference with the right). This element is not discussed.
