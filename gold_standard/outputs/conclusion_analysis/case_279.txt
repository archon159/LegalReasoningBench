**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was within the scope of Article 11 protection. The Court proceeds directly to discuss interference and necessity, which logically presupposes that the scope conditions are satisfied. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied — the text refers to "public assemblies" that were dispersed, presupposing that assemblies existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," presupposing participation.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court applies Article 11 protection without any suggestion that the assemblies were violent or outside the scope of protection. The leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies, and the Court found no reason to depart from those conclusions.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and earlier the text describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. The Court thus explicitly treats these acts as interferences with freedom of assembly.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. Since the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis (finding the interference not necessary in a democratic society), the "prescribed by law" element is not discussed — neither explicitly nor by clear implication. The Court's reliance on leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) suggests it found the analysis in those cases applicable, but no specific finding on "prescribed by law" is made here.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or assessed. The Court moves directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied, leading to the violation. The Court references its case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and the leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov), finding no reason to depart from those conclusions.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative interference (arrest, dispersal, conviction). No positive obligation is spelled out or assessed.
