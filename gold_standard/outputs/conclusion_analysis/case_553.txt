**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and describes "the dispersal of these assemblies" — this implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without questioning whether the assembly was outside the scope of protection). The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov further implies that the assemblies were peaceful and within scope.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as "yes" — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied as "yes" — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as "yes" — the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the scope of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and earlier the text describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This confirms that an interference was found.

Conclusion: explicitly discussed, yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without reaching the "prescribed by law" question, this element is not discussed. It is not even implied, as the Court's reasoning does not logically require a finding on this element before reaching necessity — the Court simply bypasses it.

Conclusion: not discussed for all three sub-elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. No specific legitimate aim is identified or discussed. This element is not discussed.

Conclusion: not discussed, no aims found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court relies on its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and finds no fact or argument capable of leading to a different conclusion.

Conclusion: explicitly discussed, no (not necessary in a democratic society, i.e., the necessity requirement was not satisfied — the interference was disproportionate).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (interference by the state through arrests and convictions).

Conclusion: not discussed.
