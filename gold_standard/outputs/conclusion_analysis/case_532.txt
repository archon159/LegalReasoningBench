**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court explicitly states that the complaints "disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The scope is not explicitly discussed in the reasoning text. However, it is implied by the Court's reference to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" — this logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to participate) in it, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since Article 11 only protects peaceful assemblies). The Court describes the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and refers to "the dispersal of these assemblies," which implies all three sub-elements are satisfied.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "these assemblies" without questioning their existence.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies were non-peaceful, and the leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used directly, and the factual background refers to "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" requirement is not explicitly discussed. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds (without needing to address prescribed by law), this element is not discussed — neither explicitly nor by clear implication. It is not_discussed.

- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not_discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not_discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not_discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify which legitimate aim the interference pursued. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not_discussed, and no specific aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" (the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not spell out any positive obligation in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (unjustified interference). This element is not_discussed.
