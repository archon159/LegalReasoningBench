**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is found in the final paragraph of Section B.2 ('The Court's assessment'):

'Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion in the present case. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly was based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's "quality of law" requirements, and was moreover not "necessary in a democratic society". There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicant participated, or whether it was peaceful. However, these elements are implied by the Court's explicit finding of an 'interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly.' For there to be an interference with freedom of assembly, there must logically be an assembly within the scope of Article 11, the applicant must have participated (or tried to), and the assembly must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). The applicant's submissions also note the event 'had been peaceful and had not caused any disturbances of public order or of traffic, or any material damage. No calls for violence or insulting statements had been made during the meeting,' which the Court does not contradict. Scope is therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the Court refers to 'the interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly,' presupposing an assembly existed.
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the applicant organised and held the event; the Court treats her as the subject of the interference.
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court proceeds to assess the interference under Article 11 without questioning the peaceful nature of the assembly; the applicant's uncontested submissions describe it as peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds an interference: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly was based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's "quality of law" requirements.' The refusal to approve the location, the arrest, and the conviction are all identified as the relevant state acts. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this element: 'the interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly was based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's "quality of law" requirements.' This is a direct finding that the 'prescribed by law' requirement was not satisfied. The Court does not separately and explicitly discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse — it simply states the overall 'quality of law' standard was not met, referencing its leading case Lashmankin and Others (where those sub-elements were examined in detail). The overall prescribed_by_law conclusion is explicit (no), but the sub-elements are not individually discussed in this judgment.

- **prescribed_by_law**: explicit, conclusion: no.
- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not individually discussed in this judgment (the Court relies on Lashmankin by reference); conclusion: null.
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not individually discussed; conclusion: null.
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not individually discussed; conclusion: null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly resolve the legitimate aim question. The Government argued the measures 'pursued the legitimate aims of protecting public order and the rights of others.' The applicant disputed this. However, the Court's assessment does not address legitimate aim — it jumps from finding the interference to finding it fails the 'quality of law' test and is not necessary in a democratic society. Given the Court found the interference was not prescribed by law, it did not need to resolve legitimate aim. The legitimate aim element is therefore not discussed by the Court itself (though raised by the parties). Conclusion: null, not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly addresses this: 'the interference ... was moreover not "necessary in a democratic society".' This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied. The Court does not elaborate further, relying on its case-law (Lashmankin and Others). Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary), discussed explicitly.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. This element is entirely absent. Conclusion: null, not discussed.
