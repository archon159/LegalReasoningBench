**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to find a violation under Article 11, which only protects peaceful assemblies, and no suggestion of violence is made.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court explicitly states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" confirms that interference is found. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, which is the element it finds violated. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, these elements remain not_discussed. No conclusion can be drawn.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It does not mention national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, protection of health, protection of morals, or rights of others. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity finding without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that necessity is not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim), leading to the violation. The conclusion is "no" (necessity was not established/satisfied).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is not discussed at all.
