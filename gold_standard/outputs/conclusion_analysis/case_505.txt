**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage states: "Having examined all the material before it, the Court concludes that they also disclose violations of the Convention in the light of its well-established case-law (see ... Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, 5 January 2016, concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies)."

The Court does not assign explicit paragraph numbers in the excerpt provided, but the finding of a violation is contained in the section titled "III. OTHER ALLEGED VIOLATIONS UNDER WELL-ESTABLISHED CASE-LAW." The reference to Frumkin v. Russia specifically concerns Article 11 (freedom of assembly), and the Court's conclusion that violations are disclosed applies to the Article 11 complaints as well.

**Question 2: Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope:**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicant participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, by referencing Frumkin v. Russia as the leading case and describing the complaints as concerning "disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies," the existence of an assembly and the applicants' participation are implied. The peaceful nature of the assembly is not discussed. These elements are implied rather than explicitly addressed.

**(b) Interference:**
The Court does not explicitly discuss interference, but it is implied by the reference to "disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies." The word "measures" implies governmental action that interfered with the right. This is implied rather than explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law:**
The Court does not discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These elements are not discussed at all.

**(d) Legitimate aim:**
The Court does not discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:**
The Court references "disproportionate measures," which directly invokes the necessity/proportionality test. This is the core finding — that the measures were disproportionate — which is the central element of the necessity test under Article 11. This is implied (and arguably explicit through the word "disproportionate") rather than fully elaborated. Given the Court proceeds by reference to well-established case-law without detailed reasoning, this is best characterized as implied.

**(f) Positive obligation:**
The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is not discussed.

**Summary:**
The reasoning is extremely compressed, relying entirely on the well-established case-law approach. The Court simply declares violations by reference to Frumkin v. Russia without walking through the canonical steps. Scope and interference are implied by the description of the facts; necessity/proportionality is implied by the word "disproportionate"; all other elements (prescribed by law and its sub-elements, legitimate aim, positive obligation) are not discussed.
