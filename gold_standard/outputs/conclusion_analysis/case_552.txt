**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was within the scope of Article 11 protection. The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov — all of which involved peaceful assemblies — further implies that the assemblies were peaceful. These elements are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the description "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the same description.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied by the overall framing and reference to prior cases involving peaceful assemblies; no suggestion of violence.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and earlier the text notes "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This confirms that interference is explicitly discussed and found to exist.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not_discussed. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. The sub-elements are therefore not_discussed, and no conclusion can be drawn from the text.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified in the text. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not_discussed, and no aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. This is explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not_discussed.
