**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of the merits assessment: 'There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant.' The key reasoning paragraph leading to this conclusion states: 'Accordingly, the Court considers that in the instant case the overall reaction of the domestic authorities towards the applicants' freedom of assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society".' The Court also notes earlier: 'even accepting that the applicants did indeed hold a group public event rather than solo demonstrations, and that the authorities thus interfered with their right of peaceful assembly, the Court considers that the circumstances of the case still disclose a violation of Article 11 of the Convention interpreted in the light of Article 10.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court explicitly discusses whether there was an assembly. It notes the domestic courts found 'the applicants held a group public event in the form of a "picket" without giving prior notice.' The Court then states: 'even accepting that the applicants did indeed hold a group public event rather than solo demonstrations.' This is explicitly discussed, and the conclusion is that an assembly existed (the Court accepts this for the purposes of its analysis, even if the applicants disputed it).

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly discusses the applicants' participation. It notes 'it was crucial to ascertain whether the applicants were involved in a single "assembly" with others (in the present case, also with each other)' and accepts that they participated in a group public event. This is explicitly discussed with a positive conclusion.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly: 'It was not suggested that they had demonstrated any violent behaviour or in any other way disrupted peace or public order.' Also, referencing prior case law: 'in the absence of any acts of violence on the part of the demonstrators.' The assembly was peaceful — explicitly discussed, positive conclusion.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: 'The parties agreed that there had been interference with the applicants' right to peaceful assembly, although they had different views as to the elements of that interference.' The Court then specifies: 'the interference by the authorities consisted of the termination of the applicants' participation in the event, their arrest at the venue of the event and their administrative conviction.' Conclusion: yes, there was interference. Explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court discusses the 'prescribed by law' element, but specifically in the context of foreseeability. It states: 'solo demonstrators, such as the applicants asserted to be, could not rely in the exercise of their freedom of expression on a foreseeable legislative framework which, at the same time, had the potential of interfering with that freedom by way of classifying certain conduct or omissions as an offence on account of a breach of the notification requirement under the PEA.' This is discussed in the context of the solo demonstrator scenario. However, the Court then pivots to accept the group event characterization and proceeds to find a violation on necessity grounds, without making a definitive finding on whether the interference was 'prescribed by law' in the group event scenario. The Court states the standard test ('Such interference constitutes a breach of Article 11 of the Convention unless it is prescribed by law, pursues one or more legitimate aims under paragraph 2 of that provision and is "necessary in a democratic society"') but does not explicitly resolve the prescribed by law question for the group event scenario — it jumps to necessity. The foreseeability sub-element is explicitly discussed (negatively, at least for the solo demonstrator scenario). Accessibility and safeguards are not discussed.

Given the Court's approach — it raises the prescribed by law issue regarding foreseeability in the solo demonstrator context, then accepts the group event characterization and finds a violation on necessity grounds without resolving prescribed by law for the group scenario — I treat prescribed by law overall as not conclusively resolved (null conclusion), with foreseeability explicitly discussed (with a negative conclusion in the solo context), and accessibility and safeguards not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court recites the standard formula that interference must pursue 'one or more legitimate aims under paragraph 2' but does not explicitly identify or assess any specific legitimate aim. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without resolving the legitimate aim question. This element is not discussed substantively — it is implied by the structure of the analysis but not resolved. Conclusion: null, not discussed (beyond the formulaic recitation).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and resolved. The Court states: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the overall reaction of the domestic authorities towards the applicants' freedom of assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society".' It reasons: 'both applicants were arrested and sentenced to non-negligible fines for holding a group public event without giving prior notice. It was not suggested that they had demonstrated any violent behaviour or in any other way disrupted peace or public order. Nor did the authorities explain why they had preferred not to allow the demonstrators, including the applicants, to complete their assembly and to impose a reasonable fine on the spot or later on.' The Court also references its consistent case law finding no 'pressing social need' for arrest and conviction in the absence of violence. Conclusion: no, the interference was not necessary. Explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (interference and its justification). Conclusion: not discussed.
