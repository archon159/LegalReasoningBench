**Question 1: Does the Court find a violation of Article 11?**

Yes, the Court finds violations of Article 11 on two distinct grounds:

1. **Positive obligations (Article 11 alone and in conjunction with Article 14):** "The Court therefore considers that the domestic authorities failed to comply with their positive obligations under Article 11 of the Convention, taken alone and in conjunction with Article 14 (compare Identoba, cited above, §§ 92 and 100)."

2. **Negative obligations (Article 11 alone):** "The Court therefore considers that there has been a violation of the State's negative obligations Article 11 of the Convention taken alone."

However, the Court does NOT find a violation of Article 14 in conjunction with the negative obligations under Article 11: "There has been no violation of Article 14 taken in conjunction with negative obligations under Article 11 of the Convention."

---

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that a public meeting (Coming Out Day) constitutes an assembly within the meaning of Article 11. This is implied throughout the judgment, e.g.: "The Court is satisfied that the St Petersburg authorities did not ban the public meeting in support of the LGBTI community, where the applicant intended to participate." The existence of an assembly is never questioned.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation: "The applicant's arrest prevented her from participating in the public event." Her attempt to participate is also described: "the applicant and other participants were unable to find a place for the event at the square which was occupied by counter-demonstrators."

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly: "Moreover, given that the applicant's conduct was clearly of non-violent nature, the Court considers that the reasons relied upon by the domestic authorities for the arrest were insufficient to justify that the applicant was prevented from continuing to participate in the event." The general principles section also references "peaceful demonstration" and "peaceful assembly" throughout, and the applicant's assembly is treated as peaceful throughout.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference in the context of the negative obligations analysis: "The Court observes that police officers arrested the applicant and other participants of the meeting on the grounds of the alleged use of foul language. The applicant's arrest prevented her from participating in the public event. Therefore, this action of the police amounted to an interference with her rights enshrined by Articles 11 of the Convention."

In the positive obligations strand, the Court frames the issue differently — as a failure to act rather than an interference — but the overall finding of a violation is established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses the lawfulness requirement in the context of the negative obligations/interference analysis: "The Court has already found that the applicant's arrest fell short of the lawfulness requirement (see paragraph 38 above), accordingly, the interference in question was unlawful (compare Kasparov v. Russia, no. 53659/07, § 69, 11 October 2016 and Hakim Aydın v. Turkey, no. 4048/09, § 51, 26 May 2020)."

The Court references paragraph 38 for the lawfulness finding, but in the reasoning section provided, the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually discussed in the excerpt provided. The conclusion is that the interference was NOT prescribed by law (it "fell short of the lawfulness requirement"), but the sub-elements are not broken down in this excerpt.

**Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed in this excerpt (the finding is by reference to paragraph 38).
**Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed in this excerpt.
**Safeguards:** Not explicitly discussed in this excerpt.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. Because the Court found the interference was unlawful (not prescribed by law), it did not need to proceed to the legitimate aim or necessity analysis in the negative obligations strand. In the positive obligations strand, the analysis focuses on whether the State fulfilled its positive duties, not on the three-part justification test. No legitimate aim is identified or discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court does not explicitly conduct a necessity analysis in the canonical sense. In the negative obligations strand, having found the interference unlawful, the Court also adds: "Moreover, given that the applicant's conduct was clearly of non-violent nature, the Court considers that the reasons relied upon by the domestic authorities for the arrest were insufficient to justify that the applicant was prevented from continuing to participate in the event." This touches on proportionality/necessity but is not framed as a full necessity-in-a-democratic-society analysis. The positive obligations analysis is conducted under a different framework.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses positive obligations. Key passages:

- "Genuine, effective freedom of peaceful assembly cannot, therefore, be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere: a purely negative conception would not be compatible with the object and purpose of Article 11 of the Convention. This provision sometimes requires positive measures to be taken, even in the sphere of relations between individuals, if need be."

- "That positive obligation is of particular importance for persons holding unpopular views or belonging to minorities, because they are more vulnerable to victimisation."

- "The Court notes that the State's compliance with their positive obligations under the Convention in the present case should be assessed in the light of the subject matter of the assembly. Those obligations were of paramount importance in the present case, because the applicant as well as other participants in Coming Out Day belonged to a minority."

- "Accordingly, the authorities failed to duly facilitate the conduct of the planned event by restraining homophobic verbal attacks and physical pressure by counter-demonstrators."

- "The Court therefore considers that the domestic authorities failed to comply with their positive obligations under Article 11 of the Convention, taken alone and in conjunction with Article 14."

The Court finds that the positive obligation WAS violated — the State failed to take adequate measures to protect the assembly from counter-demonstrators, despite deploying 540 officers, because those officers remained passive in the face of homophobic attacks and did not secure a safe perimeter or take preliminary measures to facilitate the event.
