**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The scope is not explicitly discussed in the reasoning text. However, it is implied by the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred, which logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to participate) in it, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since the Court applies Article 11 protection without qualification). The opening paragraph also describes the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," implying all three sub-elements are satisfied.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether an assembly existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied as yes — the Court applies Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies were non-peaceful; the leading cases cited also involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The interference is explicitly acknowledged. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit confirmation of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

This element is not explicitly discussed. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. It is not implied either, as the Court does not reason through the prescribed-by-law test at all — it simply references leading cases and concludes on necessity. The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are likewise not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the government. However, by proceeding to the necessity analysis, one might infer that a legitimate aim was assumed (as in the leading cases cited), but the Court does not spell this out. Given the absence of any explicit or clearly implied discussion, this is best treated as not discussed, with no aims identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

No positive obligation is discussed or implied in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference), not positive obligations to facilitate or protect assemblies.
