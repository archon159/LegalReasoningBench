**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." This follows from the Court's finding that the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly could not be regarded as 'necessary in a democratic society,' based on the Regional Governor's reliance on the same grounds that the Court had already found deficient on at least three prior occasions.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 exists, but this is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers throughout to 'rallies' and 'meetings' that the applicants planned to hold, and the entire analysis proceeds on the assumption that Article 11 is engaged. The admissibility section notes the complaint concerns being 'banned from holding a meeting on 22 April 2007,' and the merits section discusses the Regional Governor's ban on 'the rally that UMO Ilinden – PIRIN intended to hold at Yane Sandanski's grave near the Rozhen Monastery and the one it wanted to stage in front of Yane Sandanski's monument in Melnik.' The existence of an assembly is implied.

*Participation:* The Court implies that the applicants were participants/organisers. The merits section notes: 'the police ordered the members of UMO Ilinden – PIRIN's managing board and the first and second applicants to refrain from organising or taking part in the two rallies.' This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a doctrinal element.

*Peaceful:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the entire analysis proceeds under Article 11's protection of 'peaceful assembly,' and there is no suggestion of violence. The Court's finding of interference and its analysis of justification implicitly assumes the assembly was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. In the merits section: 'Those measures clearly indicate an intention to prevent the rally. They had a chilling effect on the individuals concerned and on the other participants in the rallies, and thus amounted in themselves to interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly.' The Court also finds that the police vehicle checks and road block 'also amounted to interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly.' The Court further elaborates: 'The Court observes in that connection that an interference does not need to amount to an outright ban, legal or de facto, but can consist in various other measures taken by the authorities.'

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the 'prescribed by law' requirement but does not reach a definitive conclusion. It states: 'The Court has misgivings as to whether the above measures were "prescribed by law", for several reasons. First, the 1990 Meetings and Marches Act envisages no role for regional governors in the policing of rallies. Secondly, in banning the rally the Regional Governor relied on some clearly irrelevant grounds... Thirdly, section 55(1) of the 2006 Ministry of Internal Affairs Act, which served as a basis for the orders issued by the police, is formulated in very general terms and gives no indication of the circumstances in which the police may use the power conferred on them... Fourthly, no legal basis has been cited for the police block of the road to the Rozhen Monastery.' However, the Court then states: 'the Court does not consider it necessary to determine whether the interference was "prescribed by law" or pursued a legitimate aim, as it finds... that, even if it did, it cannot be regarded as being "necessary in a democratic society".' So the Court leaves the prescribed by law question unresolved.

*Foreseeable:* The Court's third point about section 55(1) being 'formulated in very general terms and gives no indication of the circumstances in which the police may use the power conferred on them' addresses foreseeability explicitly, though without a definitive conclusion.

*Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed.

*Safeguards:* The Court's concern about the very general terms of section 55(1) and the lack of any legal basis for the road block implicitly touches on the absence of safeguards, but this is not explicitly framed as a 'safeguards' sub-element.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly declines to determine whether a legitimate aim was pursued: 'the Court does not consider it necessary to determine whether the interference was "prescribed by law" or pursued a legitimate aim.' The Regional Governor's stated grounds included 'propaganda against the country's sovereignty and territorial integrity,' a 'conjectural threat to public order,' and 'the holding of meetings of other organisations at the same time and place,' which could relate to national security, public safety, or prevention of disorder — but the Court does not endorse any of these as legitimate aims.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses and decides this element. It finds the interference was not necessary in a democratic society: 'even if it did [pursue a legitimate aim], it cannot be regarded as being "necessary in a democratic society".' The Court reasons: 'The Regional Governor's decision... relied on exactly the same grounds as those given to ban previous rallies of UMO Ilinden or UMO Ilinden – PIRIN, in respect of which the Court has, on no less than three occasions, found violations of Article 11... The fact that the Regional Governor should rely on grounds which the Court had, at the time when the Governor made his decision, already found deficient is indicative of a troubling disregard for the Court's judgments and the applicants' right to freedom of peaceful assembly.'

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Government raised positive obligations in its merits submissions: 'The Government submitted that the planned rally had in fact taken place... The authorities had complied with their positive obligations under Article 11 and ensured that the event proceeded smoothly.' The Court does not explicitly endorse or analyze a positive obligation framework in its own reasoning — it focuses on the negative obligation (interference) analysis. However, in the admissibility section, the Court does note in passing that 'if States were able to confine their response to such incidents to the mere payment of compensation, without putting in place effective procedures ensuring the possibility of staging such rallies, it would be possible in some cases for the authorities arbitrarily to deprive groups of individuals and organisations of their right to freedom of peaceful assembly' — which touches on positive obligations but in the context of effective remedies, not as a standalone positive obligation finding. The Court does not make a separate finding on positive obligations in the merits; the violation is found on the basis of unjustified interference (negative obligation). Positive obligations are mentioned but not the basis of the violation finding.
