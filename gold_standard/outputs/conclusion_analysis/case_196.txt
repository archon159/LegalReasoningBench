**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated or tried to participate, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since Article 11 only protects peaceful assemblies, and the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without questioning the peaceful nature). These are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" and "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the description of applicants as "organisers or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court proceeds to assess the interference without questioning whether the assemblies were peaceful, which logically presupposes they were.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and earlier the Court notes "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit discussion of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity, relying on its prior case-law. The sub-elements are therefore not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. This element is not discussed. However, since the Court proceeds to assess necessity (which logically presupposes a legitimate aim exists), one could argue it is implied. But given the Court's very compressed reasoning — essentially a repetition of prior findings — it is safer to treat this as not_discussed, with no aims identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that necessity was not satisfied, meaning the interference was disproportionate. The Court also references its prior case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and the leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is not discussed at all.
