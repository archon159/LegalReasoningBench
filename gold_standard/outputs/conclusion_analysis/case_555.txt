**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11's protection. These are implied rather than explicitly analyzed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the same passage — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without excluding the assemblies from protection, which logically requires that they were peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the opening paragraph describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Interference is thus explicitly discussed (the Court names it and proceeds to assess its justification), though the conclusion on interference is embedded in the broader analysis rather than stated as a separate finding.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not_discussed. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment, relying on its prior case-law in Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. This element is not_discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of any legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" — the interference was not necessary.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is not_discussed.
