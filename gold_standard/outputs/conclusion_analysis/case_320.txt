**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The key paragraph is the final one of section (c) 'Application of these principles to the circumstances of the present case':

'All in all, the Court considers that the domestic authorities failed to ensure that the march of 17 May 2012, which was organised by the first applicant and attended by the thirteen individual applicants (from the second to the fourteenth), could take place peacefully by sufficiently containing homophobic and violent counter-demonstrators. In view of those omissions, the authorities fell short of their positive obligations under Article 11 taken in conjunction with Article 14 of the Convention.'

This is a clear finding of a violation of Article 11 (in conjunction with Article 14).

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly affirms that there was a peaceful march: 'the disruption of the applicants' participation in the peaceful march of 17 May 2012, organised to mark the International Day Against Homophobia, undoubtedly constituted an interference under Article 11 of the Convention.' The existence of an assembly (a public march) is explicitly confirmed.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes that the applicants participated (or attempted to participate) in the march: 'the disruption of the applicants' participation in the peaceful march of 17 May 2012' and 'the march of 17 May 2012, which was organised by the first applicant and attended by the thirteen individual applicants.' Participation is explicitly discussed.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly characterises the march as peaceful throughout: 'the disruption of the applicants' participation in the peaceful march of 17 May 2012' and 'the domestic authorities failed to ensure that the march of 17 May 2012 ... could take place peacefully.' The peaceful nature of the assembly is explicitly confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly addresses interference: 'the disruption of the applicants' participation in the peaceful march of 17 May 2012, organised to mark the International Day Against Homophobia, undoubtedly constituted an interference under Article 11 of the Convention, read in the light of the relevant principles under Article 10. Indeed, the Convention protects public forms of expression, including through holding a peaceful assembly, and the expression of opinions in relation to campaigning for and raising awareness of the fundamental rights of various sexual minorities.' The Court notes this 'is not even in dispute between the parties.' The interference here is framed as the disruption caused by private individuals combined with police passivity/inaction — i.e., a failure of positive obligation rather than a direct state act. Conclusion: yes, interference found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss the 'prescribed by law' requirement at all. This is because the case is framed as a positive obligation case — the State failed to protect the applicants from private violence — rather than a case where the State directly restricted the assembly through a legal measure. Since there is no State-imposed restriction to be justified, the 'prescribed by law' test is not applicable and not discussed. Conclusion: not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Similarly, because the case is analysed as a positive obligation case rather than a justification of a State restriction, the Court does not discuss whether any interference served a legitimate aim. Not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court does not apply the necessity test in the traditional sense (balancing the interference against a legitimate aim). Again, this is because the case is framed as a positive obligation case. Not discussed in the traditional sense.

**(f) Positive obligation**

This is the central element of the Court's analysis and is explicitly and extensively discussed.

In the general principles section: 'Genuine, effective freedom of peaceful assembly cannot, therefore, be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere: a purely negative conception would not be compatible with the object and purpose of Article 11 of the Convention. This provision sometimes requires positive measures to be taken, even in the sphere of relations between individuals, if need be ... That positive obligation is of particular importance for persons holding unpopular views or belonging to minorities, because they are more vulnerable to victimisation.'

Also: 'The participants must, however, be able, with the State's assistance, to hold the demonstration without having to fear that they will be subjected to physical violence by their opponents; such a fear would be liable to deter associations or other groups supporting common ideas or interests from openly expressing their opinions on highly controversial issues affecting the community.'

In the application section, the Court finds the positive obligation was violated: 'despite the fact that the domestic authorities were given prior notice on 8 May 2012 about the intention to organise a peaceful march on 17 May 2012, they did not manage to use that generous period of nine days for careful preparatory work ... the authorities knew or should have known of the risk of tensions ... They were thus under an obligation to use any means possible, for instance by making public statements in advance of the demonstration to advocate, without any ambiguity, a tolerant, conciliatory stance ... as well as to warn potential law-breakers of the nature of possible sanctions. Furthermore, it was apparent from the outcome of the LGBT procession, that the number of police patrol officers dispatched to the scene of the demonstration was not sufficient ...'

Conclusion: positive obligation explicitly discussed, and the Court finds it was violated (conclusion: yes, the positive obligation exists and was breached).
