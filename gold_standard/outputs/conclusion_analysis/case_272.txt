The reasoning section is a summary disposition under 'well-established case-law,' where the Court does not engage in a step-by-step canonical Article 11 analysis. Instead, it simply declares the complaints admissible and concludes they disclose violations of the Convention by reference to prior leading cases.

**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR:**
The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage states: 'Having examined all the material before it, the Court concludes that they also disclose violations of the Convention in the light of its well-established case-law (see Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, 5 January 2016, concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies...).' The reference to Frumkin v. Russia — which is an Article 11 case concerning disproportionate measures against participants of public assemblies — makes clear that the Article 11 violation is among those found. The Court does not cite a specific paragraph number within this reasoning section; the entire passage constitutes the finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine:**

**(a) Scope (assembly exists, participation, peaceful):** None of these sub-elements are explicitly discussed in the text. However, the reference to Frumkin v. Russia 'concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies' implies that there was an assembly, that the applicants participated (or attempted to), and that the assembly was within the scope of Article 11. These are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference:** Not explicitly discussed. It is implied by the finding of a violation and the reference to 'disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants,' which logically presupposes an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards):** Not discussed at all — neither explicitly nor by clear implication. The Court jumps directly to a violation finding without addressing the prescribed-by-law requirement.

**(d) Legitimate aim:** Not discussed. No aims are identified or assessed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:** Not explicitly discussed, but the reference to 'disproportionate measures' in the Frumkin citation implies that the necessity/proportionality test was the basis for the violation finding. This is implied rather than explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation:** Not discussed at all.

In summary, the Court's reasoning is extremely compressed, relying entirely on a reference to Frumkin v. Russia to establish the Article 11 violation. The only substantive doctrinal signal is the word 'disproportionate,' which implies that the necessity/proportionality element was the decisive one, and which in turn implies that scope and interference were also satisfied. None of the prescribed-by-law sub-elements, legitimate aim, or positive obligation are addressed.
