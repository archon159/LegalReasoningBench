**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits assessment states: "There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." This conclusion follows from the Court's finding that the authorities failed to give relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the prohibitions of the rallies, and that the bans were not necessary in a democratic society.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*Assembly exists:* The Court implicitly accepts that the planned rallies constitute assemblies within the meaning of Article 11. The Court does not explicitly define them as assemblies but proceeds on that basis throughout.

*Participation:* The Court implicitly accepts that the applicants (as organisers of UMO Ilinden – PIRIN) were participants/organisers of the planned assemblies. This is implied by the entire analysis.

*Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses this sub-element under the heading "(a) Applicability of Article 11": "It has not been argued by the Government that the organisers and the participants in the events planned by UMO Ilinden – PIRIN had violent intentions. The Court sees no reason to hold otherwise. Article 11 is thus applicable (see Stankov and the United Macedonian Organisation Ilinden, cited above, §§ 76-78)." This is an explicit finding that the assembly was peaceful, making Article 11 applicable.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly addresses interference under heading "(b) Whether there were interferences": "The Government have not disputed that the first rally planned by UMO Ilinden – PIRIN was effectively banned by the authorities and that this ban constituted an interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly. The Court sees no reason to reach a different conclusion." Regarding the second rally, the Court concludes: "It may thus be concluded that the authorities interfered with the applicants' freedom of assembly with respect to both events." This is an explicit finding of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this under heading "(c) Justification for the interferences": "Concerning the lawfulness of the interferences, the Court notes that although the mayor did not provide detailed reasons for imposing the bans, he nevertheless referred to an alleged threat to public order, which under domestic law could justify an interference with the right to peaceful assembly... Moreover, the bans were imposed in accordance with the procedure laid down in the Meetings and Marches Act. It may thus be accepted that they were prescribed by law (see Stankov and the United Macedonian Organisation Ilinden, cited above, §§ 81 and 82)." The Court thus finds the prescribed-by-law requirement satisfied.

Regarding the sub-elements:

*Foreseeable:* The Court does not explicitly discuss foreseeability as a separate sub-element. The overall finding that the bans were "prescribed by law" is made without breaking down the analysis into the sub-components of accessibility, foreseeability, and safeguards. This is implied by the overall positive finding.

*Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element; implied by the overall positive finding.

*Safeguards:* Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element; implied by the overall positive finding.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court addresses legitimate aim under "(c) Justification for the interferences": "As to the legitimate aim for and the necessity of the interferences, the Court must scrutinise the reasons given by the national authorities... Thus, even assuming that the legitimate aims pursued were public safety and the prevention of disorder, it can hardly be concluded that the authorities gave relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the prohibitions of the rallies..." The Court does not definitively confirm a legitimate aim was pursued — it proceeds on an assumption ("even assuming") that public safety and prevention of disorder were the aims, but moves directly to finding the necessity test not satisfied. The Government had also cited national security, protection of rights and freedoms of others, and prevention of crime. The Court's approach is to assume the aims arguendo rather than conclusively finding them, so the conclusion on legitimate aim is effectively left open (null) while the analysis focuses on necessity.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly addresses necessity: "it can hardly be concluded that the authorities gave relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the prohibitions of the rallies, substantiating their finding that there was a risk to public order, and that the bans were thus necessary in a democratic society." The Court also notes: "Even if it was not unreasonable for the authorities to suspect that certain leaders of UMO Ilinden – PIRIN... harboured separatist views... such a probability could not per se justify their banning." The Court finds necessity NOT satisfied — the bans were not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Government mentioned positive obligations in its submissions: "they had complied with their positive obligations to guarantee the citizens' rights under Article 11 of the Convention." However, the Court does not explicitly address or spell out a positive obligation in its own assessment. The Court's analysis focuses entirely on the negative obligation (unjustified interference). Positive obligations are not discussed by the Court itself.
