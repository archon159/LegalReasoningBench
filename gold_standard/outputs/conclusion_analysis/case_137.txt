**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant.' The Court found that the reasons adduced by the national authorities to justify the interference were not 'relevant and sufficient,' and that the measures had a chilling effect on the applicants and others from attending protest rallies.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Yaroslav Belousov v. Russia, nos. 2653/13 and 60980/14, §§ 168-71, 4 October 2016).' The Court confirms this finding applies in the present case.

**ii) Participation:** Explicitly discussed. The Court examines whether the applicants personally could rely on Article 11: 'As to whether the applicants personally could rely on the provisions of Article 11, the Court recalls that peaceful participants in a demonstration marred by isolated acts of violence committed by other participants do not cease to enjoy the right to peaceful assembly.' The Court concludes: 'The Court therefore considers that the applicants enjoyed the protection of Article 11 in the present case.'

**iii) Peaceful assembly:** Explicitly discussed. The Court examines whether the applicants' conduct was peaceful: 'It does not transpire from any submissions that the applicants were among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's originally peaceful character. As regards breaking through the police cordon imputed to the applicants, they denied having pushed through the cordon, and even if the applicants found themselves beyond the cordon, there is no evidence that it had been the result of their deliberate effort... Apart from that, the applicants' behaviour remained strictly peaceful.' Conclusion: yes, the assembly was peaceful as regards the applicants.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court therefore considers that the termination of the demonstration, the applicants' arrest at the venue of the event and conviction for administrative offences constituted interference with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly.' The Court also notes the general principle: 'measures taken by the authorities during a rally, such as dispersal of the rally or the arrest of participants and penalties imposed for having taken part in a rally, amount to an interference.' Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly raises the question of lawfulness but then declines to examine it separately. The Court states: 'The applicants contested the lawfulness of the measures taken against them, alleging, in particular, that the police had had no grounds to give them orders to disperse. The Court considers that in this case the questions of compliance with the law and of the existence of a legitimate aim cannot be dissociated from the question whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society", and that there is no need to examine them separately.' Thus, the prescribed-by-law question is raised but merged into the necessity analysis. No separate conclusion is reached on foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards sub-elements.

**i) Foreseeable:** Not separately discussed; merged into necessity analysis. Conclusion: null.

**ii) Accessible:** Not separately discussed. Conclusion: null.

**iii) Safeguards:** Not separately discussed. Conclusion: null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly raises the question of legitimate aim but again declines to examine it separately, merging it with the necessity analysis: 'the questions of compliance with the law and of the existence of a legitimate aim cannot be dissociated from the question whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society", and that there is no need to examine them separately.' The Government had argued the measures were 'necessary "for the prevention of disorder or crime" and "for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others",' and the Court notes the authorities' submission that measures 'were specifically aimed at preventing illegal campsites from being set up.' However, no formal conclusion is reached on legitimate aim as a standalone element. The aims mentioned in the Government's submissions (prevention of disorder/crime, rights of others) are referenced but not formally accepted or rejected as legitimate aims.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed and is the central focus of the Court's merits analysis. The Court finds that the domestic courts 'failed to establish key facts in the administrative proceedings against the applicants, namely whether they had received an order from the police, or that it was lawful, or that the applicants had disobeyed it.' The domestic courts 'rejected the applicants' accounts of events supported by other evidence, including testimony of eyewitnesses, and relied on the police statements and reports' without adequate comparative assessment. The Court concludes: 'the Court is not satisfied that the reasons adduced by the national authorities to justify the interference under Article 11 of the Convention, namely the applicants' arrest at the site of the venue and their conviction for administrative offences, were relevant and sufficient.' Additionally, the measures had a chilling effect: 'the measures taken against the applicants must have had the effect of discouraging them and others from attending protest rallies and, more generally, from participating in open political debate.' Conclusion: no, the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'It has also found that the domestic authorities failed to discharge their positive obligation to ensure the peaceful conduct of that assembly (see Frumkin v. Russia, cited above, §§ 93-130). Noting that the present case contains no element capable of altering this conclusion, the Court adheres to that finding.' Thus, the Court explicitly finds a violation of the positive obligation, adopting the finding from Frumkin. Conclusion: yes, a positive obligation was identified and the government violated it.
