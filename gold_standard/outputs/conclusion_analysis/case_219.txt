**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final paragraph of the Court's assessment states: "Accordingly, Court concludes that the interference did not correspond to a pressing social need and was not necessary in a democratic society. There has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the planned event constitutes an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the judgment — the Court refers to "a demonstration" and "protest demonstrations" without questioning whether these fall within the scope of Article 11. The existence of an assembly is implied by the Court's acceptance that Article 11 applies.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss participation as a separate sub-element. It is implied: the applicant party applied to hold the demonstration and was refused authorisation. The Court treats the applicant party as the organiser/participant throughout.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court does not explicitly address the 'peaceful' character of the assembly as a threshold question. However, it is implied: the Court refers to "freedom of peaceful assembly" and notes the applicant party's "record of numerous protest demonstrations held in 2002 which were peaceful and at which no violent clashes had occurred." The Court also states: "there was nothing to suggest in the applicant party's actions that it intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities or with supporters of the governing party." This implies the assembly was peaceful within the meaning of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly discusses interference: "It is common ground between the parties, and the Court agrees, that the decision to reject the applicant party's application to hold a demonstration on 25 January 2004 amounted to 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11." The Government also accepted this: "The Government accepted that there has been an interference with the applicant's rights as guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention." Conclusion: yes, there was interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The Court explicitly notes that the parties do not dispute lawfulness: "The parties do not dispute that the interference was lawful within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention." The Government argued it was "prescribed by law, namely by the Assemblies Act." The Court accepts this without further analysis, moving on to proportionality. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed. The overall 'prescribed by law' element is treated as conceded/accepted. Conclusion: yes (not disputed), but the sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court explicitly declines to decide whether a legitimate aim was served: "The Court, for the reasons set out below, does not consider it necessary to decide this point and will focus on the proportionality of the interference." The Government argued national security and public order (prevention of disorder). The Court does not make a finding on legitimate aim. Conclusion: null (not decided). The aims_found list reflects what the Government argued (national security, public safety/prevention of disorder) but the Court made no finding.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
This is the central element explicitly discussed at length. The Court applies the proportionality test and finds the interference was not necessary. Key passages include:
- "the State's margin of appreciation was correspondingly narrow and that only very compelling reasons would have justified the interference with the CDPP's right to freedom of expression and assembly."
- "the Court is not convinced that the above reasons relied upon by the domestic authorities to refuse the applicant party authorisation to demonstrate could be considered relevant and sufficient within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention."
- "this reason for refusing authorisation could not be considered relevant and sufficient within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention too."
- "the interference did not correspond to a pressing social need and was not necessary in a democratic society."
Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court explicitly discusses positive obligations in the abstract: "There may thus be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of the right to freedom of association and assembly... even in the sphere of relations between individuals... Accordingly, it is incumbent upon public authorities to guarantee the proper functioning of a political party, even when it shocks or gives offence to persons opposed to the ideas or claims that it is seeking to promote. Their members must be able to hold meetings without having to fear that they will be subjected to physical violence by their opponents."

However, the Court does not separately conclude that the government violated a positive obligation — the violation is found under the negative obligation (the refusal to authorise the demonstration). The positive obligation discussion is used to reinforce the duty of the state to protect demonstrators from counter-demonstrators, and to explain why the risk of clashes could not justify refusal. The Court states: "even if there was a theoretical risk of violent clashes between the protesters and supporters of the Communist Party, it was the task of the police to stand between the two groups and to ensure public order." This implies a positive obligation to protect the assembly rather than ban it. The Court discusses positive obligations explicitly but does not make a separate finding of violation on this ground — the violation is found under necessity. Conclusion: discussed explicitly, but the conclusion on whether the positive obligation was violated is implied (yes, by failing to protect rather than ban, the state failed its positive obligation, though this is subsumed into the necessity analysis).
