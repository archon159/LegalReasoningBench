**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "There has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of both applicants on account of their convictions under Article 20.2 § 5 of the CAO."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

*Assembly exists:* The Court implicitly confirms that an assembly existed. It refers to "the event of 5 May 2018" as a "single public event," a "non-approved event," and a "meeting" and "demonstration." The Court also refers to "the right of peaceful assembly in a public place" and "the right to freedom of peaceful assembly." While not explicitly framed as a threshold question, the existence of an assembly is clearly implied throughout.

*Participation:* The Court explicitly states: "The applicants were not in charge of organising or running any assembly on 5 May 2018... They were convicted as ordinary demonstrators." This confirms their participation. It is explicitly discussed.

*Peaceful:* The Court explicitly states: "The event and their participation in it were peaceful." This is an explicit finding.

**(b) Interference**

The Court implicitly confirms interference. The opening line of the section states: "An 'interference' infringes Article 11 of the Convention unless it satisfies the requirements of paragraph 2 of that Article." The Court then proceeds to analyze the requirements of paragraph 2, which logically implies that an interference was found. Additionally, the Court refers to "The interferences in the form of prosecution for failing to comply with those orders to disperse" — this is an explicit reference to interference. The applicants were convicted (administrative convictions), which constitutes the interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" requirement under a dedicated sub-heading (Section A). It notes that the Public Events Act provided for hybrid public events, but leaves open the foreseeability question: "in view of the findings below concerning the 'necessity' of the convictions, in the present case the Court leaves open the question of whether it was foreseeable to the applicants that they would be prosecuted, in separate proceedings, under Article 20.2 of the CAO on account of their non-compliance with the police order to disperse issued after the meeting and what was then classified as a separate non-approved demonstration."

*Foreseeable:* Explicitly discussed but left open (no conclusion reached). The Court declines to decide this sub-element.

*Accessible:* Not discussed. The Court does not address accessibility of the legal provision.

*Safeguards:* Not discussed as a separate sub-element under "prescribed by law," though the absence of safeguards is touched upon under necessity (the courts did not ensure police acted compatibly with the right to assembly).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or confirm a legitimate aim. The section heading is "Pursuing a legitimate aim and necessary in a democratic society," but the Court's analysis focuses entirely on necessity. The Court states: "Even assuming that the reasons relied on by the courts were relevant, they were not sufficient to show that the convictions were 'necessary in a democratic society.'" The phrase "even assuming" suggests the Court leaves the legitimate aim question open rather than confirming it. No specific legitimate aim is identified or endorsed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed. The Court finds that the necessity requirement was NOT met. Key passages:
- "The courts did not substantiate that there had been any real risk of disruption to ordinary life due to the actions of the applicants or their fellow protestors that, in the concrete circumstances, would have exceeded the level of minor disturbance that follows from normal exercise of the right of peaceful assembly in a public place."
- "The courts did not ascertain that, in exercising the discretion afforded to them by the domestic law by way of issuing orders to disperse, the police had acted in a manner compatible with the essence of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly..."
- "Nor does it appear that the courts in the CAO cases struck a fair balance between those interests, on the one hand, and those of the applicants in exercising their right to freedom of peaceful assembly, on the other."
- "The interferences in the form of prosecution for failing to comply with those orders to disperse were not justified with reference to specific and averred substantive grounds."
- "Even assuming that the reasons relied on by the courts were relevant, they were not sufficient to show that the convictions were 'necessary in a democratic society' in the circumstances of the case."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (interference with the right). This element is not discussed.
