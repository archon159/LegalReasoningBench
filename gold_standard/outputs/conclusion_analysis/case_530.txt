**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to find a violation of Article 11, which only protects peaceful assemblies, without any suggestion that the assemblies were not peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The opening paragraph states: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court explicitly states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" confirms that interference is found. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed. Given that the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis and finds a violation on that basis, the prescribed-by-law element is not_discussed (though one might argue it is implied as satisfied since the Court proceeds to the necessity stage, but the Court does not make this reasoning explicit).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. No aims are named. The element is not discussed. As with prescribed by law, the Court's structure implies it may have been assumed satisfied (since the Court reaches necessity), but no explicit or clearly implied finding is made.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the interference was not justified), leading to the violation. The Court references its case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) as the basis for this conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is not discussed.
