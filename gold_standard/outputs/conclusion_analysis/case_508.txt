**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court explicitly concludes that Article 11 has been violated in the final paragraph of the merits section (the conclusion paragraph under section B.2.(iii)):

"In view of the foregoing, the Court concludes that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant in applications nos. 29356/19 and 31119/19."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the events qualify as 'assemblies' under Article 11. However, it is implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to the applicants' intended 'public events,' a 'picket,' and a 'public gathering,' and proceeds directly to analyze interference, which logically presupposes that the events fall within the scope of Article 11. The Court states: "The applicants intended to hold public events at specific locations" and treats the entire matter as falling under Article 11 without questioning whether an assembly exists. This is implied.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss participation as a separate element. However, it is implied throughout — the applicants are described as organizers who notified authorities and sought approval for their events. The Court's entire analysis presupposes their participation/attempted participation.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court does not explicitly address whether the assemblies were peaceful. However, it is implied: the Court refers to the right of 'peaceful assembly' throughout, and in the section on Ms Astrakhantseva, it notes that simultaneous assemblies should not be prohibited 'provided they remain peaceful and do not pose direct threats of violence or serious danger to public safety.' There is no suggestion that the applicants' assemblies were anything other than peaceful. This is implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "It is undisputed by the parties that the authorities' refusals to approve the locations for the public events in question amounted to an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly. Such an interference will constitute a breach of Article 11 unless it was 'prescribed by law', pursued one or more legitimate aims under paragraph 2, and was 'necessary in a democratic society' to achieve those aims."

This is explicit, and the conclusion is affirmative (yes, there was interference).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court discusses lawfulness but does not reach a definitive conclusion on it, instead moving on to necessity as the more conspicuous problem. The relevant passage states: "With regard to the lawfulness of the interference, the Court has previously asserted that domestic law granted an excessively broad discretion to the executive authorities in proposing a change of location for public events, these powers often being used in an arbitrary and discriminatory way... While the Supreme Court Ruling provided welcome clarifications for the judiciary... further measures will be necessary to address the general issues identified earlier. However, given that a more conspicuous problem arises with respect to the necessity of the interference, the Court will not limit its examination under Article 11 of the Convention in the present case to the lawfulness of the interference only."

The Court thus raises concerns about lawfulness but declines to make a definitive finding, proceeding instead to necessity. The overall 'prescribed by law' element is explicitly discussed but left without a clear yes/no conclusion — the Court signals problems but does not formally find a violation on this ground alone.

**i) Foreseeable?**
The Court's reference to 'excessively broad discretion' and 'arbitrary and discriminatory' use of powers implicitly raises foreseeability concerns, but no explicit sub-analysis is conducted. This is implied at best.

**ii) Accessible?**
Not discussed.

**iii) Sufficient safeguards against abuse?**
The Court's reference to 'excessively broad discretion' and the need for 'further measures' implies concern about safeguards, but this is not explicitly analyzed as a sub-element. This is implied.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "The Court is prepared to accept the Government's argument that the interference in question pursued the legitimate aims of ensuring public safety and protecting the rights and freedoms of others (see paragraph 46 above), even if in Mr Pleshkov's case the concerns related to public safety were rather vague."

The aims found are: public safety and protection of the rights and freedoms of others. The conclusion is affirmative (yes, legitimate aim established).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element of the Court's analysis and is explicitly and extensively discussed.

For Mr Pleshkov: "the domestic courts failed to assess specific facts that allegedly made it impossible to hold the 'picket' of twenty participants at the chosen location... There is no indication in the domestic courts' decisions that the security concerns clearly outweighed the adverse impact of such an exclusion on the event organiser's interests... the domestic courts paid little attention to the importance of the event's intended location... they failed to examine whether holding the 'picket' in Lermontov Park would have fundamentally altered the character of the protest... the domestic courts cannot be said to have applied standards which were in conformity with the principles embodied in Article 11 of the Convention... and to have struck a fair balance between the competing interests."

For Ms Astrakhantseva: "the authorities, including the domestic courts, failed to propose acceptable alternative arrangements to the applicants, such as a non-peak hour time slot or an alternative venue suitable for conveying the message... They have thus failed to provide 'relevant and sufficient reasons' to justify the restrictions imposed on the applicants in exercising their right to freedom of assembly. In such circumstances, these restrictions cannot be said to have been necessary in a democratic society."

The conclusion is negative (no, the interference was not necessary in a democratic society — i.e., the necessity test was not satisfied).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not frame its analysis explicitly in terms of 'positive obligations' under Article 11. However, the judgment does contain language that implies positive obligations on the state — particularly the obligation to make good-faith efforts to find alternative arrangements. For example: "it was incumbent on the domestic courts to assess whether the alternative location offered to Mr Pleshkov, Lermontov Park, was well suited for his 'picket' to have a meaningful impact"; "the authorities should make good-faith efforts to ensure that any alternative location allows the assembly to still take place within 'sight and sound' of the target audience"; "it was incumbent upon the authorities to at least consider a solution that would have allowed both events to proceed peacefully"; "the authorities adopted a formalistic attitude and made no genuine efforts to explore in a timely manner potential solutions for accommodating the applicants' gathering."

While these passages describe obligations on the state to facilitate assembly, the Court frames them within the necessity analysis rather than as a standalone positive obligation analysis. The positive obligation dimension is implied rather than explicitly labeled as such. Given that the Court found these obligations were not met, the conclusion on positive obligation (to the extent it is implied) is that the government violated it.
