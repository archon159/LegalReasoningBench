**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the Court's assessment section: "In these circumstances, the Court finds a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

Additionally, the reasoning leading to this conclusion is found in the preceding paragraphs: "the Court concludes that the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations" and "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court implicitly accepts that assemblies took place. The text refers throughout to "demonstrations of 12 and 26 January 2013" and treats them as assemblies without questioning whether they qualify as such. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

*ii) Participation:* The Court implicitly accepts that the applicant participated. The text states "the demonstrations in which he participated" and refers to "the applicant's arrests and convictions," implying participation is not in dispute. This is implied.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies. In the assessment section: "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention." The Government also submitted that "The demonstrations had been dispersed in a fairly peaceful manner," and the applicant submitted that "both demonstrations had been intended to be peaceful and had been held in a peaceful manner." The Court's reference to "unauthorised peaceful demonstration" confirms it accepts the assemblies were peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court implicitly accepts that there was interference. The dispersal of demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions are treated throughout as interferences with Article 11 rights. The Court states: "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies." This is implied rather than explicitly framed as an "interference" finding, but it is clearly treated as such.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" element. In the assessment: "In such circumstances the Court is ready to accept that the dispersal of the demonstrations of 12 and 26 January 2013 was lawful (compare Ibrahimov and Others v. Azerbaijan...)." The Court examines whether the demonstrations qualified as "spontaneous assemblies" under Article 5 § IV of the Law on Freedom of Assembly (which would have exempted them from prior notification requirements), and concludes that the applicant failed to substantiate this claim. The Government also submitted that "the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly was precise and foreseeable."

*i) Foreseeable:* The Government explicitly argued that "the domestic legislation regulating freedom of assembly was precise and foreseeable." The Court's acceptance that the dispersal was "lawful" implies it accepted foreseeability, though it does not separately analyze this sub-element in detail. This is implied.

*ii) Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed. The Court's acceptance of lawfulness implies accessibility, but this sub-element is not separately addressed. Implied.

*iii) Safeguards:* Not discussed at all. Not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued "that the dispersal of both demonstrations had been necessary in the interests of national security, for the prevention of disorder or crime, and had been proportionate to the aims pursued." The Court does not explicitly endorse or reject these aims — it proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without making a formal finding on legitimate aim. The aims are mentioned in the parties' submissions but the Court does not explicitly conclude on this element. Given that the Court proceeds to necessity, legitimate aim is implied to be accepted (or at least assumed arguendo). The aims mentioned are national security, prevention of disorder, and prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is explicitly and centrally discussed. The Court states: "Turning to the question whether it was necessary in a democratic society to disperse the demonstrations of 12 and 26 January 2013 and to convict the applicant..." It concludes: "the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations" and "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention." Furthermore: "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies... undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly." The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), leading to the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not spell out a positive obligation for the government in this case. The analysis is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (i.e., the state's duty not to interfere). Not discussed.
