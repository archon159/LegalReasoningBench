**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. This is stated in the final paragraphs of the merits section:

'Having regard to the foregoing considerations, the Court finds that the dispersal of the applicant's demonstration was necessary in a democratic society and cannot be regarded as having been a disproportionate measure in order to achieve the legitimate aims pursued. Accordingly, there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that there was an assembly. The Government 'did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the dispersal of the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of the applicant's rights under that provision. The Court sees no reason to hold otherwise.' This implies the existence of an assembly.

**(ii) Participation:** Similarly implied. The applicant's participation is not disputed. The text notes 'the applicant joined them at about 7 p.m.' and the Court proceeds on the basis that she participated in the assembly.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The assembly is treated as peaceful throughout. The Court refers to 'the peaceful demonstration in which she had participated' in the complaint section, and the entire analysis proceeds on the basis that the assembly was peaceful (the dispersal was 'solely because of the absence of the requisite prior notice, without any illegal conduct by the participants'). The Court does note that the events 'originated in an illegal demonstration' at Erzsébet Bridge, but the Kossuth Square demonstration itself is treated as peaceful for Article 11 purposes.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: 'The Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the dispersal of the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of the applicant's rights under that provision. The Court sees no reason to hold otherwise.' Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed: 'There was no dispute between the parties that the restriction imposed on the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly was based on section 14 of the Assembly Act, the wording of which is clear. Therefore, the requirement of lawfulness was satisfied.'

**(i) Foreseeable:** Implied by the statement that 'the wording of which is clear.' The Court does not separately analyze foreseeability but the reference to the clarity of the wording implies foreseeability is satisfied.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court simply notes the law exists and its wording is clear, but does not separately address accessibility. This is implied as satisfied given the overall conclusion that lawfulness was satisfied.

**(iii) Safeguards:** Explicitly discussed in the necessity section (though framed there): 'Moreover, the Court observes that, at the material time, no authorisation was required in Hungary for the holding of public demonstrations; however, the notification of the police was required seventy-two hours prior to the event. If the police decided to ban a demonstration, the organisers could seek judicial review within three days. Therefore, the Court is satisfied that there were procedural safeguards in place preventing unreasonable restrictions on freedom of assembly.' This is discussed explicitly, though placed within the necessity analysis rather than the prescribed by law section. Conclusion: yes, safeguards exist.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: 'In the light of these considerations, the Court is satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others.' The Government had submitted that restrictions served 'to protect the rights of others, for example the right to freedom of movement or the orderly circulation of traffic' and also the prevention of disorder. Aims found: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court conducts a detailed proportionality analysis:
- It reiterates that prior notification requirements do not normally encroach on the essence of the right.
- It acknowledges the Bukta principle for spontaneous demonstrations but finds it inapplicable here because no special circumstances warranted an immediate response: 'the facts of the instant case do not disclose such special circumstances to which the only adequate response was an immediate demonstration.'
- It notes procedural safeguards were in place.
- It notes the disorderly character of the combination of events.
- It notes the demonstrators had several hours to manifest their views before dispersal.
- Conclusion: 'the Court finds that the dispersal of the applicant's demonstration was necessary in a democratic society and cannot be regarded as having been a disproportionate measure in order to achieve the legitimate aims pursued.' Conclusion: yes, the interference was necessary (i.e., the necessity test is satisfied from the government's perspective, meaning no violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The positive obligation dimension is mentioned but not as the primary focus. The Government raised it: 'freedom of peaceful assembly could not be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere. On certain occasions, positive measures had to be taken in order to ensure that an assembly was peaceful. The three-day time-limit was therefore necessary to enable the police, inter alia, to co-ordinate with other authorities...' The Court also references it indirectly: 'the prior notification scheme was not an instrument for arbitrary use by the public authorities but a tool for the fulfilment of the positive obligations of the State, namely the protection of the rights of others.' However, the Court does not separately analyze whether the State violated any positive obligation toward the applicant. The positive obligation framing is used to justify the notification requirement, not to find a separate violation. This is implied rather than explicitly resolved as a standalone element.
