**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court explicitly finds a breach of Article 11, relying on its prior leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finding no new facts or arguments to depart from those conclusions.

**Question 2: Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

- **(i) Assembly exists:** Not explicitly discussed. However, the Court's reference to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" and the description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies that an assembly existed. This is implied.

- **(ii) Participation:** Not explicitly discussed. The description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies their participation. This is implied.

- **(iii) Peaceful:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without questioning whether the assemblies were peaceful. Given that the Court applies the full Article 11 framework and finds a violation on necessity grounds, it is implied that the assemblies were peaceful (otherwise they would fall outside Article 11's protection). This is implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the introductory paragraph describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing "prescribed by law," this element is not discussed. However, one could argue it is implied (the Court would not reach necessity if it had found the interference was not prescribed by law), but the reasoning is so compressed that it is safer to treat it as not_discussed, with the conclusion being null. Given the Court's approach of relying on leading cases and finding the interference not necessary, the "prescribed by law" sub-elements are not_discussed.

- **(i) Foreseeable:** not_discussed
- **(ii) Accessible:** not_discussed
- **(iii) Safeguards:** not_discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss a legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. The legitimate aim element is not discussed. However, since the Court reaches the necessity stage, it is implied that a legitimate aim was accepted (otherwise the analysis would have stopped earlier). The specific aim is not identified in this text, so aims_found is empty.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not satisfied, meaning the interference was disproportionate. The conclusion is "no" (the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society, i.e., the necessity test was failed by the government).

**(f) Positive obligation**
