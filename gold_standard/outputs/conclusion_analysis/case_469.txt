**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section: 'There has accordingly been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court found that while there was an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly, the measures taken were not disproportionate to the legitimate aims pursued, and the authorities did not overstep their margin of appreciation.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

The scope elements are not explicitly discussed as a formal threshold question, but they are implied throughout the judgment. The Court proceeds on the basis that there was an assembly, that the applicants participated in it, and that it was peaceful.

- **Assembly exists:** Implied. The Court refers throughout to 'the demonstration in which they had participated' and 'the applicants' demonstration,' treating the existence of an assembly as given. The applicants 'gathered at that location and remained there for more than two months' displaying 'placards and banners.' This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a threshold question.

- **Participation:** Implied. The Court refers to 'the demonstration in which they had participated' and notes that 'none of the protesters was brought to liability in connection with his participation in the demonstration.' Participation is assumed throughout.

- **Peaceful:** Implied. The Court notes that 'where participants do not engage in acts of violence it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful assemblies.' The applicants' act of protest 'consisted of displaying placards criticising the authorities for their unlawful actions' and the protesters 'had not chanted any slogans or made any other noise.' The assembly is treated as peaceful throughout, though this is not formally analyzed as a threshold question.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'Turning to the present case, the Court finds that the authorities' refusal to approve the location chosen by the applicants, the judicial decision declaring the demonstration unlawful, the order to disperse and the subsequent dismantling of the protesters' tents by the police constituted an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly.' The Court also provides an extensive general discussion of what constitutes interference, noting that it 'does not need to amount to an outright ban, legal or de facto, but can consist of various other measures taken by the authorities' and that 'an order to change the time or the place of the assembly may constitute an interference as well.' Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed, but only in a summary/concessive manner. The Court states: 'It is not contested that the interference was "prescribed by law" and "pursued a legitimate aim"...' The Court does not analyze the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) individually, as the matter is uncontested. Conclusion: yes, prescribed by law, but the sub-elements are not individually discussed.

- **Foreseeable:** Not discussed individually (subsumed in the uncontested finding).
- **Accessible:** Not discussed individually.
- **Safeguards:** Not discussed individually.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed, again in a concessive/summary manner. The Court states: 'It is not contested that the interference was "prescribed by law" and "pursued a legitimate aim", that of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others, for the purposes of Article 11 § 2.' The aims identified are prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others. Conclusion: yes, legitimate aim found.

Aims found: prevention_of_disorder, rights_of_others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court sets out the general standard: 'An interference will be considered "necessary in a democratic society" for a legitimate aim if it answers a "pressing social need" and, in particular, if it is proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued and if the reasons adduced by the national authorities to justify it are "relevant and sufficient".' The Court then applies this to the facts, noting that the authorities showed tolerance: the protesters remained for more than two months, the police did not use force, the protesters were able to re-erect tents and display placards, and none was brought to liability. The Court concludes: 'the applicants' demonstration lasted sufficiently long for them to express their position of protest and to draw the attention of the public to their concerns' and 'it cannot be said that the authorities overstepped the margin of appreciation afforded to them in that particular sphere or that the measures taken against the applicants' demonstration were disproportionate to the legitimate aims pursued.' Conclusion: yes, the interference was necessary (i.e., the government's measures were proportionate and did not violate Article 11).

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The Court does not frame any part of its analysis in terms of a positive obligation on the state to facilitate or protect the assembly. The analysis is entirely in terms of negative obligations (whether the state's interference was justified). Conclusion: not discussed.
