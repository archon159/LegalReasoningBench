**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's explicit finding of an "interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" logically implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated (as "organisers and participants of public assemblies," as stated in the opening paragraph), and the assembly must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

The opening paragraph does describe the applicants as "organisers and participants of public assemblies," which implies the existence of assemblies and participation. The peaceful nature is implied by the Court's application of Article 11 without qualification.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies, and their conviction for administrative offences." The Court then confirms: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" (plural) confirms that the Court treats the governmental acts — arrest, dispersal, and conviction — as interferences with Article 11 rights. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its leading case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). Since the Court finds a violation at the necessity stage, it had no need to address prescribed by law. These elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court references its case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and its leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov), finding no fact or argument capable of reaching a different conclusion. The necessity element is explicitly discussed and answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary, hence a violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is not discussed at all.
