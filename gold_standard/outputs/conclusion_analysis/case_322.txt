**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is established in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

- 'The domestic courts found the applicant guilty of non-compliance with a lawful police order without analysing whether the police had in fact given him any order prior to having forced him into their car, and, if so, what that order had been and whether it could be regarded lawful. This is a sufficient indication for the Court of a breach of the applicant's right to freedom of assembly under Article 11 of the Convention.'
- 'There has therefore been a violation of that provision.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. It refers to 'his participation in the protest against the construction works in the park' and notes that 'both the applicant's presence at the construction site and his apprehension by the police were directly related to his participation in the protest.' The protest is treated as an assembly within the meaning of Article 11. This is explicit.

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly addresses the applicant's participation: 'both the applicant's presence at the construction site and his apprehension by the police were directly related to his participation in the protest against the construction works in the park.' This is explicit.

*iii) Peaceful assembly:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly: 'Contrary to the Government's submissions, it was not alleged that the applicant had manifested any violent behaviour prior to his apprehension by the police. He was therefore protected by the Article 11 safeguards (see Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania [GC], no. 37553/05, § 94, ECHR 2015, with further references).' The Court also notes that the absence of prior authorisation does not remove the protection: 'That conclusion stands even in the absence of prior authorisation of the protest action, given that an unlawful situation, such as the staging of a demonstration without prior authorisation where such authorisation is required under domestic law, does not per se justify an infringement of freedom of assembly.' This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. It notes that 'the applicant's forceful removal from the public assembly on 20 April 2013, his ensuing deprivation of liberty for several hours and his conviction for an administrative offence' are the acts complained of, and it treats the apprehension and conviction as directly related to his participation in the protest. The Court states: 'both the applicant's presence at the construction site and his apprehension by the police were directly related to his participation in the protest.' The interference is thus explicitly established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law in the sense of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. However, the Court's finding of a violation is grounded in the domestic courts' failure to analyse whether a lawful police order was actually given: 'The domestic courts found the applicant guilty of non-compliance with a lawful police order without analysing whether the police had in fact given him any order prior to having forced him into their car, and, if so, what that order had been and whether it could be regarded lawful.' This touches on the lawfulness of the interference but does not systematically address the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) of the 'prescribed by law' test. The Court shortcuts the analysis and finds a violation without going through the full three-part justification test. Therefore, 'prescribed by law' as a formal sub-test is not discussed, though the lawfulness issue is implicitly raised.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. The analysis is cut short after establishing that the domestic courts failed to properly examine the lawfulness of the police order. No legitimate aim is identified or assessed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court does not explicitly discuss necessity/proportionality. The violation is found on the basis of the domestic courts' failure to analyse the lawfulness of the police order, without proceeding to a full proportionality analysis.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The complaint is framed as a negative interference by the state, and the Court's analysis focuses on that interference. No positive obligation is spelled out or assessed.

**Summary of findings:**
- The Court finds a violation of Article 11.
- Scope (assembly, participation, peaceful): explicitly discussed and affirmed.
- Interference: explicitly discussed and affirmed.
- Prescribed by law (and sub-elements): not formally discussed as a structured test; the lawfulness issue is raised implicitly in the context of the violation finding but the three sub-elements are not addressed.
- Legitimate aim: not discussed.
- Necessity: not discussed.
- Positive obligation: not discussed.
