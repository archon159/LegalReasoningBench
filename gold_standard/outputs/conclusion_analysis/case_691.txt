**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits assessment: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention as regards both applicants."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly confirms this by reference to prior case law: "The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Yaroslav Belousov v. Russia, nos. 2653/13 and 60980/14, §§ 168-72, 4 October 2016)." This is an explicit discussion.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly addresses participation: "It is common ground that they were not among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character." Also, in the admissibility section: "It was not disputed that both applicants had participated in the assembly." This is explicit.

**(iii) Peaceful character:** The Court explicitly discusses this sub-element. In the general principles section, it reiterates: "An individual does not cease to enjoy the right to peaceful assembly as a result of sporadic violence or other punishable acts committed by others in the course of the demonstration, if the individual in question remains peaceful in his or her own intentions or behaviour." Applied to the applicants: "it is common ground that they were not among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character. Article 11 is applicable to both applicants' complaints." The conclusion is that the assembly (as regards the applicants) was peaceful. This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: "The Court considers that the intervention by the police and their conduct vis-à-vis the applicants also constituted an interference with the applicants' rights under Article 11 of the Convention (see İzci v. Turkey, no. 42606/05, § 82, 23 July 2013)." This is explicit, and the conclusion is yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly accepts that the interference was prescribed by law: "The Government submitted in their observations that the Police Act allowed police officers to use force and special equipment in order to, inter alia, prevent a crime and suppress resistance shown towards a police officer. Thus, the Court accepts that the interference in question was 'prescribed by law'..." The conclusion is yes, but the Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. It simply accepts the Government's submission without further scrutiny. Therefore, the overall prescribed-by-law element is explicitly discussed (conclusion: yes), but the three sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are not individually discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly finds a legitimate aim: "...and pursued the legitimate aim of the prevention of disorder and crime." This is explicit. The aims found are prevention of disorder and prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses and negatively concludes on necessity: "Turning to the question of the 'necessity' of the interference, the Court notes the Government's submissions that the force was used to arrest those participants in the assembly who had acted violently and disobeyed the police. However, the Government have not submitted any explanations as to why force had to be applied in respect of the applicants, who were not arrested and did not engage in any acts of violence. In the light of its finding that the force used in respect of the applicants was unnecessary and excessive and thus contrary to Article 3 of the Convention (see paragraph 74 above), it finds that it was 'not necessary in a democratic society' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention. Moreover, it could have had a chilling effect and discouraged the applicants and others from taking part in similar public gatherings." The conclusion is that the interference was NOT necessary (i.e., the necessity requirement was not satisfied), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (interference by police). This element is not discussed.
