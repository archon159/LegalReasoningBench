**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final paragraph of the reasoning section:

"These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

Additionally, the Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope** — The scope elements (assembly exists, participation, peaceful nature) are not explicitly discussed in the reasoning. However, they are implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and to "restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events," which logically presupposes that there were assemblies, that the applicants participated (or tried to), and that the assemblies were peaceful. Without these preconditions being satisfied, the Court would not have proceeded to analyze interference and justification. The scope is therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference** — The Court explicitly states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used directly, confirming that an interference was found. This is explicit, though the Court does not separately reason through the interference element — it is treated as established by reference to the leading case Lashmankin and Others v. Russia.

**(c) Prescribed by law** — The Court explicitly addresses this element: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements..." This is an explicit (though brief) finding that the prescribed-by-law requirement was not satisfied in at least some cases. The Court does not separately discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards — it uses the umbrella phrase "quality of law" requirements. Since the Court references Lashmankin and Others v. Russia as the leading case where similar issues were found to violate Article 11, the sub-elements are implied but not individually spelled out in this text. The conclusion on prescribed_by_law is "no" (the requirement was not met), which is a negative finding for the government.

**(d) Legitimate aim** — The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society** — The Court explicitly states that the interferences "were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding, though without detailed reasoning (the Court relies on the Lashmankin precedent). The conclusion is that necessity was not satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately justify the interference).

**(f) Positive obligation** — The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is absent entirely.

**Summary of findings:**
- The Court finds a violation of Article 11.
- Scope is implied (yes) by the Court's proceeding to analyze the case on the merits.
- Interference is explicitly found (yes).
- Prescribed by law: explicitly found not to be satisfied (no) in at least some cases, with sub-elements implied but not individually discussed.
- Legitimate aim: not discussed.
- Necessity: explicitly found not to be satisfied (no).
- Positive obligation: not discussed.
