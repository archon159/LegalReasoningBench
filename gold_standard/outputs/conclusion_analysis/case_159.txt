**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of section B.2(c):

"In view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicant, the Court concludes that his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society."

Followed by: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Yaroslav Belousov, cited above, §§ 168-71)." The Court affirms this finding by reference to prior case law. Conclusion: yes.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Explicitly discussed, though briefly. The Court notes the applicant's prosecution and conviction arose from "participation in mass disorder" at the assembly, and the entire analysis concerns his personal involvement. The Court states: "As regards the applicant personally, it does not transpire from the description of his offences that he was among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character. Accordingly, he enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention." This implies his participation in the assembly is taken as given. Conclusion: yes (implied by the explicit discussion).

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court notes: "it does not transpire from the description of his offences that he was among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character. Accordingly, he enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court distinguishes the applicant personally from those who caused the assembly to lose its peaceful character, finding that as to him, the assembly retained its peaceful character for Article 11 purposes. Conclusion: yes (as to the applicant personally).

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court also finds that the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for participation in mass disorder constituted an interference with the exercise of the freedom of assembly." Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The overall "prescribed by law" question is explicitly discussed, though the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually broken down. The Court states: "The Court sees no reason to reach a different conclusion in this case (ibid., §§ 174-75). It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful..." This is a brief, cross-referenced finding. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually addressed in the text provided; the Court simply defers to its earlier Yaroslav Belousov judgment. Conclusion on overall prescribed by law: yes (explicit, by reference). Sub-elements: not individually discussed in this text (implied yes by the overall finding).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful, and pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others." Aims found: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, protection of the rights and freedoms of others. Conclusion: yes.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court examines whether the criminal conviction was "necessary in a democratic society" at length, considering: the nature of the applicant's acts (hitting a police officer once on the head and once on the body), the severity of the sentence (three years and seven months total, with three years for mass disorder), the lack of pre-existing intention to participate in mass disorder, the applicant's minor and incidental role, the failure of domestic courts to establish particular circumstances or rebut the applicant's allegations about police use of force, the chilling effect of the conviction, and the large-scale media coverage. The Court concludes: "the Court concludes that his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society." Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The positive obligation issue is raised and then explicitly set aside. In the admissibility section, the Court states: "the Court is not required to assess the authorities' alleged failure to discharge their positive obligation in respect of the conduct of the assembly at Bolotnaya Square as a separate issue under Article 11 of the Convention. It will examine whether the measures taken against the applicant personally complied with that provision in the light of all the material submitted by the parties concerning the planning, conduct and dispersal of the assembly." The Court thus declines to examine positive obligations as a separate issue, folding the relevant factual material into its assessment of the interference with the applicant's rights. Conclusion: null (not separately resolved — the Court explicitly declined to examine it as a standalone issue).
