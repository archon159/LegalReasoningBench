**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the overall conclusion section:

'There has accordingly been a breach of Article 11 of the Convention.'

This conclusion is grounded in the preceding analysis: 'Overall, the situation suggests that the interference, in addition to raising serious doubts concerning lawfulness, as noted in paragraph 48 above, failed to answer a pressing social need and to strike a fair balance between the competing interests.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In the interference section: 'In the present case, a peaceful manifestation organised by the applicant as a leader of the Chumatskiy Shlyakh Association was dispersed pursuant to a court order...' The Court also states in the general principles: 'the right to freedom of assembly enshrined in Article 11 of the Convention is a fundamental right in a democratic society... this right covers both private meetings and meetings in public places, whether static or in the form of a procession.' The assembly (a picket/manifestation) is explicitly acknowledged to exist.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes the applicant's participation: 'The applicant in the present case was the organiser and a participant of the protest.' This is stated in the admissibility section on victim status. The merits section also refers to 'a peaceful manifestation organised by the applicant as a leader of the Chumatskiy Shlyakh Association.'

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly treats the assembly as peaceful. In the interference section: 'a peaceful manifestation organised by the applicant.' In the necessity section: 'peaceful assemblies require the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance.' The Court never questions the peaceful nature of the assembly; indeed, the domestic courts' allegations of misconduct are treated as unsubstantiated.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'Those measures represent a clear case of interference with freedom of assembly guaranteed by Article 11. It remains to be seen whether the interference was justified under paragraph 2 of Article 11 of the Convention.' The measures were: dispersal of the picket pursuant to a court order, and an injunction prohibiting the Association from organising further pickets in the city for an indefinite period.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element but ultimately declines to make a definitive finding, instead proceeding to necessity. The overall conclusion is one of 'serious doubts' rather than a clear finding of non-compliance.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'the Court has doubts that, given the above-mentioned wording, the applicant could reasonably have foreseen that the procedure provided for by Article 182 would be applied for disbanding an on-going picket.' The Court also references prior findings in Vyerentsov and Shmushkovych that 'the law applicable to holding assemblies did not meet the foreseeability requirement.' Additionally, regarding the prohibition on 'pickets': 'the actual scope of the prohibition on holding "pickets" could be open to various interpretations and lacked the necessary precision to enable the applicant to regulate his future conduct.' Conclusion: doubted/no (but not definitively decided).

**(ii) Accessible:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The aforementioned Acts, which constituted the legal basis for the interference complained of, had been published and there is no dispute as to their accessibility to the general public.' Conclusion: yes (accessibility is not disputed).

**(iii) Safeguards:** Explicitly discussed. The Court notes that 'the general rules of procedure established by the CAJ contained a series of important safeguards inherent in the requirements of a fair trial' but then states: 'it cannot be discerned either from the texts of the domestic judgments, or from the Government's observations or other available material, that in the particular case at issue the aforementioned procedural safeguards were, in fact, put in use.' The Court notes the courts took allegations at face value without verifying facts, and the Court of Appeal gave no reasons for dismissing the applicant's arguments. Conclusion: no (safeguards existed in law but were not applied in practice).

**Overall prescribed by law:** The Court expresses 'serious doubts as to whether the interference complained about met the lawfulness requirement' but declines to decide this definitively: 'the Court does not find it necessary to decide whether the above considerations alone can serve as a basis for finding a violation of Article 11.' Conclusion: null (unresolved, serious doubts expressed).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'As is implied in the domestic judgments, the interference in question, which was based on the findings that the picket was disruptive, offensive and potentially dangerous, pursued legitimate aims: the prevention of disorder and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.' However, the Court immediately qualifies this: 'the Court, however, finds that the actual motives for the impugned measures of interference are open to question in light of the considerations expressed in paragraphs 51-56 below.' The Court thus implies the legitimate aim is questionable but proceeds to necessity without making a definitive negative finding on legitimate aim. The aims identified are: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds the interference was not necessary in a democratic society on multiple grounds:

1. The allegations of public danger were not substantiated: 'the judicial authorities did not explain, by reference to the particular circumstances of the relevant incidents, exactly how the protesters had been "breaching public order"... The conclusions that the protesters had "obstructed the passage of pedestrians" and "endangered road users" were likewise not supported by any factual references.'

2. 'the Court can only conclude that the interference complained of did not respond to any demonstrated risk of insecurity or disturbance and did not correspond to any "pressing social need".'

3. The measures were disproportionate: 'instead of looking for an accommodating solution, not only were the protesters dispersed, but a blanket ban was imposed on the defendant Association's ability to hold any other "pickets" on any street or square in the entire city of Vinnytsia for an undefined period of time. The Court fails to see any justification for such an overarching restriction.'

4. The political nature of the assembly warranted greater protection: 'public events related to political life should be given particular deference.'

5. 'the interference complained of did not strike a "fair balance" between the applicant's rights protected under Article 11 and the other competing interests at stake.' Conclusion: no (not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The Court frames the entire case as one of unjustified interference (negative obligation), not as a failure to protect assembly rights (positive obligation). The Court does note that 'peaceful assemblies require the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance' and that authorities should 'consider measures to minimise the disruption, while at the same time accommodating the organisers' legitimate interests,' but this is framed in the context of proportionality/necessity analysis rather than as a standalone positive obligation. Conclusion: not discussed.
