**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court explicitly finds a violation of Article 11 in the final paragraph of the merits assessment: "In these circumstances, the Court finds a violation of Article 11 of the Convention as regards both the demonstration of 20 October 2012 and the assembly of 6 May 2014."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court treats both events as assemblies/demonstrations without question. For the first case, it refers to "the demonstration of 20 October 2012" and for the second, "the assembly of 6 May 2014." This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a legal threshold question.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation: "his arrest and conviction following his participation in the demonstration of 20 October 2012" and "his arrest and conviction following his participation in the assembly of 6 May 2014." The Court also explicitly notes that "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov, §§ 63-64). This is explicitly discussed.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of both assemblies. For the first case, it notes "the lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration, which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov, § 61). For the second case, it refers to "an unauthorised peaceful assembly" and notes the authorities "failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful assembly was by itself protected by Article 11." This is explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. It refers to "the dispersal of the protests by the police and his arrest and conviction for an administrative offence" as the subject of the complaint. For the second case, it explicitly discusses "whether the dispersal of the assembly of 6 May 2014 was prescribed by law" and "whether the dispersal of the assembly of 6 May 2014 and the applicant's conviction were necessary in a democratic society." The interference is treated as established and explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" requirement, particularly for both cases.

For the first case, it notes "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov, § 55). It also notes "doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely, Article 310.1 of the CAO, invoked by the authorities" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov, §§ 56 and 62).

For the second case, the Court explicitly states: "In such circumstances the Court has doubts as to whether the dispersal of the assembly of 6 May 2014 was prescribed by law." However, it declines to resolve this definitively: "given that a more conspicuous problem arises with respect to the necessity of the interference, the Court considers that it is not appropriate to limit its examination under Article 11 of the Convention to the lawfulness of the interference only."

**(i) Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed. The Court notes "serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies" (first case, referencing Gafgaz Mammadov § 55). The applicant also argued that the domestic legislation "did not comply with principles of foreseeability and precision." The conclusion is negative (not foreseeable/concerns raised) but not definitively resolved for the second case.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed as a standalone sub-element. The applicant's submissions mention that the Constitution required only prior notification while a system of prior authorisation was applied in practice, which touches on accessibility, but the Court does not explicitly address accessibility as a separate sub-element. Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Explicitly discussed. The Court notes concerns about "the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed" (referencing Gafgaz Mammadov § 55), which directly addresses the adequacy of safeguards against abuse. The conclusion is negative (insufficient safeguards).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued in general terms that the dispersal "had been necessary in the interests of national security, for protection of the rights and freedoms of others, for protection of public safety and the prevention of disorder or crime." However, the Court does not explicitly accept or endorse any legitimate aim. The Court moves directly to necessity without confirming a legitimate aim. The legitimate aim element is not explicitly resolved by the Court — it is mentioned in the parties' submissions but the Court does not make a finding on it. Given the Court proceeds to necessity, a legitimate aim is implied to exist (or at least assumed arguendo), but the Court makes no explicit finding. The aims_found list reflects what the Government claimed: national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. For the second case: "Turning to the question whether the dispersal of the assembly of 6 May 2014 and the applicant's conviction were necessary in a democratic society, the Court notes that the issues raised by the applicant and the facts of the present case closely resemble those of the Gafgaz Mammadov case. Therefore, for the same reasons as those outlined in the Gafgaz Mammadov judgment, the Court concludes that the authorities in the present case have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the protest." The Court also notes the chilling effect: "The dispersal of the assemblies and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies... undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly." The conclusion is negative (not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the government. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (interference with the right). Not discussed.
