**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of section B.2(c): 'In view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicant, the Court concludes that his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Yaroslav Belousov, cited above, §§ 168-71).' Conclusion: yes.

**(ii) Participation:** Explicitly discussed. The Court examines the applicant's personal involvement: 'As regards the applicant personally, it does not transpire from the description of his offences that he was among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character. Accordingly, he enjoyed the protection of Article 11 of the Convention.' Conclusion: yes.

**(iii) Peaceful:** Explicitly discussed. The Court notes that the applicant was not among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression that deteriorated the assembly's peaceful character, and therefore he enjoyed Article 11 protection. The assembly itself had an initial peaceful character. The Court implicitly finds the applicant's participation was sufficiently peaceful to attract protection, even though some violence occurred later. Conclusion: yes (as to the applicant personally).

**(b) Interference:** Explicitly discussed. 'The Court also finds that the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for participation in mass disorder constituted an interference with the exercise of the freedom of assembly.' Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law:** Explicitly discussed at the overall level, though sub-elements are not individually broken down. The Court states: 'The Court sees no reason to reach a different conclusion in this case (ibid., §§ 174-75). It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful...' This is a reference to the Yaroslav Belousov analysis. The Court finds the interference was prescribed by law. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed in this judgment — they are addressed by reference to Yaroslav Belousov. Conclusion on overall prescribed by law: yes (implied from the finding of lawfulness). Sub-elements: not explicitly discussed in this text, implied yes.

**(d) Legitimate aim:** Explicitly discussed. 'It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful, and pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.' Aims found: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, rights of others. Conclusion: yes.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:** Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court examines the proportionality of the criminal conviction and sentence at length, ultimately concluding: 'the Court concludes that his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society.' Key passages include analysis of the applicant's minor role, the severity of the sentence, the chilling effect, and the lack of pressing social need. Conclusion: no (not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation:** The Court explicitly addresses this in the admissibility section, deciding NOT to examine it as a separate issue: 'the Court is not required to assess the authorities' alleged failure to discharge their positive obligation in respect of the conduct of the assembly at Bolotnaya Square as a separate issue under Article 11 of the Convention. It will examine whether the measures taken against the applicant personally complied with that provision in the light of all the material submitted by the parties concerning the planning, conduct and dispersal of the assembly.' So positive obligation is explicitly mentioned but not separately adjudicated — the Court folds the positive obligation question into the overall Article 11 analysis of the interference with the applicant personally. Conclusion: null (not separately resolved).
