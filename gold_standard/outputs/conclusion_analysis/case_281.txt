**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is found in the final paragraph of the merits section (under 'δ. Conclusion'):

'The Court accepts that the organisers of the demonstration at issue failed to comply with the regulations in force at the material time... However, taking into account the authorities' impatience in seeking to disperse the march and the manner in which the force was used, the Court considers that the intervention of the police was disproportionate and not necessary in a democratic society. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 exists, but it is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to 'the demonstration,' 'the march,' and 'the gathering' throughout, and proceeds to analyze the case under Article 11 without questioning whether an assembly existed. The existence of an assembly is implied by the entire structure of the analysis.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicants participated in the assembly, but it is implied. The Court notes: 'it appears from the police reports that the applicants joined the front line of demonstrators at about 4.40 p.m.' and 'the video footage shows that the police intervention began less than two minutes after the applicants joined the front lines.' This implies the Court accepted the applicants participated in the assembly.

**(iii) Peaceful character:** The Court explicitly discusses the peaceful character of the assembly. Under section (α) 'The conduct of the demonstrators,' the Court states: 'the Court considers that despite certain sporadic violent acts involving the throwing of stones – which were not committed by the applicants – the demonstration was mainly of a peaceful character before the intervention of the police.' The Court also reiterates the general principle that 'an individual does not cease to enjoy the right to peaceful assembly as a result of sporadic violence or other punishable acts committed by others in the course of the demonstration, if the individual in question remains peaceful in his or her own intentions or behaviour.' The Court notes: 'it is not disputed between the parties that the applicants did not engage in acts of violence.'

**(b) Interference:** The Court explicitly discusses and confirms the existence of an interference: 'It is not in dispute between the parties, and the Court accepts, that the intervention of the police to disperse the demonstration at issue constituted an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly.' This is found in section (i) 'Existence of an interference, its legal basis and the legitimate aims pursued.'

**(c) Prescribed by law:** The Court explicitly discusses this element and concludes affirmatively: 'The Court further accepts that the interference in question had a legal basis, namely sections 22 and 24 of Law no. 2911... It was thus "prescribed by law" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.' However, the Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse. The Court simply accepts the legal basis without further elaboration on these sub-elements, citing Oya Ataman v. Turkey. The sub-elements are therefore not explicitly discussed; the overall 'prescribed by law' conclusion is explicit but the sub-elements are not individually addressed.

**(d) Legitimate aim:** The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aims: 'The disputed measure may also be regarded as having pursued at least two of the legitimate aims set out in paragraph 2 of Article 11, namely the prevention of disorder and the protection of the rights of others.' The Government had also argued national security, public safety, and prevention of crime, but the Court only formally accepted prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:** The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. The Court concludes the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages include:
- On the conduct of demonstrators: 'the demonstration was mainly of a peaceful character before the intervention of the police.'
- On security considerations: 'the mere existence of some buildings belonging to the security forces along the road taken by the demonstrators cannot be considered, in itself, sufficient to justify the dispersal of the demonstration.'
- On disruption: 'the extent of the disruption to ordinary life caused by the demonstration at issue prior to its dispersal was not such as to justify such swift intervention by the police.'
- Conclusion: 'the Court considers that the intervention of the police was disproportionate and not necessary in a democratic society.'

**(f) Positive obligation:** The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the government in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of a negative obligation (i.e., whether the state's interference was justified). There is no mention of a positive duty to protect or facilitate the assembly.
