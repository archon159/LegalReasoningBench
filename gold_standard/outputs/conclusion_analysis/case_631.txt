**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "In these circumstances, the Court finds that the disciplinary measure taken against the applicant was not disproportionate and thus that there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

*Assembly exists:* The Court explicitly finds that the picket on 3 March 1999 constituted an assembly for purposes of Article 11. The Court states: "The Court further notes that the applicant's participation in the picket constituted the exercise of the right to peaceful assembly and therefore fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention." This is an explicit affirmative finding.

*Participation:* The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation in the picket. The parties did not dispute that "the applicant's absence from work on 3 March 1999, one of the grounds for her dismissal, was the direct result of her participation in the picket on 3 March 1999." Participation is explicitly confirmed.

*Peaceful:* The Court refers to the picket as a "protest action" and characterizes the applicant's participation as "the exercise of the right to peaceful assembly." The assembly is treated as peaceful, though this is not separately analyzed — it is implied by the Court's framing of the picket as falling within the scope of Article 11 as a peaceful assembly. The word "peaceful" is used but not separately analyzed as a sub-element.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: "Accordingly, the Court holds that there was an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly and that it must be determined whether the interference was justified." The interference was the disciplinary sanction (dismissal) imposed on the applicant, which was causally linked to her participation in the picket. The Court also notes that "the term 'restrictions' in paragraph 2 of Article 11 of the Convention is not limited to bans and refusals to authorise the exercise of Convention rights, but also embraces punitive measures taken after such rights have been exercised, including various disciplinary measures."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this element: "In the light of the foregoing, the Court finds that the disciplinary sanction was lawfully imposed on the applicant and thus the interference was 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." The Court notes that domestic courts found the dismissal was in accordance with domestic labour law, and that Ukrainian law provides a procedure for strikes with guarantees for employees. The applicant did not follow the strike procedure under Section 19 of the Resolution of Labour Disputes (Procedure) Act and did not challenge the conformity of that procedure with Article 11.

The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not separately and explicitly analyzed. The Court's conclusion that the interference was "prescribed by law" is reached without breaking down these sub-elements. The Court notes the existence of safeguards (immunity from disciplinary action for lawful strikes) implicitly, but does not separately analyze foreseeability or accessibility. These sub-elements are at best implied by the overall "prescribed by law" conclusion.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states: "The Court also considers that the interference pursued a 'legitimate aim,' which was the protection of the rights of the applicant's former employer under domestic labour law." This maps onto the aim of "protection of the rights and freedoms of others" under Article 11 § 2.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly addresses proportionality/necessity. It notes that the applicant's notice to local authorities did not indicate the planned duration of the picket or that she would be absent from work; that her two-hour absence caused serious disruption to workplace processes; and that even if a colleague agreed to replace her, this was insufficient to show she took all necessary steps to exercise her freedom of assembly with due respect to her employer's rights and interests. The Court concludes: "the Court finds that the disciplinary measure taken against the applicant was not disproportionate and thus that there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The necessity element is thus explicitly discussed and resolved in favor of the government (i.e., the interference was necessary/proportionate).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of whether the state's interference (through the employer's disciplinary action and the courts' endorsement of it) was justified. No positive obligation is identified or analyzed.
