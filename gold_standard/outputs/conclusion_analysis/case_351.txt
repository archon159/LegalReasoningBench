**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of the merits assessment: "In the light of the foregoing the Court concludes that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention as regards Mr Kasparov, Mr Tarasov and Mr Toropov."

The penultimate paragraph also states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the police's forceful intervention was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. It notes that the applicants were "walking in a big group of people shouting anti-government slogans" and that this constituted a "public demonstration." The Court also refers to the applicants as being "in the process of conducting an unauthorised rally" (Government's characterisation) or heading to an authorised public gathering. The assembly's existence is explicitly treated as given. The Court states: "irrespective of whether Mr Kasparov, Mr Tarasov and Mr Toropov were heading to an authorised public gathering, as they alleged, or were already in the process of conducting an unauthorised rally, as the Government claimed, their arrest constituted an interference with their right of peaceful assembly." This is explicit.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly addresses participation. The three applicants (Kasparov, Tarasov, Toropov) are found to have participated or attempted to participate. The admissibility section distinguishes them from the other applicants who denied participation: "in making their complaints under Article 10 and 11 these applicants denied that they had had any intention of taking part in a public assembly on 14 April 2007 ... They have therefore not made out a prima facie case of interference with their freedom of expression or freedom of assembly." For the three admitted applicants, participation is treated as established.

**iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful character of the assembly: "given the modest size of the group (estimated by the police as fifty to sixty persons) and the undeniably peaceful character of the march, the Court is not persuaded that the threat of the marchers penetrating the security area was imminent." The assembly is found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference. It states: "their arrest constituted an interference with their right of peaceful assembly, as did the ensuing administrative charges brought against them. The Court observes, moreover, that the Government did not dispute the existence of the interference with the right to peaceful assembly in the present case."

The Court also provides a general restatement of the law on what constitutes interference: "The term 'restrictions' in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an assembly and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not resolve the "prescribed by law" question as a standalone finding. It explicitly states: "the Court considers that in this case the issue of compliance with the law is indissociable from the question whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society'. It will therefore examine this issue below." The Court then folds the lawfulness analysis into the necessity/proportionality assessment. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed. The overall "prescribed by law" question is raised but not resolved separately — it is merged into the necessity analysis. Given that the Court ultimately finds a violation on necessity grounds without separately resolving lawfulness, the conclusion on "prescribed by law" is effectively null/unresolved. The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly accepts a legitimate aim: "the Court will accept that the applicants' arrest and their conviction of an administrative offence had pursued the legitimate aim of maintaining public order, as the Government claimed." The aim identified is the prevention of disorder / maintenance of public order, which corresponds to "prevention of disorder" under Article 11 § 2.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It finds that the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages:

- "It follows that the applicants were arrested and charged with administrative offences for the sole reason that the authorities perceived their demonstration to be unauthorised. The Court therefore concludes that the Government have failed to demonstrate that there existed a 'pressing social need' to arrest them."

- "the Court considers that in the instant case the police's forceful intervention was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court's reasoning includes: (1) an unlawful situation (staging a demonstration without prior authorisation) does not by itself justify an infringement of freedom of assembly; (2) where irregular demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, authorities must show a certain degree of tolerance; (3) the threat of marchers penetrating the security area was not imminent given the peaceful character and distance from Red Square; (4) the police had anticipated the rally and had sufficient resources to manage it without arrests; (5) other justifications (nuisance to traffic, etc.) were not reflected in domestic court reasoning.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). No positive obligation is spelled out or found violated.
