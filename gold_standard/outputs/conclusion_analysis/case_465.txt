**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits assessment:

- 'In view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicant, the Court concludes that his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and that it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society.'
- 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly refers to 'an authorised public assembly' and 'an authorised demonstration,' and discusses the events at Bolotnaya Square as a public assembly. This is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation: 'his acts... were committed during his participation in an authorised public assembly' and 'the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for acts he had committed in the course of the dispersal of the demonstration.' This is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**iii) Peaceful character:** The Court discusses the peaceful character in a nuanced way. It notes that 'the majority of whom were peaceful' and that the applicant's conduct 'appeared to be a spontaneous reaction to the force used by the police against the protesters.' The Court also notes that 'Ms. N... confirm[ed] that the applicant had been sporadically pulled out of the crowd by the police and that his initial behaviour had been peaceful.' The assembly is treated as initially peaceful and the applicant's involvement in violence is treated as incidental and reactive. This is explicitly discussed and the assembly is treated as within the scope of Article 11 protection (peaceful assembly), though the Court acknowledges some violence occurred.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed and affirmed: 'The Court considers that the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for acts he had committed in the course of the dispersal of the demonstration constituted an interference with the exercise of his freedom of assembly (see, for similar reasoning, Yaroslav Belousov v. Russia...).' The Government had argued there was no interference, but the Court rejected this.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court addresses this briefly and affirmatively by reference to prior case law: 'the Court is also satisfied that the applicant's prosecution was provided for by law' (citing Yaroslav Belousov §§ 174-76 and Barabanov v. Russia § 70). This is explicitly discussed as a composite finding, but the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually broken down. However, the Court does discuss the lack of procedural safeguards in the domestic proceedings in the context of necessity rather than 'prescribed by law': 'it cannot be said that the applicant enjoyed a procedural safeguard against arbitrary interference with the right to peaceful assembly in the domestic proceedings.' This safeguards discussion appears under necessity, not under 'prescribed by law.' Therefore, prescribed_by_law overall is explicitly discussed and found satisfied; the sub-elements of foreseeability and accessibility are not individually discussed (implied yes by the overall finding); safeguards are discussed but in the necessity context rather than the prescribed-by-law context.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed and affirmed: 'the Court is also satisfied that the applicant's prosecution was provided for by law and pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.' The aims found are: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds the conviction was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages:
- The domestic courts failed to examine the circumstances of the applicant's arrest and relied on police statements given years after the events.
- The courts dismissed eyewitness testimony from Ms. N. without explanation.
- 'it cannot be said that the applicant enjoyed a procedural safeguard against arbitrary interference with the right to peaceful assembly in the domestic proceedings.'
- The conviction under Article 212 (mass disorder) was more severe than under Article 318 (violence against official) and the courts did not distinguish which acts constituted mass disorder as opposed to violence against officials.
- The applicant's conduct 'was sporadic in nature and appeared to be a spontaneous reaction to the force used by the police against the protesters.'
- 'the reasons advanced by the Government could not justify the sentencing of the applicant by a "pressing social need".'
- The chilling effect on political participation.
- 'his criminal conviction was a measure that was disproportionate to the legitimate aims... and... therefore not necessary in a democratic society.'

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly frame any finding in terms of a positive obligation. The analysis is entirely framed as a negative obligation (interference by the State through prosecution and conviction). Positive obligations are not discussed.
