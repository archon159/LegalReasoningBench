**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies" and proceeds to analyze interference with "the applicants' freedom of assembly." This implies that the Court accepted that there were assemblies, that the applicants participated (as organisers and/or participants), and that the assemblies were peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: The text refers to "public assemblies" without questioning whether they qualify as assemblies. Implied affirmative.
- **scope_participation**: The applicants are described as "organisers and/or participants of public assemblies." Implied affirmative.
- **scope_peaceful**: Not explicitly discussed, but the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 protection, implying the assemblies were peaceful. Implied affirmative.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and earlier the complaint is described as concerning "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." The existence of interference is thus explicitly acknowledged.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. None of the three sub-elements are addressed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches a negative conclusion on necessity (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding. The Court relies on its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no fact or argument capable of leading to a different conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is entirely absent.
