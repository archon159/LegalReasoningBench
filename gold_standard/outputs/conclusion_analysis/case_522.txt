**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court explicitly finds a breach of Article 11, grounding this in its prior case-law (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and concluding that the interferences were not necessary in a democratic society.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, and whether the assembly was peaceful). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which logically presupposes that (i) assemblies existed, (ii) the applicants participated or tried to participate, and (iii) the assemblies were within the protective scope of Article 11 (i.e., peaceful). The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin and Navalnyy and Yashin — which involved peaceful assemblies — further implies that the assemblies were peaceful.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes ("public assemblies" mentioned in the first paragraph)
- scope_participation: implied yes (applicants described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (no suggestion of violence; cases treated as falling within Article 11 protection)

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze whether there was an interference in a separate doctrinal step, but it is clearly implied. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" presupposes that an interference was found. Additionally, the first paragraph describes "disproportionate measures taken against them... namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences," which are the interferences in question. This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate doctrinal step.

- interference: implied yes

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, which is the basis for the violation finding. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it did not need to address prescribed by law.

- prescribed_by_law: not discussed
- prescribed_by_law_foreseeable: not discussed
- prescribed_by_law_accessible: not discussed
- prescribed_by_law_safeguards: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim as a separate step.

- legitimate_aim: not discussed, aims_found: []

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only canonical element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the explicit basis for the violation finding. The Court references its case-law on proportionality (Oya Ataman v. Turkey, Hyde Park and Others v. Moldova) and the leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) to support this conclusion.

- necessity: explicit, conclusion: no (not necessary in a democratic society)

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). No positive obligation is spelled out or found violated.

- positive_obligation: not discussed
