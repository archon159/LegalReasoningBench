**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court finds violations of Article 11 in respect of all three demonstrations. The relevant passages are:

- Demonstration of 30 August 2007: 'For these reasons, the Court concludes that the arrest of the four men was disproportionate and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the demonstration of 30 August 2007.'

- Demonstration of 4 September 2007: 'For these reasons, the Court concludes that the arrest of Ghenadie and Oleg Brega and the fines imposed upon them were disproportionate and thus were not necessary in a democratic society. Accordingly, there has also been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the demonstration of 4 September 2007.'

- Demonstration of 10 September 2007: 'For those reasons, the Court finds that the arrest of Mr Juraveli, Mr Oleg Brega and Mr Hristea-Stan on 10 September 2007 was disproportionate and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society. Accordingly, there has also been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the demonstration of 10 September 2007.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly define or discuss whether the gatherings constitute 'assemblies' in the abstract sense, but it treats them as such throughout. The existence of demonstrations/assemblies is taken as given and implied by the entire analysis. For example, the Court refers to 'the demonstration of 30 August 2007,' 'the demonstration of 4 September 2007,' and 'the demonstration of 10 September 2007' throughout, treating them as assemblies without question. This is implied.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
This is explicitly discussed in the admissibility section regarding Mr Juraveli and the demonstration of 4 September 2007: 'If Mr Juraveli did not attend the demonstration, there could be no interference with his Article 11 rights. It would stretch the concept of a victim too far to find that it applied to anyone who argued that he would have gone to a demonstration had it gone ahead.' The Court upheld the Government's objection that Mr Juraveli was not a victim for the 4 September demonstration because he did not participate. For the other applicants and other demonstrations, participation is implied by the Court's finding of interference.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies, particularly for the 10 September demonstration: 'For the most part, the video shows that the demonstration took place in the most peaceful manner possible and with minimal disruption to the public. It was well conducted and restrained. Although slogans were shouted in the course of the demonstration, this is a well established feature of peaceful protest.' For the 30 August demonstration, the Court notes: 'Even if their signs and chants were calculated to insult the Minister, he was clearly a public figure of some prominence in Moldova. In a democratic society, greater tolerance should be shown to those expressing opinions which are critical of such figures, even if those opinions are expressed inarticulately or intemperately.' The peaceful nature is also implied for the 4 September demonstration: 'Their demonstration had been peaceful and threatened no one.' The Court also quotes from Sergey Kuznetsov: 'any measures interfering with the freedom of assembly and expression other than in cases of incitement to violence or rejection of democratic principles... do a disservice to democracy.' The peaceful nature is thus explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly finds interference for all three demonstrations:

- 30 August: 'the Court considers that the arrest of Mr Ghenadie Brega, Mr Oleg Brega, Mr Hristea-Stan and Mr Juraveli prevented the demonstration from taking place. Consequently, there is no doubt that there has been an interference with Article 11 of the Convention.'

- 4 September: 'the Court finds that the arrest, detention and fining of the applicants amounted to an interference with their rights under Article 11.'

- 10 September: 'The Court is similarly persuaded that the arrest of Mr Juraveli, Mr Oleg Brega and Mr Hristea-Stan amounted to an interference with the applicants' Article 11 rights.'

Conclusion: yes, interference found explicitly for all three demonstrations.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly notes the dispute about whether the interference was prescribed by law but declines to resolve it separately, merging it with the necessity/proportionality analysis:

- 30 August: 'the Court considers that the issue of compliance with the law is indissociable from the question as to whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society". It will therefore examine this issue below.'

- 4 September: 'There is again a dispute as to whether this interference was prescribed by law and pursued a legitimate aim and again the Court considers that the issue of the lawfulness of the interference is indissociable from the question whether the interference was proportionate.'

- 10 September: 'As with the other two demonstrations, it considers it appropriate to consider the lawfulness and proportionality of that interference together.'

The Court does not separately resolve the prescribed-by-law question, nor does it separately address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards as sub-elements. The Government did argue that the interference was prescribed by law (citing section 11 of the Assemblies Act, noting it was 'accessible and foreseeable'), but the Court did not make explicit findings on these sub-elements. The overall 'prescribed by law' question is discussed but left unresolved (merged into necessity). The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are not discussed by the Court itself.

Conclusion on prescribed_by_law: discussed but not resolved (null conclusion); sub-elements not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court explicitly declines to decide whether a legitimate aim was pursued for all three demonstrations:

- 30 August: 'The Court further notes that the parties also disagreed as to whether the interference served a legitimate aim. The Court, for the reasons set out below, does not consider it necessary to decide this point.'

- 4 September: 'For the following reasons it is also unnecessary to decide whether it pursued a legitimate aim.'

- 10 September: 'while leaving unanswered the issue of whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim.'

The Government did argue legitimate aims: for 30 August, 'preventing disorder and protecting the honour and dignity of the Minister of Internal Affairs'; for 4 September, 'the prevention of disorder or crime'; for 10 September, similar aims. However, the Court did not adopt these. The legitimate aim element is explicitly discussed (the Court explicitly says it need not decide it), but no conclusion is reached. The aims_found list should reflect what the Government argued but the Court did not confirm, so it should be empty since the Court made no finding.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
This is the central element explicitly discussed and resolved for all three demonstrations:

- 30 August: The Court found the arrest disproportionate: 'the Court is not persuaded by the Government's submission that it was necessary to arrest the applicants and take them to a police station simply to check whether the demonstration had been properly authorised... the Court concludes that the arrest of the four men was disproportionate and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society.'

- 4 September: The Court found the arrests and fines disproportionate: 'the Court concludes that the arrest of Ghenadie and Oleg Brega and the fines imposed upon them were disproportionate and thus were not necessary in a democratic society.' The Court engaged in detailed comparative analysis with Ziliberberg, Rai and Evans, Skiba, Bukta, Éva Molnár, and Turkish cases.

- 10 September: 'the Court finds that the arrest of Mr Juraveli, Mr Oleg Brega and Mr Hristea-Stan on 10 September 2007 was disproportionate and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society.'

Conclusion: necessity not satisfied (no = the interference was not necessary), explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not explicitly frame any part of its analysis in terms of positive obligations. However, there is a passage in the 10 September assessment that comes close: 'In failing to allow the applicants to conduct their peaceful demonstration, the police failed to show the necessary tolerance which is to be expected of the authorities in a democratic society.' This could be read as implying a positive obligation to protect/facilitate peaceful assembly, but the Court does not explicitly frame it as a positive obligation analysis. The positive obligation framework is not discussed as such.

**Summary of structured conclusions:**

The case involves three demonstrations. The Court finds violations in all three. The scope elements (assembly exists, participation, peaceful) are implied/explicit. Interference is explicitly found. Prescribed by law is discussed but merged into necessity without resolution. Legitimate aim is explicitly left undecided. Necessity is explicitly found not satisfied (disproportionate). Positive obligation is not discussed as a distinct element.

For the structured results, since the case covers multiple demonstrations and the overall conclusion is violation, I will reflect the overall picture: scope yes (implied), assembly exists yes (implied), participation yes (implied, with the exception of Mr Juraveli re 4 Sept which was declared inadmissible), peaceful yes (explicit), interference yes (explicit), prescribed_by_law null (discussed but unresolved/merged), sub-elements of prescribed_by_law not_discussed, legitimate_aim null (explicitly left undecided), necessity no (explicitly found not satisfied = violation), positive_obligation not_discussed.
