**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of section B.3(c) 'Necessary in a democratic society':

'The Court therefore concludes that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society". Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In section B.2, the Court states: 'it is apparent from the police materials and the court decision that the applicant was arrested and convicted for violating public order during a demonstration and, more specifically, the demonstration of 7 April 2003 held on the Mashtots Avenue.' The Court also notes: 'nothing suggests that this demonstration was not intended to be peaceful. Furthermore, none of the materials in the case suggest that it was prohibited or not authorised.' The existence of an assembly (demonstration) is explicitly confirmed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly addresses whether the applicant participated in the assembly. In section B.2: 'The actions which led to a penalty being imposed on the applicant, according to the judge's findings, were the "obstruction of street traffic" and the "loud noise" he made during this demonstration which, in the Court's opinion, were the direct result of his participation in it.' And: 'By joining it, the applicant availed himself of his right to freedom of peaceful assembly.' The Court explicitly concludes the applicant participated.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly: 'nothing suggests that this demonstration was not intended to be peaceful.' Furthermore, in section B.3(c): 'As to the loud noise made by the applicant, there is no suggestion that this noise involved any obscenity or incitement to violence.' The assembly is explicitly found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference in section B.2: 'The Court accordingly concludes that the applicant's conviction for his participation at a lawful demonstration amounted to an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly.' The conviction and sanction (three days deprivation of liberty) constitute the interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this in section B.3(a). The Court recalls the foreseeability standard: 'a norm cannot be regarded as a "law" unless it is formulated with sufficient precision to enable the citizen – if need be, with appropriate advice – to foresee, to a degree that is reasonable in the circumstances, the consequences which a given action may entail.' The Court concludes: 'the Court considers that this norm is formulated with sufficient precision to satisfy the requirements of Article 11. It follows that the interference was prescribed by law.'

**i) Foreseeable:** Explicitly discussed. The Court applies the foreseeability standard and concludes the norm (Article 172 CAO) is sufficiently precise: 'this norm is formulated with sufficient precision to satisfy the requirements of Article 11.' Conclusion: yes.

**ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court does not separately address accessibility of the provision. This sub-element is not discussed.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court does not address whether the provision contains adequate safeguards against abuse. This sub-element is not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim in section B.3(b): 'The Court notes that it is apparent that the applicant incurred the sanction for actions which were qualified by the authorities as violating public order. The interference was therefore in pursuit of a legitimate aim, i.e. the "prevention of disorder".' The Government also mentioned 'protection of the rights of others' but the Court only explicitly identifies 'prevention of disorder' as the legitimate aim found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses this in section B.3(c). The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages:

'the freedom to take part in a peaceful assembly is of such importance that a person cannot be subjected to a sanction – even one at the lower end of the scale of disciplinary penalties – for participation in a demonstration which has not been prohibited, so long as this person does not himself commit any reprehensible act on such an occasion.'

'the Court concludes that the applicant was sanctioned for the mere fact of being present and proactive at the demonstration in question, rather than for committing anything illegal, violent or obscene in the course of it.'

'the Court considers that the very essence of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly would be impaired, if the State was not to prohibit a demonstration but was then to impose sanctions, especially such severe ones, on its participants for the mere fact of attending it, without committing anything reprehensible, as happened in the applicant's case.'

'The Court therefore concludes that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly was not "necessary in a democratic society".'

Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (the state interfered with the applicant's rights through conviction and sanction). Positive obligations are not discussed.
