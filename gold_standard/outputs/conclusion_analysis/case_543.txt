**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — i.e., there was an assembly, the applicants participated in it, and it was peaceful (otherwise Article 11 would not apply). These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies")
- scope_participation: implied yes (the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court proceeds to apply Article 11 without questioning the peaceful nature of the assemblies)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court explicitly states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming that interference is found. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. These elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. This element is not_discussed, and no aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" (necessity was not satisfied).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not spell out any positive obligation for the government in this reasoning. This element is not_discussed.
