**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of the reasoning section:

- 'The Court therefore concludes that the Government have not demonstrated that the applicant's conviction was proportionate to the aims pursued and, accordingly, "necessary in a democratic society".' 
- 'There has consequently been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Has there been an assembly?*
The Court explicitly discusses the existence of assemblies. It states: 'the majority of the acts which formed the basis of the applicant's conviction for being a member of an armed terrorist organisation under Article 314 § 2 of the Criminal Code ("the CC") fell within the ambit of Article 11 of the Convention, namely his participation in certain press conferences as well as a demonstration and two marches.' The Court also refers to 'demonstrations, press conferences and panels' and specific events such as 'events concerning the labour rights of workers at the State-run company Tekel, the centenary of International Women's Day, Nawruz celebrations, or 1st May International Workers' Day.' These are explicitly identified as assemblies falling within Article 11's scope.

*ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?*
The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation: 'the Malatya Assize Court relied on his participation in several demonstrations, press conferences and panels' and 'it is not in dispute that the applicant's participation in the above-mentioned events had a decisive impact on the national court's conclusion regarding his membership of the PKK.' Participation is explicitly confirmed.

*iii) Was the assembly peaceful?*
The Court explicitly addresses this: 'there is no finding in the reasoning of the national courts indicating that the events in question were of a violent nature or that the organisers or participants, including the applicant, had violent intentions. Significantly, it seems that the Malatya Assize Court merely listed the events without providing any information as to their nature. Likewise, the Government did not argue before the Court that those events had not been peaceful.' The Court also notes 'demonstrations and events which had not been found to be violent.' The assembly is thus explicitly found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: 'It is common ground between the parties that an interference with the applicant's rights under Article 11 of the Convention did take place, specifically on account of his criminal prosecution and resultant conviction on the basis of, inter alia, acts protected under Article 11 of the Convention.' Interference is explicitly confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court addresses this element but only by assumption, not by a definitive finding: 'The Court is prepared to assume that the interference in question was prescribed by law, namely Article 314 § 2 of the CC and section 5 of the Law on the Prevention of Terrorism.' The Court does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards — it simply assumes the condition is met without detailed sub-analysis. The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are therefore not individually discussed; the overall 'prescribed by law' element is addressed explicitly but only assumed to be satisfied.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim, again by assumption: 'the national authorities may be considered to have pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.' The aims found are: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the 'relevant and sufficient reasons' test from Kudrevičius and Others. Key passages include:
- 'there is no justification in the national courts' judgments for the conclusion that when the applicant participated in those demonstrations he had been acting for the PKK or on its behalf upon its specific instructions to him.'
- 'the trial court's conclusion appears to have been based to a very significant extent on its finding that the applicant's participation in the impugned events had been upon the instructions of the PKK, without carrying out an assessment as to whether his participation in the events could have been regarded as falling within the exercise of his right to freedom of assembly under Article 11 of the Convention.'
- 'the national authorities failed to weigh up the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly against the legitimate aims pursued in a manner that was adequate and in conformity with the criteria established by its case-law.'
- 'the applicant was sentenced to a very severe penalty, namely nine years' imprisonment, which must have had a chilling effect on his right to freedom of assembly.'
- 'neither the domestic courts nor the Government were able to demonstrate that the criminal sanction in question following demonstrations and events which had not been found to be violent had corresponded to a pressing social need.'
- 'The Court therefore concludes that the Government have not demonstrated that the applicant's conviction was proportionate to the aims pursued and, accordingly, "necessary in a democratic society".'

Necessity is explicitly discussed and found not to be satisfied.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (interference by the state through criminal conviction). Positive obligations are not mentioned.
