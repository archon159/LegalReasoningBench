**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. This is found in the final paragraphs of the assessment section:

'Having regard to the facts of the present cases and their clear similarity to those of the Gafgaz Mammadov case on all relevant and crucial points, the Court sees no particular circumstances that could compel it to deviate from its findings in that judgment, and finds that in the present cases each applicant's right to freedom of assembly was breached for the same reasons as those outlined above.'

And conclusively: 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied throughout the reasoning. The Court refers to 'demonstrations,' 'public assemblies,' and 'public gatherings' throughout, and the entire analysis presupposes that assemblies took place. The reference to 'a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner' implies the existence of an assembly.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court implies participation by the applicants. It notes 'a lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention.' This implies the applicants did participate. The Government's submission also refers to 'their specific behaviour in the course of the assemblies,' confirming participation.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly discusses the peaceful nature of the assembly: 'a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner.' The applicants also submitted 'that the demonstrations had been intended to be peaceful and had been held in a peaceful manner.' The conclusion is that the assembly was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied throughout. The Court refers to 'the dispersal of the demonstrations by the police and their arrest and conviction for an administrative offence' as the subject of the complaints. The Court notes 'doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely Article 310.1 of the CAO, relied on by the authorities to arrest and convict the participant of an unauthorised demonstration.' The dispersal, arrest, and conviction constitute interference, and this is treated as a given throughout the analysis.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the 'prescribed by law' requirement, specifically the foreseeability and safeguards sub-elements:

'the Court noted the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed.'

This addresses:
- **Foreseeability:** Explicitly discussed — 'serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation.' The conclusion is negative (not sufficiently foreseeable).
- **Accessibility:** Not explicitly discussed. No mention of accessibility of the law.
- **Safeguards:** Explicitly discussed — 'the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed' indicates insufficient safeguards against abuse. The conclusion is negative.

The overall 'prescribed by law' element is explicitly discussed with a negative conclusion (or at least serious concerns), though the Court does not formally state a definitive finding on this element alone — it folds it into the overall violation finding.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government submitted that 'the dispersal of the demonstrations had pursued the aim of protecting public safety and preventing disorder or crime.' The Court does not explicitly accept or reject this aim — it proceeds to examine necessity/proportionality without formally concluding on legitimate aim. The legitimate aim is mentioned in the parties' submissions but the Court does not make an explicit finding. It is implied that the Court may have accepted it for the sake of argument, since it proceeds to examine proportionality, but this is not stated. The aims mentioned are public safety and prevention of disorder/crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses necessity/proportionality:

'a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner' — this addresses proportionality of the dispersal.

'a failure by the authorities to take into consideration the fact that the demonstration had been notified' — relevant to proportionality.

'a lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention' — this goes to the necessity analysis.

'doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely Article 310.1 of the CAO, relied on by the authorities to arrest and convict the participant' — also relevant to necessity.

The conclusion on necessity is negative — the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (i.e., unjustified interference). No positive obligation is spelled out or found violated.
