**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant." This conclusion is reached after the Court finds that "the reasons adduced by the national authorities to justify the interference under Article 11 of the Convention, namely the applicants' arrest at the site of the venue and their conviction for administrative offences, were relevant and sufficient" — specifically, the Court is "not satisfied" that they were relevant and sufficient.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly states: "The Court has previously held that the protest in Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Yaroslav Belousov, cited above, §§ 168-71)." This is an explicit finding that an assembly existed and fell within Article 11's scope.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly addresses whether the applicants personally could rely on Article 11: "As to whether the applicants personally could rely on the provisions of Article 11, the Court reiterates that peaceful participants of a protest tarnished by isolated acts of violence committed by other participants do not cease to enjoy the right to peaceful assembly... The Court therefore considers that the applicants enjoyed the protection of Article 11 in the present case." This is an explicit discussion of participation.

**(iii) Peaceful character:** The Court explicitly discusses whether the assembly was peaceful and whether the applicants' conduct was peaceful: "It does not appear from any submissions that the applicants were among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the protest's originally peaceful character... Apart from that, the applicants' behaviour remained strictly peaceful, including the chanting of political slogans by the first applicant." The Court concludes the applicants' participation was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "As to whether the applicants' right to freedom of assembly was interfered with, the Court reiterates that measures taken by the authorities during a rally, such as dispersal of the rally or the arrest of participants and penalties imposed for having taken part in a rally, amount to an interference... The Court therefore considers that the termination of the demonstration, the applicants' arrest at the venue of the event and conviction for administrative offences constituted an interference with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly." This is an explicit finding of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not separately resolve the "prescribed by law" question. It notes: "The applicants contested the lawfulness of measures taken against them, alleging, in particular, that the police had had no grounds to give them orders to disperse. The Court considers that in this case the questions of compliance with the law and of the existence of a legitimate aim cannot be dissociated from the question of whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society', and that it is unnecessary to examine them separately." Thus, the Court explicitly declines to separately examine lawfulness (prescribed by law), merging it into the necessity analysis. No separate conclusions are reached on foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards sub-elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Similarly, the Court explicitly declines to separately examine legitimate aim, merging it with necessity: "The Court considers that in this case the questions of compliance with the law and of the existence of a legitimate aim cannot be dissociated from the question of whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society', and that it is unnecessary to examine them separately." However, the Government had argued the measures were "necessary 'for the prevention of disorder or crime' and 'for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others'." The Court also notes the authorities' submission that measures "were specifically aimed at preventing illegal campsites from being set up." No formal conclusion on legitimate aim is reached independently, though these aims are referenced in the context of the necessity analysis.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and resolved. The Court finds that the domestic courts failed to adequately examine the facts: "In the absence of adequate examination of the relevant factual and legal elements of the case on their part, the domestic courts cannot be said to have 'applied standards which were in conformity with the principles embodied in Article 11'." The Court concludes: "The Court therefore is not satisfied that the reasons adduced by the national authorities to justify the interference under Article 11 of the Convention, namely the applicants' arrest at the site of the venue and their conviction for administrative offences, were relevant and sufficient." The Court also notes the chilling effect: "the measures taken against the applicants must have had the effect of discouraging them and others from attending protest rallies and, more generally, from participating in open political debate." The necessity test is explicitly discussed and the Court concludes the interference was NOT necessary.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly references a positive obligation finding from a prior case: "It has also found that the domestic authorities failed to discharge their positive obligation to ensure the peaceful conduct of that protest (see Frumkin v. Russia, cited above, §§ 93-130)." This is an explicit reference to a positive obligation finding, though it is incorporated by reference to Frumkin rather than freshly analyzed. The conclusion is that the positive obligation was violated (the authorities failed to discharge it).
