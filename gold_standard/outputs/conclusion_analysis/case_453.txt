**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "The Court concludes that the first applicant's removal from the gathering venue, arrest and administrative conviction had not been necessary in the circumstances of the case. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that an assembly existed. It refers to "the demonstration," "the LDPR's authorised gathering," and "the gathering venue" throughout the reasoning. The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed as a threshold question, but the entire analysis presupposes it. The Court even notes: "Even assuming that Mr Nagibin's peaceful presence at the LDPR's gathering amounted to staging a demonstration of his own..." — this implies the existence of at least one assembly is taken for granted.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court implicitly accepts that Mr Nagibin participated (or attempted to participate) in the assembly. It refers to his "arrest during the demonstration" and his "peaceful presence at the LDPR's gathering." This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a threshold question.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. It states: "It was not argued that Mr Nagibin's behaviour had been considered provocative by other participants or that the leaflets distributed by him had been intimidating or capable of inciting violence and disturbing public order (see Sergey Kuznetsov v. Russia, no. 10877/04, § 45, 23 October 2008)." Furthermore, the Court refers to "Mr Nagibin's peaceful presence at the LDPR's gathering," explicitly characterizing his participation as peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "It has not been disputed by the parties that Mr Nagibin's arrest during the demonstration and his subsequent conviction constituted an interference with his right to freedom of assembly." The conclusion is affirmative and explicitly stated.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly identifies the legal basis: "That interference was based on Article 20.2 § 1 of the CAO, which establishes administrative liability for a breach of the procedure for holding a public event." However, the Court does not go on to explicitly analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or adequacy of safeguards. The Court accepts the legal basis without challenge and moves directly to legitimate aim and necessity. Therefore, the overall "prescribed by law" element is implied as satisfied (since the Court proceeds to the next steps), but the sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "The Court is ready to accept that the interference pursued the legitimate aims of 'prevention of disorder' and 'protection of the rights and freedoms of others'." Two aims are found: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It states: "It remains to be ascertained whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court then analyzes the domestic courts' reasoning, finding it deficient: "The domestic decisions contain no analysis as to what the alleged differences were between the LDPR's and Mr Nagibin's public events in terms of the message they conveyed and targeted audience." It further notes: "Even assuming that Mr Nagibin's peaceful presence at the LDPR's gathering amounted to staging a demonstration of his own, the absence of its prior authorisation and the ensuing 'unlawfulness' of the action do not as such justify his arrest, removal from the venue of the event and subsequent administrative conviction." And: "Their decisions, however, contain no such assessment and thus fall short of the requirements of Article 11 read in the light of Article 10 of the Convention." The Court concludes necessity was NOT satisfied: "the first applicant's removal from the gathering venue, arrest and administrative conviction had not been necessary in the circumstances of the case."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this reasoning. The case is analyzed entirely through the negative obligation framework (interference with the right). This element is not discussed.
