**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. This is stated in the final paragraph of the Court's assessment: 'Consequently, there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court found that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not disproportionate: 'the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that an assembly existed. It notes: 'the applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Kassianos school in Nicosia.' The existence of a demonstration/assembly is taken as a given, though not explicitly analyzed as a separate legal question.

**ii) Participation:** The Court implicitly accepts that the applicant participated in the assembly. It states: 'The demonstration was dispersed and some of the demonstrators, including the applicant, were arrested.' This implies her participation is not in dispute.

**iii) Peaceful character:** This sub-element is explicitly discussed, though the Court's conclusion is nuanced. The Government argued the demonstration was 'clearly outside the scope of Article 11' due to its violent character. The Court, rather than excluding the assembly from Article 11's scope on grounds of violence, proceeds to analyze the interference and justification. However, the Court does explicitly find that the demonstration was violent: 'the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order on the demarcation line in Cyprus... The Court sees no reason to depart from these findings.' The Court also states: 'the intervention of the Turkish and/or Turkish-Cypriot forces was not due to the political nature of the demonstration but was provoked by its violent character and by the violation of the "TRNC" borders by some of the demonstrators.' Notably, the Court does not exclude the assembly from Article 11's scope on grounds of violence — instead it proceeds to the justification analysis and uses the violent character to justify the interference. This is a somewhat unusual approach: the Court implicitly treats the assembly as within scope (since it finds an 'interference' and proceeds to justify it), but the violent character is used to justify the interference rather than to exclude scope. The conclusion on 'peaceful' is effectively 'no' (the assembly was not peaceful), but the Court does not use this to exclude Article 11 protection entirely.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'Under these circumstances, the Court considers that there has been an interference with the applicant's right of assembly.' The interference consisted of the dispersal of the demonstration and the arrest of the applicant.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: 'This interference had a legal basis, namely sections 70 and 71 of the Cypriot Criminal Code (see paragraphs 25-26 above) and section 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law (see paragraph 29 above), and was thus "prescribed by law" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.' The Court also recalls its finding from the Foka case that acts of the 'TRNC' authorities in compliance with laws in force in northern Cyprus should be regarded as having a legal basis in domestic law.

However, the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are NOT explicitly discussed. The Court simply concludes the interference was 'prescribed by law' without separately analyzing these three sub-requirements. They are at best implied by the overall conclusion.

**i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed; implied by the overall 'prescribed by law' conclusion.

**ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed; implied by the overall 'prescribed by law' conclusion.

**iii) Safeguards:** Not explicitly discussed; implied by the overall 'prescribed by law' conclusion.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed. The Court notes: 'The Government submitted that the interference pursued legitimate aims, including the protection of national security and/or public safety and the prevention of disorder and crime.' The Court proceeds to analyze the necessity question in light of these aims, implicitly accepting them as legitimate. The aims identified are: national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, and prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed. The Court engages in a detailed analysis of proportionality. It references the fundamental principles from its case-law (Djavit An, Piermont, Plattform 'Ärzte für das Leben'), notes that authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures but have a wide discretion, and finds that: 'interferences with the right guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention are in principle justified for the prevention of disorder or crime and for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others where, as in the instant case, demonstrators engage in acts of violence.' The Court concludes: 'the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2.' The necessity test is thus answered in favor of the government (the interference WAS necessary/proportionate).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court briefly mentions the positive obligation dimension when it states: 'the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey, no. 74552/01, § 35, 5 December 2006). However, they cannot guarantee this absolutely and they have a wide discretion in the choice of the means to be used.' This is mentioned in the context of the necessity analysis rather than as a standalone positive obligation claim. The Court does not find a violation of any positive obligation; indeed, the reference is used to support the government's wide margin of appreciation. The positive obligation is thus mentioned but not the primary focus of the analysis, and no violation of it is found.
