**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied.

- **(i) Assembly exists:** Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies." The Court proceeds on the basis that assemblies existed.

- **(ii) Participation:** Implied. The applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," and the Court proceeds to find an interference with their freedom of assembly, which logically requires their participation.

- **(iii) Peaceful:** Implied. The Court finds an interference with "freedom of assembly" and proceeds to the necessity analysis without suggesting the assemblies were non-peaceful. The leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the opening paragraph describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed. Since the Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis (finding the interference not necessary in a democratic society), the "prescribed by law" element is not_discussed. It is not even implied, because the Court does not need to resolve it to reach its conclusion — it finds a violation at the necessity stage.

- **(i) Foreseeable:** Not discussed.
- **(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.
- **(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation. This is explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is not discussed.
