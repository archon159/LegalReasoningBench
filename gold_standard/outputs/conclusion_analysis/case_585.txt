**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passages are:

- "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that the dispersal of the public assembly on 16 October 2006 and the arrest of the third, fourth, fifth and sixth applicants were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."
- "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."
- "There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of all applicants."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness) as separate analytical steps. However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the dispersal of the public assembly on 16 October 2006" — the use of the phrase "public assembly" implies that an assembly existed and that the applicants participated in it. The Government's submission that "Mr M. had organised and the applicants had taken part in an unauthorised picket" also implies participation. The assembly is treated as peaceful throughout (it is described as a "picket" and the Court applies Article 11 on freedom of *peaceful* assembly without questioning its peaceful character).

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied — the Court refers to "the dispersal of the public assembly on 16 October 2006" and the Government refers to "an unauthorised picket," both presupposing an assembly existed.
- **scope_participation**: Implied — the Government states "the applicants had taken part" in the picket, and the Court proceeds on this basis.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court applies Article 11 on freedom of *peaceful* assembly without any suggestion that the assembly was not peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate analytical step. The Court notes "the dispersal of the public assembly on 16 October 2006 and the arrest of the third, fourth, fifth and sixth applicants" and the applicants' complaint that "the ban and dispersal of the picket, as well as their arrest and detention, violated their freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court proceeds directly to necessity, implying that interference is taken as established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. These sub-elements are not discussed and no conclusion is drawn on them.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing whether the interference served a legitimate aim listed in Article 11(2). No aims are found or discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly addressed. The Court states: "the Court considers that the dispersal of the public assembly on 16 October 2006 and the arrest of the third, fourth, fifth and sixth applicants were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion. The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference case.
