**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is in the final paragraph of the merits section: "Having regard to its case-law on the subject and in view of the above considerations, the Court considers that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

Additionally, the preceding paragraph sets out the reasoning: "the Court notes that by participating in the demonstration organised by his trade union, the applicant exercised his right to freedom of assembly... the disciplinary sanction complained of, although very light, was such as to dissuade trade union members from participation in trade union activities."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court explicitly identifies the gathering as an assembly. It states: "the Court notes that by participating in the demonstration organised by his trade union, the applicant exercised his right to freedom of assembly." The demonstration was organised by KESK and DİSK to celebrate Labour Day and commemorate those who died in 1977. This is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation: "the applicant was a civil servant in the Istanbul Municipality. He was also a member of the KESK, namely one of the trade unions which had organised and announced the demonstration of 1 May 2008" and "in the course of the disciplinary proceedings against him, the applicant explicitly stated that he had been absent because he had participated in the demonstration organised by his trade union." The Court further states: "Bearing in mind that the applicant, a civil servant working in the Istanbul Municipality, was absent on 1 May 2008 to participate in Labour Day demonstrations that were organised and announced by his trade union, namely the KESK." This is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

*iii) Peaceful assembly:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the entire analysis proceeds on the assumption that the assembly was within the scope of Article 11 protection, and there is no suggestion of violence. The peaceful nature is implied by the Court's treatment of the case as falling within Article 11 protection.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. It refers to "the necessity of the interference under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention" and notes that "the disciplinary sanction complained of, although very light, was such as to dissuade trade union members from participation in trade union activities." The warning issued under Law no. 657 as a disciplinary sanction is treated as the interference. The Court also frames the entire analysis around the context "in which the interference took place." This is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law. It mentions that the warning was issued "under Law no. 657, as a disciplinary sanction" and refers to "Section 125 of Law no. 657," which implies a legal basis exists. However, the Court does not analyze the foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards of this legal basis. The prescribed-by-law element is not discussed as a distinct analytical step.

*i) Foreseeable:* Not discussed.
*ii) Accessible:* Not discussed.
*iii) Safeguards:* Not discussed. The Court does note that "the disciplinary authorities failed to take into consideration, either explicitly or in substance, whether it was justified to issue a warning pursuant to Section 125 of Law no. 657 in the particular circumstances of the case," but this goes to necessity rather than safeguards in the prescribed-by-law sense.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. The Government's argument was that the sanction was for absence without leave rather than for trade union activity, but the Court does not frame this as a legitimate aim analysis. No legitimate aim is identified or assessed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly discusses necessity. It states: "it is of importance for the Court, in its assessment of the necessity of the interference under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, that in the course of the investigation, the applicant had explained the reason of his absence by stating that he had participated in a trade union activity. Nevertheless, in their evaluation the disciplinary authorities failed to take into consideration, either explicitly or in substance, whether it was justified to issue a warning pursuant to Section 125 of Law no. 657 in the particular circumstances of the case."

Further: "the disciplinary sanction complained of, although very light, was such as to dissuade trade union members from participation in trade union activities." The Court finds the interference was not necessary — i.e., the necessity test is not satisfied — because the authorities failed to weigh the trade union dimension of the applicant's absence. This is explicitly discussed and the conclusion is that necessity was not established (i.e., the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. The case is analyzed entirely as a negative interference with the applicant's rights. This element is not discussed.
