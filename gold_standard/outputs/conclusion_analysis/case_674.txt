**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final conclusion is stated in the last two paragraphs of section B.2.(c):

"The Court concludes that in view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicant his criminal conviction was a measure grossly disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society."

"There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
Explicitly discussed. The Court examines whether the assembly at Bolotnaya Square falls within the scope of Article 11. It states: "In view of the foregoing, the Court considers that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention and accepts that the applicant enjoyed the protection of this provision." The Court also notes that "Article 11 of the Convention only protects the right to 'peaceful assembly', a notion which does not cover a demonstration where the organisers and participants have violent intentions" and proceeds to analyze whether the assembly qualifies. The existence of an assembly is not in dispute — it was an authorized meeting at Bolotnaya Square. This is implied throughout but most explicitly confirmed in the scope finding.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Implied. The Court does not separately discuss whether the applicant participated, but it is implicit throughout the analysis. The applicant states he "came to take part in a peaceful meeting which had been authorised by the Moscow authorities," and the Court's entire analysis of interference and proportionality presupposes his participation. The Court notes "the acts imputed to the applicant included chanting anti-government slogans during the dispersal of the assembly and throwing an unidentified round yellow object in the direction of the police cordon."

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
Explicitly discussed at length. The Court examines whether the assembly was peaceful within the meaning of Article 11. It states: "The guarantees of Article 11 of the Convention therefore apply to all gatherings except those where the organisers and participants have such intentions, incite violence or otherwise reject the foundations of a democratic society... It is therefore necessary to determine whether the facts of the present case fall within the ambit of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court then analyzes the evidence: "whatever the organisers' intentions were, they made no call for violence and showed no signs that they had been prepared for it." It also notes that "The round yellow object tossed by the applicant in the instant case remained unidentified but appeared, by all accounts, innocuous" and that "The fact that the applicant was chanting political slogans like 'One for all and all for one!' or 'United, we are invincible!' cannot be taken as evidence that he had violent intentions."

Conclusion: "the Court considers that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention" — i.e., it was peaceful enough to attract protection.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "It also finds that the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for participation in mass disorder constituted an interference with the exercise of the freedom of assembly."

The Government had argued "there had been no interference with the exercise of the applicant's right to peaceful assembly, and argued that the criminal charges against him related to acts of violence unconnected with the exercise of freedom of expression or freedom of assembly." The Court rejected this and found interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed, though not broken down into all three sub-elements. The Court addresses the lawfulness question in section (c): "The applicant contested that the events which took place at Bolotnaya Square constituted mass disorder within the meaning of Article 212 of the Criminal Code, and that his own acts could qualify as participation in such disorder."

The Court notes: "Russian law does not contain a definition of 'mass disorder', and it accepts that this term might be imprecise, akin to 'riot' or 'breach of public order'... the requirement of lawfulness would be satisfied if the domestic courts' interpretation of these terms was neither arbitrary nor unpredictable, and if their use bore reasonable relation to the circumstances at hand."

It concludes: "the applicant's criminal conviction may not be considered 'unlawful' on the grounds put forward by the applicant."

**i) Foreseeable?** Explicitly discussed in the context of the imprecision of "mass disorder" — the Court finds the provision not entirely inconceivable in its application, and that the domestic courts acted within their margin of judicial discretion. Conclusion: yes (implicitly).

**ii) Accessible?** Not explicitly discussed. The Court does not separately address accessibility of the legal provision.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse?** Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element.

Overall prescribed by law conclusion: yes (the Court finds the conviction not unlawful, though it goes on to find a violation on necessity grounds).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "Turning to the existence of a legitimate aim, the Court accepts that the authorities pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others."

Aims found: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages:

- "the applicant was sentenced to two years and three months' imprisonment for attending an authorised public assembly, chanting anti-government slogans, and throwing an unidentified small round object which hit a police officer on the shoulder and caused him pain."
- "the applicant's staying at the site of the rally and chanting anti-government slogans accounted for a staggering one-and-a-half-year addition to his prison term. These acts, unlike tossing the yellow object, were peaceful and constituted protest, a form of expression protected by Article 10 of the Convention."
- "the Court does not consider that the risks referred to by the Government – potential civil unrest, political instability and threat to public order – had any personal relation to the applicant. These reasons could not therefore justify the sentence of two years and three months."
- "The Court considers that there was no 'pressing social need' to sentence the applicant to a prison term of this duration."
- "the applicant's criminal conviction, and especially the severity of his sentence, could not but have had the effect of discouraging him and other opposition supporters, as well as the public at large, from attending demonstrations and, more generally, from participating in open political debate."
- "The Court concludes that in view of the severity of the sanction imposed on the applicant his criminal conviction was a measure grossly disproportionate to the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and it was therefore not necessary in a democratic society."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly addresses the positive obligation question in the admissibility section, but decides NOT to examine it as a separate issue: "the Court is not required in this case to assess the authorities' alleged failure to discharge their positive obligation in respect of the conduct of the assembly at Bolotnaya Square as a separate issue under Article 11 of the Convention. It will examine whether the measures taken against the applicant personally complied with Article 11 of the Convention in the light of all the materials submitted by the parties concerning the planning, conduct and dispersal of the assembly."

Thus, the positive obligation question is raised but explicitly set aside as a separate issue — the Court folds the relevant factual material into its analysis of the interference and necessity questions. No separate conclusion on positive obligation is reached.
