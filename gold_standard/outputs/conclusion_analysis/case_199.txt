**Question 1: Has Article 11 ECHR been violated?**

Yes. The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage states: "Having examined all the material before it, the Court concludes that they also disclose violations of the Convention and Protocol No. 7, in the light of its findings in Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, §§ 81-142, 5 January 2016, concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies."

The Court does not assign a specific paragraph number to this conclusion in the excerpt provided — it is contained within the section heading "II. OTHER ALLEGED VIOLATIONS UNDER WELL-ESTABLISHED CASE-LAW" and is rendered as a single block of reasoning without numbered paragraphs. The violation of Article 11 is established by reference to the Frumkin v. Russia precedent (§§ 81-142), which dealt with disproportionate measures against participants and organisers of public assemblies.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The scope is not explicitly discussed in the text. However, the reference to Frumkin v. Russia "concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies" implies that there was an assembly, that the applicant participated (or attempted to participate) in it, and that it was peaceful — otherwise the Article 11 framework would not apply. These sub-elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *Assembly exists*: Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the Frumkin citation.
- *Participation*: Implied by the reference to "organisers and participants of public assemblies."
- *Peaceful*: Implied — the Court applies Article 11 without any suggestion that the assembly was violent.

**(b) Interference**

Not explicitly discussed. However, the finding of a violation necessarily implies that there was a governmental interference with the applicant's freedom of assembly (otherwise there would be nothing to assess). The reference to "disproportionate measures taken by the authorities" implies an interference. This is implied rather than explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Not discussed — neither explicitly nor clearly implied. The Court's reasoning focuses on disproportionality (necessity) rather than the lawfulness of the legal basis. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. The Court does not identify any legitimate aim in this passage. The reasoning jumps directly to the conclusion of disproportionality by reference to Frumkin.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly addressed. The Court finds a violation by reference to Frumkin v. Russia §§ 81-142, described as "concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies." The word "disproportionate" directly invokes the necessity/proportionality test. This is explicitly discussed (by incorporation of the Frumkin reasoning), and the conclusion is that the necessity requirement was NOT satisfied (i.e., the measures were disproportionate), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The Court's reasoning is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (the State taking disproportionate measures), not positive obligations to facilitate or protect assemblies.
