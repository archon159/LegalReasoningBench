**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the Court's assessment: "In these circumstances, the Court finds a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The reasoning leading to this conclusion is found in the necessity section: "the Court concludes that the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations" and "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court implicitly accepts that assemblies took place. The entire analysis proceeds on the basis that demonstrations occurred on 12 January, 26 January, and 10 March 2013. The applicants' submissions describe these as "demonstrations" and "spontaneous assemblies," and the Court engages with these characterizations without questioning whether an assembly existed. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

*ii) Participation:* The Court implicitly accepts that the applicants participated in the assemblies. The Court discusses their "arrest and conviction for an administrative offence" in connection with the demonstrations, and notes "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was itself protected by Article 11." This is implied.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies. The applicants submitted "that the demonstrations had been intended to be peaceful and had been held in a peaceful manner." The Court refers to "unauthorised peaceful demonstration" and "unauthorised peaceful assemblies," accepting that the assemblies were peaceful. This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**

The Court implicitly treats the dispersal of demonstrations and the arrest and conviction of the applicants as constituting interference with Article 11 rights. The opening paragraph states the applicants complained that "the dispersal of the demonstrations by the police and their arrest and conviction for an administrative offence had been in breach of their freedom of assembly." The Court proceeds directly to assess lawfulness and necessity, implying interference is established. The chilling effect passage also confirms this: "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicants' arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies." This is implied rather than explicitly labeled as an "interference" finding.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the lawfulness/prescribed-by-law element. It examines whether the demonstrations were "spontaneous" (which would exempt them from the prior notification requirement under Article 5 § IV of the Law on Freedom of Assembly). The Court states: "the Court is ready to accept that the dispersal of the demonstrations on 12 January, 26 January and 10 March 2013 was lawful." This is an explicit finding that the interference was prescribed by law.

However, the Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards. It simply concludes the interference was "lawful" by reference to the notification requirement and the applicants' failure to substantiate spontaneity. The sub-elements are not individually discussed.

*i) Foreseeable:* Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element. The Government noted that "the applicants had also been aware of the authorities' position on unauthorised assemblies and of the administrative penalties," which touches on foreseeability, but the Court does not separately analyze this. Not discussed as a distinct sub-element.

*ii) Accessible:* Not discussed.

*iii) Safeguards:* Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued that "the dispersal of the demonstrations had been necessary in the interests of national security, for the protection of public safety and the prevention of disorder or crime." The Court does not explicitly endorse or reject these aims — it moves directly to the necessity analysis without making a formal finding on legitimate aim. The aims are mentioned in the parties' submissions but the Court does not explicitly conclude that a legitimate aim was or was not pursued. This is not_discussed as a Court finding, though the aims are referenced in submissions.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is explicitly and centrally discussed. The Court states: "Turning to the question whether it was necessary in a democratic society to disperse those demonstrations and to convict the applicants..." It concludes: "the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations" (referring to Gafgaz Mammadov). It also notes the chilling effect: "The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly." The necessity test is not satisfied — the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
