**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for Article 11 to be engaged at all. The description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" in the opening paragraph also implies these elements are satisfied.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants."
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to find a violation of Article 11, which presupposes the assemblies were peaceful (Article 11 does not protect non-peaceful assemblies).

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes the measures as "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is an explicit finding that there was an interference.

Quote: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'" and "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, which is the basis for the violation finding. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, these elements remain not discussed.

- *prescribed_by_law*: not discussed
- *prescribed_by_law_foreseeable*: not discussed
- *prescribed_by_law_accessible*: not discussed
- *prescribed_by_law_safeguards*: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met, constituting the basis for the violation. The Court also references leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and principles on proportionality (Oya Ataman, Hyde Park) to support this conclusion.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (i.e., unjustified interference by the state). This element is not discussed.
