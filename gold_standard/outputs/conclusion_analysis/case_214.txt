**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court reaches mixed conclusions depending on which applicants are considered:

- **Violation found** in respect of the **first and second applicants** (arrest and conviction): "There has, accordingly, been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the first and second applicants concerning their arrest and conviction."
- **No violation** in respect of the **third to fifth applicants**: "There has, accordingly, been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the third to fifth applicants concerning their arrest and conviction."
- **No violation** in respect of the **sixth applicant**: "There has, accordingly, been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the sixth applicant concerning his arrest and conviction."
- **Violation found** in respect of the **seventh and ninth applicants** (events of 31 and 27 May 2010): "There has, accordingly, been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the seventh and ninth applicants concerning the events of 31 and 27 May 2010 respectively."

Overall, Article 11 **has been violated** (in respect of at least some applicants).

---

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. It refers to the protest action in Gorky Park as a "protest action" and "assembly" throughout. For instance: "it appears to have been the position of those protesting in Gorky Park that they were defending the park as a public space open to everyone as a place of leisure, without attaching a formal label to their action." The Court also refers to "the right to freedom of peaceful assembly" and treats the events as falling within the scope of Article 11. The assembly's existence is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses whether the first and second applicants participated in the assembly, since the Government contested this. The Court states: "it has not been contested that the first and second applicants were actually among the group of protesters who, on 28 May 2010, had been blocking the railroad passage for a bulldozer in the area marked off as a construction site" and "It is evident from that sequence of events that those who continued to be present in that restrained group of protesters until the time of arrest could not have been simple bystanders." The Court concludes they did participate. For the third to sixth applicants, participation is implied/not contested. For the seventh and ninth applicants, participation is implied by the discussion of the events of 27 and 31 May.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court implicitly treats the assembly as peaceful (or at least attracting Article 11 protection). The applicants themselves stressed their actions were "peaceful and legitimate." The Court refers to "the right to freedom of peaceful assembly" and applies the full Article 11 framework. The Court notes: "the protest action had been peaceful; the protesters had not attacked the loggers, the MG staff or the police." The Court does not explicitly find the assembly was not peaceful; it proceeds on the basis that it was peaceful enough to attract Article 11 protection. The Court also notes that "the applicants were arrested and convicted not for their protest action as such but for their failure to obey the order to leave" (regarding the third to fifth applicants), implying the protest itself was peaceful. This is discussed explicitly in the context of the applicants' submissions and implicitly accepted by the Court.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. For the first and second applicants: "According to the Court's established case-law, measures taken by the authorities during an assembly, such as dispersal or arrest of participants, and penalties imposed for taking part in an assembly, amount to an interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The Court concludes: "The Court concludes that there has been an interference with the first and second applicants' right to freedom of peaceful assembly." For the third to sixth applicants, the Government conceded interference and the Court found no reason to differ: "The Government did not contest that those applicants' conviction constituted an interference with their right to freedom of assembly. The Court finds no reason to find otherwise." For the seventh and ninth applicants, the interference is framed as a failure to protect (positive obligation), discussed separately.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element in detail for the first six applicants.

**i) Foreseeable:** The Court states: "The Court reiterates its case-law to the effect that the expressions 'prescribed by law' and 'in accordance with the law' in Articles 8 to 11 of the Convention not only require that the impugned measure should have a legal basis in domestic law, but also refer to the quality of the law in question, which should be accessible to the person concerned and foreseeable as to its effects." The Court finds: "The interference had a basis in domestic law, namely Article 185 of the Code of Administrative Offences... There is no indication that the above-mentioned provision was not accessible to the applicants or not foreseeable as such." The Court then addresses the applicants' argument about the lack of a judicial order and dismisses it: "The Court, accordingly, dismisses the applicants' arguments in this respect and accepts that the interference was 'prescribed by law'." Foreseeability is thus found to be satisfied.

**ii) Accessible:** The Court states "There is no indication that the above-mentioned provision was not accessible to the applicants or not foreseeable as such." Accessibility is explicitly discussed and found satisfied.

**iii) Safeguards:** The Court does not explicitly discuss safeguards against abuse as a separate sub-element. The discussion of "prescribed by law" focuses on legal basis, accessibility, and foreseeability. Safeguards are not_discussed as a distinct element.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "the Court accepts the Government's submission that the interference had pursued the legitimate aim of protecting the health and safety of protesters and workers. In the latter respect, it can also reasonably be viewed as having been for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others." The aims found are: protection of health (of protesters and workers) and protection of the rights and freedoms of others (those with a stake in the construction project).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity/proportionality for each group of applicants.

For the **first and second applicants**: The Court finds a violation because "the domestic courts did not provide sufficient reasons for their decision to impose custodial sentences of such severity on the first and second applicants and, therefore, it was not demonstrated by the Government that the sanction imposed on those applicants was proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued." Necessity: **not satisfied** (violation).

For the **third to fifth applicants**: "the authorities remained, for a time, tolerant of even such dangerous protest activity, and the applicants were arrested and convicted not for their protest action as such but for their failure to obey the order to leave... Their removal from the construction site and conviction for the administrative offence was, in the light of the nature of the sanctions imposed, proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued." Necessity: **satisfied** (no violation).

For the **sixth applicant**: "the sanction imposed on the applicant in the form of a ten-day custodial sentence cannot be described as grossly disproportionate... the domestic courts cannot be said to have overstepped their margin of appreciation." Necessity: **satisfied** (no violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly discusses positive obligations in relation to the **seventh and ninth applicants**. It states: "The Court has already found that the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to lawful demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens." And: "The Court considers that the same obligation applies to an assembly, which, whether lawful or not in terms of domestic law, attracts the protection of Article 11 and of which the authorities have had sufficient notice, whether formal or de facto, enabling them to take such measures."

The Court finds the positive obligation was **violated**: "The Court concludes that by failing (i) to regulate in an adequate fashion the use of force by security personnel, (ii) to properly organise the division of responsibility in maintaining order between the private security personnel and the police, which would also have allowed for the identification of the security personnel deployed, (iii) to enforce the rules concerning adequate identification of persons authorised to use force, and (iv) to explain the decision of the police not to intervene on 27 and 31 May 2010 in any meaningful fashion capable of preventing or controlling effectively the clashes, the respondent State failed to comply with its obligation to ensure the peaceful nature of the protests on those dates."

The positive obligation is also mentioned in the general principles section: "States must not only refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon the right to assemble peacefully but also safeguard that right. Although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities with the exercise of the rights protected, there may in addition be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these rights."

---

**Summary of structured conclusions:**

Given the mixed outcome (violation found for some applicants, not others), the overall `violated` field is `"true"` since Article 11 was found to be violated in respect of at least some applicants. The canonical elements are assessed at the overall case level, noting that the prescribed_by_law and legitimate_aim elements were found satisfied, necessity was found not satisfied for the first and second applicants (leading to violation) but satisfied for others, and the positive obligation was found violated for the seventh and ninth applicants.
