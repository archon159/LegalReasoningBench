**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section: "the Court therefore dismisses the Government's preliminary objection regarding the applicant's alleged lack of victim status and concludes that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The finding of disproportionality is stated just before: "the Court considers that in the instant case the police's forceful intervention was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly define or discuss whether an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 exists, but it is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to the event as a 'meeting,' 'march,' 'demonstration,' 'rally,' and 'gathering,' and proceeds to analyze it under Article 11 without questioning whether it qualifies as an assembly. This is implied by the entire analysis.

*ii) Participation:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicants participated in the assembly, but this is implied. The Court states: "by participating in this meeting, the applicants aimed to draw attention to F-type prison conditions" — this confirms participation is treated as established.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court does not explicitly address whether the assembly was peaceful as a threshold question, but it is implied. The Court notes: "where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings." The Court also notes there was "no evidence to suggest that the group presented a danger to public order, apart from possibly blocking the tram line." The assembly is treated throughout as peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. Under the heading "1. Existence of any interference with the applicants' rights," the Court states: "there is no dispute between the parties as to the initial existence of an interference with the applicants' right of assembly." The Court further notes that "the interference in the meeting, the force used by the police to disperse the participants and the subsequent prosecution could have had a chilling effect." Conclusion: yes, interference is explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element: "it is noted that the interference in the present case had a legal basis, namely section 22 of Law No. 2911 (Assemblies and Marches Act), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention."

The Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. It simply states the legal basis exists and concludes the requirement is met. The sub-elements are not discussed individually.

*i) Foreseeable:* Not explicitly discussed; implied as satisfied given the overall conclusion that the measure was prescribed by law.

*ii) Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed; implied as satisfied.

*iii) Safeguards:* Not explicitly discussed; implied as satisfied.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "As concerns legitimate aim, the Government submitted that the interference pursued the legitimate aim of preventing public disorder and the Court finds no reason to differ." The aim found is prevention of disorder.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. Key passages include:
- "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to lawful demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens."
- "States must not only safeguard the right to assemble peacefully, but also refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon that right."
- "there is no evidence to suggest that the group presented a danger to public order, apart from possibly blocking the tram line."
- "The Court is therefore particularly struck by the authorities' impatience in seeking to end the demonstration."
- "although no notification had been given, the authorities had prior knowledge... that such a demonstration would take place on that date and could have therefore taken preventive measures."
- "where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings."
- "the Court considers that in the instant case the police's forceful intervention was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

Conclusion: necessity was NOT satisfied — the interference was disproportionate.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly mentions positive obligations: "although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities with the exercise of the rights protected, there may in addition be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these rights (see Djavit An, cited above, § 57; Oya Ataman, cited above, § 36)." However, the Court does not separately conclude that a positive obligation was violated — the violation is found on the basis of the disproportionate interference (negative obligation). The positive obligation is mentioned as a general principle but not applied to reach a separate conclusion. Conclusion: discussed explicitly as a general principle, but no separate finding of violation of a positive obligation.
