**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and describes "the dispersal of these assemblies" — this implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11 protection (since the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and ultimately finds a violation). The reference to leading cases such as Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov further implies that the assemblies were peaceful, as those cases involved peaceful assemblies. None of these sub-elements are explicitly discussed as standalone legal questions.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied by the phrase "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies."
- **scope_participation**: Implied by the same phrase — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants."
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied — the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and finds a violation, which logically requires the assembly to have been peaceful. No explicit discussion.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and earlier the text describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. This constitutes explicit discussion of interference, though the Court does not separately analyze whether interference occurred as a standalone step — it is treated as established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it does not need to address prescribed by law. These elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not_discussed, and no aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and states it "has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion." The conclusion is that necessity is NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not_discussed.
