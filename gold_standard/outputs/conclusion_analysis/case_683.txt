**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court refers to "the applicants" as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" and to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly," which logically presupposes that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to fall within Article 11's protection. These are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: The opening paragraph states the applicants were "organisers or participants of public assemblies," implying an assembly existed. This is implied, not explicitly analyzed.
- **scope_participation**: The same passage — "organisers or participants of public assemblies" — implies participation. Implied, not explicitly analyzed.
- **scope_peaceful**: Not explicitly discussed. The Court's willingness to apply Article 11 implies the assemblies were peaceful, but this is not stated. Implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'," explicitly using the word "interferences." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing legitimate aim. Not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is explicit, though brief, relying on the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia. The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. Not discussed.
