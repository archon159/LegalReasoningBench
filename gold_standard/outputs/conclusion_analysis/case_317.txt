**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the Court's assessment: "Bearing in mind the above circumstances, the Court concludes that the interference did not correspond to a pressing social need and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, these elements are implied by the Court's explicit finding of interference. The Court states: "It is common ground between the parties, and the Court agrees, that the decision to reject Hyde Park's application to hold a demonstration on 5 January 2005 amounted to 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11." This implies that there was an assembly (a planned demonstration), that the applicants were participants/organisers, and that the assembly was peaceful (the Court later notes: "There was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities.").

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied as yes — the Court treats the planned demonstration as falling within the scope of Article 11 without question.
- **scope_participation**: Implied as yes — the applicants are treated as the organisers/participants throughout.
- **scope_peaceful**: Explicitly discussed in the necessity section: "There was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities. Rather their intention was to draw attention to the plight of Moldovan students in Romania." This is discussed in the context of necessity rather than scope, but it confirms the peaceful nature of the assembly.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed and confirmed: "It is common ground between the parties, and the Court agrees, that the decision to reject Hyde Park's application to hold a demonstration on 5 January 2005 amounted to 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element but ultimately declines to reach a definitive conclusion, treating it as indissociable from the necessity question: "In so far as the lawfulness of the interference is concerned, the Court notes that under section 14 of the Assemblies Act the Chișinău Municipality was obliged to give reasons in writing for rejecting Hyde Park's application... Contrary to this provision, the Municipality's decision appears to have questioned the reasonableness of Hyde Park's decision to stage a protest... This in itself might be a sufficient basis for the conclusion that the impugned measures were not 'prescribed by law'. However, in the present case, the Court considers that the issue of compliance with the law is indissociable from the question as to whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society'. It will therefore examine this issue below."

The Court raises concerns about foreseeability (the Municipality's decision appeared inconsistent with the Assemblies Act's requirements) and implicitly about safeguards (section 12(6) required evidence of serious consequences before rejection). The Court does not separately discuss accessibility. Since the Court merges the prescribed-by-law analysis into the necessity analysis without a standalone conclusion, the overall prescribed_by_law conclusion is null (unresolved as a standalone finding).

- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: Explicitly raised but not resolved — the Court notes the Municipality's decision "appears to have questioned the reasonableness" in a way inconsistent with the Assemblies Act, suggesting the interference may not have been foreseeable, but no final conclusion is given.
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: Not discussed.
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: Implicitly raised — the Court references section 12(6) which requires evidence of serious consequences, suggesting the safeguard was not respected, but no standalone conclusion is given.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this point: "The parties also disagreed as to whether the interference served a legitimate aim. The Court, for the reasons set out below, does not consider it necessary to decide this point either." The Government argued national security as the aim. The Court does not accept or reject this. Conclusion is null.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages:
- "the Municipality rejected Hyde Park's application to hold a protest demonstration planned for 5 January 2005 on the grounds that, in its opinion, 'the protest was unwelcome and unfounded'. The Court noted above that such a reason appears to be inconsistent with the requirements of the Assemblies Act... Such reasons cannot be considered compatible with Article 11 of the Convention."
- "There was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities."
- "The guarantee of the right to freedom of assembly cannot be left to the whim of the authorities and their perception of what is or is not deserving of authorisation. The Court can only conclude that the Municipality's refusal to authorise the demonstration did not respond to a pressing social need."
- "Bearing in mind the above circumstances, the Court concludes that the interference did not correspond to a pressing social need and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference case.
