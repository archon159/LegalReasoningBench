**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is found in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "In the light of the foregoing, the Court finds that there has been a violation of the respondent State's positive obligations under Article 11 taken in conjunction with Article 14 of the Convention (see Identoba and Others, cited above, §§ 98-100)."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly walk through the three sub-elements of scope as a formal doctrinal exercise, but they are clearly implied by the framing of the case.

- *Assembly exists*: Implied. The Court refers throughout to "the IDAHO event of 17 May 2013," "a peaceful public rally," and "the peaceful demonstration," making clear that an assembly existed. This is not formally discussed as a separate doctrinal step but is taken for granted.

- *Participation*: Implied. The applicants are described as participants in the IDAHO event who "had been unable to proceed with their plans to hold a peaceful public rally." Their participation (or attempted participation) is the factual premise of the entire complaint.

- *Peaceful*: Implied, and arguably explicit. The Court repeatedly refers to the assembly as "peaceful": "their plans to hold a peaceful public rally," "the peaceful demonstration had been disrupted," "the applicants to proceed with their peaceful event," and "the IDAHO event of 17 May 2013 could take place peacefully." The Court treats the assembly as peaceful without any doubt.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not frame the case in terms of a classic "interference" by the state with the right to assembly (i.e., a prohibition or dispersal order). Instead, the case is analyzed entirely through the lens of positive obligations — the state's failure to protect the assembly from third-party violence. The Court states: "the applicants' complaints that the State failed to protect their freedom to participate in the protest rally of 17 May 2013 and protect them from hate-motivated violence." The interference is thus implied through the positive obligation framework rather than explicitly discussed as a separate doctrinal step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Not discussed. Because the case is framed as a positive obligation case (failure to act) rather than a negative interference case (a legal measure restricting assembly), the "prescribed by law" test — which applies to state restrictions — is not relevant and is not addressed. None of the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. Again, because the case is analyzed as a positive obligation case, the Court does not examine whether any interference served a legitimate aim. No aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Not discussed as a formal doctrinal step. The proportionality/necessity analysis is subsumed into the positive obligation analysis. The Court does assess the adequacy of the state's response — noting that "no proper evaluation of the resources necessary for neutralising the serious threat posed to the LGBT demonstrators was conducted by the authorities during the planning phase" and that "the police, instead of considering more effective measures which could have allowed the applicants to proceed with their peaceful event, limited their role to designing the dispersal plan as the only alternative" — but this is framed as part of the positive obligation assessment, not as a formal necessity test.

**(f) Positive obligation**

This is the central and explicitly discussed element. The Court states: "the Court considers that the police failed in their positive duty to ensure that the IDAHO event of 17 May 2013 could take place peacefully (see Plattform 'Ärzte für das Leben', cited above, §§ 32 and 34)." The Court elaborates: "Whilst the domestic authorities were obviously free in the choice of means which would have enabled the event to take place without disturbance, the Court finds it regrettable that no proper evaluation of the resources necessary for neutralising the serious threat posed to the LGBT demonstrators was conducted by the authorities during the planning phase. Despite being fully aware of the reality and magnitude of the risk, the police, instead of considering more effective measures which could have allowed the applicants to proceed with their peaceful event, limited their role to designing the dispersal plan as the only alternative... Such an attitude suggests that taking measures aimed at enabling the IDAHO event to take place had never been a real priority for the domestic authority." The Court concludes that the positive obligation was violated: "there has been a violation of the respondent State's positive obligations under Article 11 taken in conjunction with Article 14 of the Convention."
