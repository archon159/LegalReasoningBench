**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits section:

'The Court finds that the reason for banning the planned peaceful assembly was, if at all relevant, not sufficient to meet any pressing social need. The ban has therefore not been shown to be necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued.'

'Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness) as separate analytical steps. However, scope is implied by the Court's statement: 'The Court notes that the Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the ban on the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of his rights under that provision. It sees no reason to hold otherwise.' This implies that the assembly existed, that the applicant sought to participate, and that the assembly was peaceful (the Court later refers to it as a 'planned peaceful assembly'). The peacefulness element is also explicitly referenced: 'where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence' and 'the reason for banning the planned peaceful assembly.' So peacefulness is at least implied if not explicitly discussed as a sub-element.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: Implied affirmatively — the Court treats the planned demonstration as an assembly without question.
- **scope_participation**: Implied affirmatively — the applicant is treated as someone whose Article 11 rights were engaged.
- **scope_peaceful**: Implied/explicit — the Court refers to the 'planned peaceful assembly' and notes the demonstrators did not engage in acts of violence.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly addresses interference: 'The Court notes that the Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the ban on the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of his rights under that provision. It sees no reason to hold otherwise.' This is an explicit finding that there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly states: 'The Court observes that the ban was based on section 8(1) of the Assembly Act and considers that it was thus prescribed by law.' This is an explicit finding that the measure was prescribed by law. However, the Court does not separately discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. The conclusion is stated in a single sentence without elaboration on these sub-elements. The applicant had argued the authorities 'stretched' the provision, but the Court dismissed this without detailed sub-element analysis. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are therefore not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states: 'Moreover, the Court is satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others.' This is an explicit finding. The aims found are prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others (other road users).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It refers to principles from Körtvélyessy v. Hungary and states: 'a demonstration in a public place may cause a certain level of disruption to ordinary life including disruption of traffic. However, where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings if the freedom of assembly guaranteed by Article 11 is not to be deprived of all substance.' The Court then concludes: 'the reason for banning the planned peaceful assembly was, if at all relevant, not sufficient to meet any pressing social need. The ban has therefore not been shown to be necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued.' The necessity test is explicitly discussed and answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (a ban constituting an interference). Positive obligations are not mentioned.
