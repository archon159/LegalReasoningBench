**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the Court's assessment (Section B.2): "There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**Question 2: Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly identifies the assembly as "the March of Dissent in Samara," describing it as "a peaceful assembly" and "an opposition rally." The Court states: "The thrust of the applicant's complaint is that he was prevented from attending a peaceful assembly, the March of Dissent in Samara." The existence of an assembly is explicitly confirmed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's attempt to participate: "The applicant wanted to fly from Sheremetyevo airport to Samara to participate in the March of Dissent scheduled for the same day." It further states: "Nor is there any doubt that participation in such a rally would have been an exercise of the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly."

**iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly characterizes the assembly as peaceful: "The thrust of the applicant's complaint is that he was prevented from attending a peaceful assembly, the March of Dissent in Samara." Also: "Nor is there any doubt that participation in such a rally would have been an exercise of the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly."

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly finds interference: "The applicant's arrest and detention prevented him from attending the rally. There has, therefore, been an interference with his right to freedom of assembly." The Court also cites precedent: "A refusal to allow an individual to travel for the purpose of attending a meeting amounts to an interference with individual's freedom of assembly (see Kudrevičius, cited above, § 100; Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, § 84, 3 October 2013; and Huseynli and Others v. Azerbaijan, nos. 67360/11, 67964/11 and 69379/11, §§ 84-97, 11 February 2016)."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly finds that the interference was NOT prescribed by law: "The Court has already found that the applicant's arrest and detention were not 'lawful' within the meaning of Article 5 § 1 (c). Since the requirement under Article 11 § 2 that an interference with the exercise of freedom of assembly be 'prescribed by law' is similar to that under Article 5 § 1 that any deprivation of liberty be 'lawful', it follows that the applicant's arrest and detention were not 'prescribed by law' under Article 11 § 2."

The Court does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. It instead cross-references its earlier Article 5 § 1(c) finding. The overall conclusion (not prescribed by law) is explicit, but the sub-elements are not individually discussed.

**i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element; the conclusion follows by reference to the Article 5 finding.

**ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.

**iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states that because the interference was not prescribed by law, it is unnecessary to consider legitimate aim: "The conclusion that the inference was not 'prescribed by law' makes it unnecessary to consider whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was 'necessary in a democratic society' (see, as recent authority, Huseynli and Others, cited above, § 100)."

The Government had argued the aim was "ensuring the security of air travel by preventing travel by people who had no legal right to travel," which could relate to public safety or prevention of crime, but the Court did not assess this. No legitimate aim is found by the Court.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly not examined, for the same reason as legitimate aim: "The conclusion that the inference was not 'prescribed by law' makes it unnecessary to consider whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference by the state. This element is not discussed at all.
