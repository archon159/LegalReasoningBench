**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two sentences of the reasoning section:

"These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

"There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

The key reasoning paragraph leading to this conclusion states: "...the Court considers that the intervention of the police and the force applied were disproportionate and not necessary within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention. In the Court's view, the use of force by police could have had a chilling effect and discouraged the applicants and others from taking part in similar meetings."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. It notes: "the Court notes that it is not disputed between the parties that the applicants participated in the rally of 15 April 2007." The rally is treated throughout as a public assembly within the meaning of Article 11. This is explicit.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
Also explicitly discussed: "it is not disputed between the parties that the applicants participated in the rally of 15 April 2007." The Court confirms participation without dispute. This is explicit.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
Explicitly discussed: "it is not disputed between the parties that the applicants participated in the rally of 15 April 2007 and that their conduct was peaceful. It has not been alleged that they were among those who had, according to the Government, disturbed the public order during the assembly." The Court also cites the principle that "[a]n individual does not cease to enjoy the right to peaceful assembly as a result of sporadic violence or other punishable acts committed by others in the course of the demonstration, if the individual in question remains peaceful in his or her own intentions or behaviour." This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court, therefore, considers that the intervention by the police and the ill-treatment constituted an interference with the applicants' right under Article 11 of the Convention (İzci v. Turkey, no. 42606/05, § 82, 23 July 2013)." The Court also reiterates the general principle that "[t]he term 'restrictions' in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an assembly and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards." Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The Court addresses this element briefly and accepts it: "The Court is ready to accept that in the present case the use of force by police was 'prescribed by law'..." This is an explicit but summary acceptance without detailed sub-analysis.

**(i) Foreseeable?**
Not separately discussed. The Court simply accepts the "prescribed by law" requirement without breaking it into sub-elements. Implied yes (since the overall "prescribed by law" test is accepted).

**(ii) Accessible?**
Not separately discussed. Same as above — implied yes.

**(iii) Sufficient safeguards against abuse?**
Not separately discussed. Implied yes, since the Court accepts the "prescribed by law" requirement without qualification.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
Explicitly discussed: "The Court is ready to accept that in the present case the use of force by police was 'prescribed by law' and pursued the legitimate aim of prevention of disorder and crime." The Court accepts both prevention of disorder and prevention of crime as legitimate aims. Conclusion: yes.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Explicitly discussed and found to be violated: "the Court considers that the intervention of the police and the force applied were disproportionate and not necessary within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention. In the Court's view, the use of force by police could have had a chilling effect and discouraged the applicants and others from taking part in similar meetings." The Court bases this on its earlier finding of a violation of Article 3 (ill-treatment) and the domestic courts' finding that police exceeded their powers. Conclusion: no, the interference was not necessary (i.e., the necessity requirement was not satisfied — the interference was disproportionate).

**(f) Positive obligation**
Not discussed. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by state agents). No positive obligation analysis is undertaken. Conclusion: not discussed.
