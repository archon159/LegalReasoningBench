**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Analysis of canonical doctrine elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and its description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The existence of an assembly is implied by the phrase "public assemblies"; participation is implied by describing the applicants as "organisers or participants"; and the peaceful nature is implied by the fact that the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without any suggestion that the assemblies fell outside Article 11 protection.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — "organisers or participants of public assemblies"
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — "organisers or participants of public assemblies"
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court proceeds to full Article 11 analysis without excluding the assemblies from protection

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an interference in a dedicated analytical step, but it is clearly implied. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" confirms that an interference is treated as established. Additionally, the introductory paragraph describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of, which are the interferences. This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity. Given that the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it did not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment, which logically implies that a legitimate aim was assumed or not contested, but this is not stated. The legitimate aim element is not_discussed, and no specific aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The conclusion is "no" (necessity was not satisfied), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is not_discussed.
