**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final two paragraphs of the merits section state:

"Accordingly, the Court considers that in the instant case the forceful intervention of the police was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

"There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether a qualifying assembly existed, but it is implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers to the applicants "participating in the meeting," notes they had "obtained prior authorisation from the Adıyaman Governor to celebrate May Day in the Adıyaman Amphitheatre," and refers to them as "demonstrators." The existence of an assembly is thus implied by the Court's entire framing of the case.

**(ii) Participation:** Similarly, the Court implies the applicants participated in the assembly. The interference is described as "the subsequent arrest of the applicants for participating in the meeting," which implies their participation is taken as given.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly addresses this sub-element: "The Court also notes from the decision of the Public Prosecutor that the group had not presented a danger to public order, or engaged in acts of violence." And further: "where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence, it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings." The assembly is thus explicitly found to be peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed and found: "The Court considers that the police intervention and the subsequent arrest of the applicants for participating in the meeting constituted, in itself, an interference with the applicants' rights under Article 11."

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly finds the interference was prescribed by law: "it is noted that the interference in the present case had a legal basis, namely section 22 of the Assemblies and Marches Act (Law no. 2911), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention."

However, the Court does not separately discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. It simply states there was a legal basis without further analysis. These sub-elements are therefore not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: "As regards a legitimate aim, the Government submitted that the interference pursued the legitimate aim of preventing public disorder and the Court finds no reason to differ." The aim found is prevention of disorder.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed and found in the negative (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary): "the Court considers that in the instant case the forceful intervention of the police was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court reasons that the group had not presented a danger to public order or engaged in violence, that no prior warning was issued before force was used, and that authorities must show tolerance towards peaceful gatherings.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly mentions positive obligations in the context of its general principles: "although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities with the exercise of the rights protected, there may in addition be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these rights (see Djavit An, cited above, § 57; Oya Ataman, cited above, § 36; Balçık and Others, cited above, § 47)." It also states: "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to lawful demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens." However, the Court does not ultimately base its violation finding on a failure of positive obligation — it focuses on the disproportionate active interference by police. The positive obligation is mentioned as part of the general framework but not concluded upon as a separate violation. The conclusion is that the positive obligation framework is discussed but the Court does not make a separate finding of violation on that basis; the violation is grounded in the negative obligation (unjustified interference).
