**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

- 'Having regard to the above considerations, the Court concludes that Mr Navalnyy's arrest at the march and subsequent conviction for holding an unauthorised protest had not been necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued (see, mutatis mutandis, Körtvélyessy v. Hungary, no. 7871/10, § 31, 5 April 2016).'
- 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly define or discuss whether a qualifying 'assembly' existed, but it is implied throughout the reasoning. The Court refers to 'the march,' 'the protest organised by Mr Navalnyy,' and 'peaceful gatherings,' treating the event as an assembly without question. This is implied by the Court's direct engagement with the Article 11 framework and its references to 'the march' and 'the protest.'

*ii) Participation:* The Court states: 'Mr Navalnyy was arrested while walking peacefully with other participants in the march.' This implies that he participated in the assembly. It is implied rather than explicitly framed as a doctrinal sub-question.

*iii) Peaceful assembly:* The Court explicitly addresses this: 'Mr Navalnyy was arrested while walking peacefully with other participants in the march. Nothing suggests that he had shown any violent intentions or had called on the participants to engage in the acts of aggression (see, a contrario, Razvozzhayev v. Russia and Ukraine and Udaltsov v. Russia, nos. 75734/12 and 2 others, § 284, 19 November 2019).' This is an explicit finding that the assembly was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly addresses interference: 'It has not been disputed by the parties that Mr Navalnyy's arrest during the march and ensuing conviction for administrative offences constituted an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly.' This is an explicit, undisputed finding of interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court states: 'That interference was based on the relevant provisions of the domestic law (see Lashmankin and Others, cited above, §§ 216-312).' The Court accepts that the interference was prescribed by law by reference to its earlier analysis in Lashmankin. However, the Court does not separately and explicitly discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or adequacy of safeguards in this passage — it simply cross-references Lashmankin. The overall conclusion that the interference was 'based on' domestic law is explicit, but the sub-elements are not individually discussed in this excerpt. They are implied as having been resolved affirmatively (since the Court proceeds to examine legitimate aim and necessity without finding a violation at this stage).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states: 'That interference...pursued the legitimate aims of prevention of disorder and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.' Two legitimate aims are identified: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central and most extensively discussed element. The Court explicitly states: 'It remains to be assessed whether the interference was "necessary in a democratic society".' It then conducts a detailed analysis:
- The protest concerned a matter of public interest (fight against corruption).
- The authorities' refusal to approve the location based on traffic disruption risks was insufficient: 'any demonstration in a public place may cause a certain level of disruption to ordinary life, including disruption of traffic; this fact in itself does not justify an interference with the right to freedom of assembly.'
- The absence of prior authorisation does not give 'carte blanche to the authorities.'
- Mr Navalnyy was walking peacefully and showed no violent intentions.
- The domestic courts 'did not attempt to examine what the reasons were for arresting Mr Navalnyy in Tverskaya street' and 'justified his arrest and conviction by the mere reference to the "unlawfulness" of the march, without assessing whether it had been possible to ensure that the march proceed peacefully while minimising the disruption.'
- 'It was not demonstrated that the disruption caused by the march in Tverskaya street went beyond of what was inevitable in the circumstances or that the march represented a danger to public order.'
- 'The authorities should show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence...such tolerance has not been shown in the present case.'

The Court concludes the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation (interference by the state). No positive obligation is spelled out or found violated.
