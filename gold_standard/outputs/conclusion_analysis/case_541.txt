**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

'[T]he Court cannot but reach a conclusion similar to that in Christian Democratic People's Party, namely that the interference with their freedom of peaceful assembly was not proportionate to the aim pursued and that it did not meet a "pressing social need".

Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly refers to 'CDPP gatherings' and 'unauthorised demonstrations organised by the CDPP' throughout the reasoning. The existence of an assembly is treated as an established fact: 'The Court notes that the applicants were fined for taking part in unauthorised demonstrations organised by the CDPP.' This is explicitly discussed and affirmed.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court explicitly states: 'the applicants were fined for taking part in unauthorised demonstrations organised by the CDPP' and 'the present applicants were members and/or supporters of the CDPP organising and/or participating at the latter's peaceful gatherings.' Participation is explicitly confirmed.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court explicitly addresses this, quoting from Christian Democratic People's Party (para. 76): 'Since the CDPP's gatherings were entirely peaceful, there were no calls to violent overthrow of the Government or any other acts undermining the principles of pluralism and democracy...' and in the final paragraph: 'the present applicants were members and/or supporters of the CDPP organising and/or participating at the latter's peaceful gatherings.' The peaceful nature is explicitly confirmed.

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly identifies an interference. It states: 'the applicants were fined for taking part in unauthorised demonstrations' and in the conclusion: 'the interference with their freedom of peaceful assembly was not proportionate to the aim pursued.' The fining of the applicants constitutes the interference, and this is explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The Court does not explicitly resolve the 'prescribed by law' question as a standalone test. In para. 73 (quoted from Christian Democratic People's Party), the Court notes: 'there was a dispute as to the applicability of the provisions of the Assemblies Act to the CDPP's gatherings. The Municipal Council, which was the only authority empowered to issue authorisations under that law, considered the legislation unclear and refused to apply it to the CDPP until Parliament had given its official interpretation.... Hence, it would appear questionable whether non-compliance with the legislation in those circumstances would justify such a serious measure as a temporary ban. However, even assuming that the legislation was clear...' The Court leaves the question open ('even assuming') and proceeds to the proportionality analysis. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually and explicitly resolved. The foreseeability issue is touched upon implicitly (the law was 'unclear'), but no definitive conclusion is reached on 'prescribed by law' as a whole. The Court essentially bypasses this element by assuming it in favour of the government and proceeding to necessity.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Implicitly raised but not resolved — the Court notes the law was considered 'unclear' but proceeds on the assumption it was clear. No definitive conclusion.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss a specific legitimate aim. In para. 73, it notes that non-compliance with the Assemblies Act 'could be considered as a relevant and sufficient reason' — implying the government invoked some aim — but no specific aim from Article 11(2) is named. In the conclusion, the Court refers to 'the aim pursued' without specifying it. The legitimate aim element is not explicitly discussed, and no specific aim is identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
This is the central element explicitly discussed. The Court (quoting Christian Democratic People's Party, para. 71) states: 'only very compelling reasons would have justified the interference with the CDPP's right to freedom of expression and assembly.' It then examines the three grounds offered by the government (paras. 72-75) and finds none of them 'relevant and sufficient.' Para. 76 concludes: 'it cannot reasonably be said that the measure applied was proportionate to the aim pursued and that it met a "pressing social need."' The final paragraph of the present case applies this reasoning: 'the interference with their freedom of peaceful assembly was not proportionate to the aim pursued and that it did not meet a "pressing social need".' Necessity is explicitly discussed and the Court concludes it was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (the state fined the applicants, constituting a direct interference). No positive obligation is mentioned or implied.
