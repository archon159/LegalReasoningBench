**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "Bearing in mind the above circumstances, the Court concludes that the interference did not correspond to a pressing social need and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the planned demonstration constitutes an "assembly" within the meaning of Article 11. However, it is implied by the Court's entire analysis — it treats the planned protest demonstration as falling within the scope of Article 11 without question. The Court refers to "Hyde Park's application to hold a demonstration" and "the protest demonstration" throughout, implying that such a demonstration qualifies as an assembly. This is implied rather than explicit.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss participation. It is implied that Hyde Park (the applicant organisation) applied to hold the demonstration and was thus seeking to participate in/organise the assembly. This is implied.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court touches on this implicitly when it states: "There was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities. Rather their intention was to stage a protest concerning the Eurovision song contest and the manner in which the Moldovan authorities had organised the voting in Moldova." This passage implies the assembly was peaceful, though the Court does not frame it explicitly as a "scope" or "peaceful assembly" analysis. It is discussed in the context of necessity/proportionality rather than as a threshold scope question, so it is implied rather than a formal explicit scope finding.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "It is common ground between the parties, and the Court agrees, that the decision to reject Hyde Park's application to hold a demonstration on 25 February 2006 amounted to 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11." The conclusion is affirmative (yes, there was interference), and it is explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" requirement. It notes: "In so far as the lawfulness of the interference is concerned, the Court notes that under section 14 of the Assemblies Act the Chișinău Municipality was obliged to give reasons in writing for rejecting Hyde Park's application... According to section 12(6) of the Assemblies Act, an application could be rejected only if the Municipality was in possession of evidence that the provisions of sections 6 and 7 would be breached with serious consequences for society. Contrary to this provision, the Municipality rejected the application... Since the reasons relied upon by the Municipality to reject Hyde Park's application to hold a protest had nothing in common with those provided by sections 6 and 7 of the Assemblies Act, the Court could consider this in itself as a sufficient basis for the conclusion that the impugned measure was not 'prescribed by law'. However, in the present case, the Court considers that the issue of practical compliance with the law is indissociable from the question as to whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society'. It will therefore examine this issue below."

So the Court explicitly raises the prescribed-by-law issue but declines to resolve it separately, folding it into the necessity analysis. The overall conclusion on prescribed by law is therefore left unresolved as a standalone finding (null as a formal conclusion), though the Court strongly implies it was not prescribed by law.

**(i) Foreseeable?**
The Court does not separately discuss foreseeability as a sub-element. The discussion focuses on whether the Municipality's reasons were compatible with the statutory grounds for rejection, which relates more to compliance with the law than to foreseeability per se. Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element; not_discussed.

**(ii) Accessible?**
The Court does not discuss accessibility of the legal provision. Not_discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse?**
The Court implicitly addresses safeguards by noting that section 12(6) of the Assemblies Act limits the grounds on which an application can be rejected (only if sections 6 and 7 would be breached with serious consequences), and that the Municipality acted outside those grounds. This could be read as an implied discussion of safeguards, but the Court does not frame it in those terms explicitly. Not_discussed as a formal sub-element.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states it does not consider it necessary to decide this point: "The parties also disagreed as to whether the interference served a legitimate aim. The Court, for the reasons set out below, does not consider it necessary to decide this point either." The conclusion is therefore null (not decided), and the discussion is explicit (the Court explicitly declines to rule on it). No aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. Key passages include: "the Court observes that the Municipality rejected Hyde Park's application to hold the protest demonstration planned for 25 February 2006 on grounds which were not provided in sections 6 and 7 of the Assemblies Act... Such reasons cannot be considered compatible with the requirements of Article 11 of the Convention. There was never any suggestion that the organisers intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities... The Court finds it unacceptable from the standpoint of Article 11 of the Convention that an interference with the right to freedom of assembly could be justified simply on the basis of the authorities' own view of the merits of a particular protest. Therefore, the Court can only conclude that the Municipality's refusal to authorise the demonstration did not respond to a pressing social need." And finally: "the Court concludes that the interference did not correspond to a pressing social need and thus that it was not necessary in a democratic society." The conclusion is negative (the interference was NOT necessary), explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the state. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference case. Not_discussed.
