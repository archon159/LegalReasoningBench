**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion.

**2. Analysis of canonical doctrine elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's framing of the case. The Court describes the complaints as being about "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences." This language implies that: (i) assemblies existed, (ii) the applicants participated in them, and (iii) the assemblies were peaceful (since the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 protection without excluding the applicants from its scope). These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not explicitly analyze whether there was an interference as a separate doctrinal step. However, it is clearly implied. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" confirms that the Court treats the dispersal of assemblies and the arrest/conviction of the applicants as interferences with Article 11 rights. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate element.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not discuss the "prescribed by law" requirement at all — neither explicitly nor by clear implication. The reasoning jumps directly to the necessity assessment. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are therefore not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing whether the interference served a legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only canonical element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its established case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and finds no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion. The necessity element is explicitly discussed and resolved in the negative (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. This element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
