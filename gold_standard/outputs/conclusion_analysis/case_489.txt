**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding. The Court does not assign explicit paragraph numbers in the excerpt provided, but both statements appear in the final portion of Section III.

**Question 2: Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

- **(i) Assembly exists:** Not explicitly discussed. However, the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening sentence: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies." This implies that assemblies existed. The conclusion is implied (yes).

- **(ii) Participation:** Not explicitly discussed. The reference to the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies that they participated or attempted to participate. The conclusion is implied (yes).

- **(iii) Peaceful:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without questioning whether the assemblies were peaceful, which implies it accepted them as peaceful. The conclusion is implied (yes).

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" confirms that an interference was found. Additionally, the opening paragraph describes "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences" as the measures complained of. The conclusion is explicit (yes).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor do any of the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) receive explicit treatment. The Court jumps directly to necessity. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, this element is not discussed. The conclusion is null/not discussed.

- **(i) Foreseeable:** Not discussed.
- **(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.
- **(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. This element is not discussed, and no aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The conclusion is explicit (no — i.e., the interference was NOT necessary, which is why there is a violation).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
