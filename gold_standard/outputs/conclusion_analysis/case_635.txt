**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final section (c) Conclusion: 'Having regard to the findings in paragraphs 28, 47 and 50 above, the Court considers that the respondent State breached the requirements of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, seen in the light of Article 10. There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court discusses this explicitly, though in a nuanced way. In section (a) on solo demonstrations, the Court notes that 'the applicants' submission that both attempted to stage solo demonstrations, which happened to coincide as to the venue (the object being picketed) and the timing.' The domestic courts classified the situation as a group event. In section (b) on spontaneous assembly, the Court states: 'Alternatively, should it be accepted as convincingly established that the applicants did indeed take part in an "assembly" (with each other and/or others), seen in the context of Article 11 of the Convention...' This shows the Court explicitly discusses whether an assembly existed, treating it as an open question but proceeding on both hypotheses. The Court does not definitively resolve whether there was a group assembly, but proceeds under both scenarios (solo demonstrations and spontaneous assembly). The assembly question is explicitly discussed.

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly discusses the applicants' participation. In section (b): 'The Court observes that the applicants wanted to protest against a draft law prohibiting the adoption of Russian children by US nationals.' And: 'In the proceedings against each applicant the courts limited their assessment to establishing that he had taken part in a "picket" held without prior notification...' Participation is implied as established.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses this in section (a): 'each applicant's expressive conduct was peaceful and non-disruptive.' This confirms the assembly was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. In section 2 (The Court's assessment): 'The parties agreed that the termination of the applicants' participation in the event, their escorting to the police station, their retention there and their administrative conviction constituted an interference with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly and their right to freedom of expression.' This is an explicit finding of interference, agreed by both parties.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court addresses lawfulness briefly but does not conduct a full sub-element analysis. In section 2: 'As regards the "lawfulness" requirement, the Court refers to its findings under Article 5 § 1 of the Convention in paragraph 28 above relating to the applicants' escorting to the police station and retention there. The applicants submitted no convincing argument that other elements of the "interference" as described in paragraph 43 above had had no basis in the domestic law. Thus, the Court will focus in the present case on the arguments pertaining to other aspects of the applicable test...' The Court essentially passes over the lawfulness question for most elements, noting there is a domestic legal basis, and moves on to other aspects. The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually discussed. However, in section (a), the Court does implicitly raise a safeguards concern when discussing the 'reclassification rule' under section 7(1.1) of the PEA: 'the Court refers to its findings made in Novikova and Others...in relation to the PEA which, irrespective of a statutory distance requirement and compliance with it by simultaneous solo demonstrators, empowered courts to classify post factum a situation as a group event (the "reclassification rule" under section 7(1.1) of the PEA)...' This touches on the quality of the law (safeguards), but the Court does not explicitly conclude on prescribed by law — it moves to necessity. The overall prescribed by law question is discussed but not conclusively resolved (the Court sidesteps it by focusing on necessity). Given the Court's statement that it will focus on 'other aspects,' and that it ultimately finds a violation on necessity grounds, prescribed by law is discussed but left without a definitive conclusion (implied to be satisfied or at least not the basis for the violation).

*Foreseeable:* Not explicitly discussed. The Court notes the domestic legal basis exists but does not analyze foreseeability specifically.

*Accessible:* Not discussed.

*Safeguards:* Implicitly touched upon in the discussion of the reclassification rule (section a), but not explicitly concluded upon as a standalone sub-element.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or confirm a legitimate aim. In section (a), the Court mentions 'the intended statutory purposes (such as affording the authorities an opportunity to take timely and adequate measures to ensure the requisite order for running a given civic initiative and to secure public safety and protection of the rights of the event participants and others)' but frames these as purposes that could be achieved through other means. In section (b), the Court states: 'the Government have not given any convincing reason why it should have been "necessary in a democratic society" to make no exceptions to the application of the notification procedure...' The Court does not formally conclude that a legitimate aim was or was not established — it proceeds directly to necessity. The legitimate aim element is not explicitly resolved; it is implied to be potentially present (public safety, prevention of disorder, rights of others are mentioned in passing) but the Court does not make a formal finding.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and decided. In section (a): 'no compelling considerations relating to public safety, prevention of disorder or protection of the rights of others were at stake and relied upon when using this factual assertion for convicting each applicant specifically on account of the non-observance of the notification procedure... The only relevant consideration was the need to punish the unlawful conduct solely arising from the non-observance of the notification procedure. This was not sufficient in the absence of any aggravating element to justify the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of expression in the circumstances of the case.' In section (b): 'the Government have not given any convincing reason why it should have been "necessary in a democratic society" to make no exceptions to the application of the notification procedure, including its requirement of specific time-limits for notification, where it is impossible to comply with such time-limits. In the Court's view, the automatic and inflexible application of the notification time-limits without any regard to the specific circumstances of the present case amounted to an interference which was not justified under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.' The Court concludes necessity was NOT satisfied — the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss positive obligations in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by state authorities). No positive obligation analysis is present.
