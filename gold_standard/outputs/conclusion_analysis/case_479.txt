**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of the necessity section: "Accordingly, the Court considers that in the instant case the police's forceful intervention was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention. There has accordingly been a violation of that provision."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied throughout the reasoning. The Court refers to "the demonstration," "the group of demonstrators," "the rally," and "the gathering in question" without questioning whether an assembly existed. The existence of an assembly is taken for granted. Implied by the Court's direct engagement with interference and necessity.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicant participated in the assembly, but it is implied. The applicant is described as having "taken part in a demonstration" (in the Government's submission, which the Court does not contest) and as having not complied with dispersal orders. Implied.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly in the necessity section: "where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings." Earlier, the Court notes "There is no evidence to suggest that the group in question represented a danger to public order, apart from possibly disrupting traffic. There were at most fifty people, who wished to draw attention to a topical issue." The assembly is treated as peaceful, though this is discussed in the context of necessity rather than as a threshold scope question.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly states: "The Court notes at the outset that there is no dispute as to the existence of an interference in the applicant's right of assembly." This is an explicit finding that interference occurred, and it is undisputed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly states: "This interference had a legal basis, namely section 22 of Law no. 2911 on assemblies and marches, and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." The Court concludes the interference was prescribed by law, but does not separately analyze the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. These sub-elements are not discussed individually.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court simply states the interference had a legal basis without analyzing foreseeability. Not discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed. Not discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(iii) Safeguards:** Not explicitly discussed. Not discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aim: "The Court considers that the disputed measure may be regarded as having pursued at least two of the legitimate aims set out in paragraph 2 of Article 11, namely the prevention of disorder and the protection of the rights of others, specifically the right to move freely in public without restriction." Two aims are found: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. Key passages include: "the Court considers that in the instant case the police's forceful intervention was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court reasons that: (1) the group did not represent a danger to public order beyond possibly disrupting traffic; (2) there were at most fifty people; (3) the rally lasted only about half an hour; (4) the authorities showed impatience; (5) "where demonstrators do not engage in acts of violence it is important for the public authorities to show a certain degree of tolerance towards peaceful gatherings." The Court concludes necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly mentions positive obligations: "although the essential object of Article 11 is to protect the individual against arbitrary interference by public authorities with the exercise of the rights protected, there may in addition be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these rights (see Djavit An, cited above, § 57)." The Court also states: "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to lawful demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens" and "States must not only safeguard the right to assemble peacefully but also refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon that right." However, the Court does not separately conclude that the government violated a positive obligation — the violation is found on the basis of disproportionate interference (negative obligation). The positive obligation is mentioned as part of the general principles but not applied as a separate ground of violation. The conclusion on positive obligation is therefore null (mentioned but not resolved as a separate finding).
