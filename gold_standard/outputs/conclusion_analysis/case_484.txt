**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there was an assembly, the applicants participated in it, and it was peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to "the public assembly" and "the applicants' freedom of assembly")
- scope_participation: implied yes (the applicants are described as "participants of the public assembly")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without questioning the peaceful nature of the assembly)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states the applicants complained of "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of the public assembly, in particular about the dispersal of the assembly, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences." The Court then refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly," confirming that interference is found. This is explicit, though the Court does not dwell on it as a separate analytical step — it is treated as established by the facts.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly or implicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law. The analysis jumps directly to necessity. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not discussed at all.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity assessment without addressing this step.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law, particularly the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, and the general principles from Kudrevičius and Others v. Lithuania and Oya Ataman v. Turkey. The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this decision. The case is framed entirely as one of unjustified interference (negative obligation), not a failure to protect assembly rights.

**Summary of implied elements:** The scope elements and interference are implied by the Court's framing of the case and its reference to "interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly." Prescribed by law and legitimate aim are not discussed, which is consistent with the Court's streamlined approach in repetitive cases where it finds a violation at the necessity stage without needing to resolve earlier steps. The necessity element is the only one explicitly addressed and resolved.
