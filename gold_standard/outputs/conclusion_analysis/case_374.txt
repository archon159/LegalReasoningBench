**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of Section B ("4. Conclusion"): "In the light of the above, the Court accordingly holds that there has been a violation of the applicants' (except Ms Kotova, application no. 106/19) right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention."

Intermediate conclusions supporting this are found in:
- Section B.1 (Meeting at Klin Ice Palace): "his arrest, prosecution and conviction were not necessary in a democratic society."
- Section B.2 (Three protest actions): "the measures applied to the applicants as peaceful participants in the public assembly did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society. These complaints therefore disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."
- Section B.3 (Refusal to approve notices): "the measures applied to the applicant were not necessary in a democratic society and, in particular, were not proportionate to any legitimate aim pursued."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly accepts that assemblies existed. The applicants participated in a public meeting at the Klin Ice Palace, protest actions on the road leading to Aleksinskiy Quarry, and submitted notices for public events. The Court describes these as "a public meeting," "protest actions," and "public assembly" without questioning whether they constitute assemblies. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court refers to the applicants as "participants in a peaceful public assembly" (Section B.2) and describes their involvement in the various events. This is implied — the Court proceeds on the basis that the applicants participated without explicitly analyzing this sub-element.

**(iii) Peaceful:** The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies, particularly in Section B.2: "The Court observes that there is nothing in the present case file to indicate that the protest actions on the road were violent or that any of the applicants engaged in violent behaviour." In Section B.1, the Court notes: "The applicant was not found guilty of any violent acts." The Court also refers to the applicants as "peaceful participants in the public assembly" (Section B.2). This is explicitly discussed and the conclusion is that the assemblies were peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly states in the merits section: "It is not disputed in the present case that there has been an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly in all the applications concerned..." This is explicitly discussed and the conclusion is that there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly states: "It is not disputed in the present case that there has been an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly in all the applications concerned, that it was prescribed by law..." This is explicitly discussed (though conceded by the parties rather than analyzed in depth). The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually discussed — the Court simply accepts that the interference was prescribed by law without breaking it down into sub-elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states: "It is not disputed in the present case that there has been an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly in all the applications concerned, that it was prescribed by law and pursued the legitimate aim of providing safety to citizens and keeping public order." The aims identified are public safety and prevention of disorder. This is explicitly discussed. However, in Section B.3, the Court notes the measures were "not proportionate to any legitimate aim pursued," suggesting some doubt about whether a legitimate aim was even established in that context, though the overall framing accepts it.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court frames the dispute as follows: "The parties did disagree, however, and it remains to be ascertained, as to whether the interference was necessary in democratic society, that is to say, whether it corresponded to a 'pressing social need', was proportionate to the aim pursued and whether the reasons adduced by the national authorities to justify it were 'relevant and sufficient'." The Court then analyzes necessity for each group of episodes:
- Section B.1: "neither the applicant's arrest six days after the meeting nor his subsequent conviction for an administrative offence corresponded to a 'pressing social need' or was proportionate to the aim of ensuring public safety" and "the reasons given in support of his conviction for an administrative offence were not 'relevant and sufficient' for the purposes of Article 11 § 2" and "his arrest, prosecution and conviction were not necessary in a democratic society."
- Section B.2: "the measures applied to the applicants as peaceful participants in the public assembly did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society."
- Section B.3: "the measures applied to the applicant were not necessary in a democratic society and, in particular, were not proportionate to any legitimate aim pursued."
The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court makes a passing reference to a positive obligation in Section B.1: "it does not appear from the case material that the police assisted the applicant in any way to ensure its orderly conduct (see Frumkin, cited above, § 96) and the domestic courts summarily dismissed his argument concerning this, without any further inquiry." This implies a positive obligation on the state to assist organizers in ensuring orderly conduct, but the Court does not explicitly frame this as a standalone positive obligation analysis. It is mentioned as a factor in the necessity/proportionality assessment rather than as a separate positive obligation finding. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a distinct element, and the conclusion is that the government failed to meet it (at least in the context of application no. 51640/18).
