**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage reads: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant." Additionally, the Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements and were moreover not 'necessary in a democratic society'." These passages together confirm a finding of violation.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope** — The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's explicit finding of an "interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" logically implies that the scope conditions (assembly exists, applicants participated, assembly was peaceful) were satisfied. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied (yes) — the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and "public religious events," implying an assembly existed.
- scope_participation: implied (yes) — the Court refers to "the applicants'" freedom of assembly, implying their participation.
- scope_peaceful: implied (yes) — the Court treats the case as falling within Article 11 protection without any suggestion of violence.

**(b) Interference** — The Court explicitly discusses interference: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming this element is explicitly discussed and found to be present (yes).

**(c) Prescribed by law** — The Court explicitly addresses this element: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements." This is an explicit finding that the prescribed-by-law requirement was NOT met (conclusion: no). The Court does not separately and explicitly discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards, but by referencing the leading case Lashmankin and Others v. Russia and finding that the "quality of law" requirements were not met, these sub-elements are implied. In Lashmankin, the Court found issues with foreseeability and safeguards in particular. However, since the present judgment does not spell out which sub-elements fail, they are treated as implied rather than explicit.

- prescribed_by_law_foreseeable: implied (no)
- prescribed_by_law_accessible: implied (no)
- prescribed_by_law_safeguards: implied (no)

**(d) Legitimate aim** — The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. Given that the Court found the interference failed the "quality of law" test, it did not need to proceed to legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society** — The Court explicitly states the interference was "not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding (conclusion: no). The Court does not elaborate on the balancing exercise but references the Lashmankin case for the reasoning.

**(f) Positive obligation** — The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this passage. This element is not discussed.
