**Question 1: Does the Court find a violation of Article 11?**

Yes, the Court finds a partial violation of Article 11. Specifically, it finds a violation in relation to the blockade of Usukhchay village on 25 April 2006, but no violation in relation to the dispersal near Miskindzha or the arrest of the first applicant.

The violation finding is stated in the section on the blockade of Usukhchay: 'The Court concludes that, to the extent that the first and third applicants complained about the impossibility to demonstrate on the morning of 25 April 2006 in the village of Usukhchay, the response of the authorities was disproportionate. There was, therefore, a violation of Article 11 of the Convention on this account.'

The no-violation findings are: (1) regarding the Miskindzha dispersal: 'In the context of Article 11 the Court is prepared to conclude that the authorities' overall response to the blocking of the road and the aggressive behaviour of a big group of protesters was not disproportionate. It follows that there was no violation of Article 11 of the Convention on this account.' (2) regarding the first applicant's arrest: 'In such circumstances the Court is prepared to conclude that the two months' detention of the first applicant following the events of 25 April 2006 was not contrary to Article 11 of the Convention. Therefore, there was no violation of this Convention provision on that account.'

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly treats the events as involving a public assembly/demonstration throughout. The admissibility section states: 'the Court is prepared to consider that the blocking of the village of Usukhchay and the subsequent dispersal of the demonstration in Miskindzha, affected their rights under Article 11 of the Convention.' The existence of an assembly is treated as given and not contested.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly discusses whether the applicants participated. For the second applicant: 'The Court notes that the second applicant was arrested on 21 April 2006, and was therefore unable to take part in the demonstration of 25 April. His allegation that he had been arrested in order to prevent him from participating in the demonstration of 25 April 2006 has no support in the materials of the case. In the Court's opinion, the mere wish of a person to take part in a demonstration does not suffice to conclude that this person was affected by banning or dispersal of a public gathering.' For the first and third applicants: 'the Government did not dispute that the first and third applicants were amongst the protesters on 25 April 2006... they acknowledged having been at the scene at the time of the clash.'

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court explicitly discusses the peaceful character of the assembly, particularly in the context of the two phases. Regarding the first phase: 'The behaviour of the demonstrators in the morning of 25 April 2006 was non-violent as well.' Regarding the second phase: 'The Court recalls that Article 11 does not cover demonstrations where the organisers and participants have violent intentions... however, an individual does not cease to enjoy the right to peaceful assembly as a result of sporadic violence or other punishable acts committed by others in the course of the demonstration if the individual in question remains peaceful in his or her own intentions or behaviour.' The Court concludes: 'as a preliminary conclusion the Court is prepared to assume that during the second phase of the events of 25 April 2006 the protesters still enjoyed protection of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**(b) Interference:**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. In the admissibility section: 'the Court reiterates that the term "restrictions" in paragraph 2 of Article 11 must be interpreted as including measures taken before or during the public assembly – such as banning the event, dispersal of the gathering or the arrest of participants – and those, such as punitive measures, taken after the meeting.' The Court finds interference in the blockade of Usukhchay and the dispersal, and also considers the first applicant's arrest as an interference: 'the Court considers that his arrest can be regarded, on the arguable basis, as an instance of "interference" with his Article 11 rights.' The Court rejects interference findings for the second and third applicants' arrests: 'The Court concludes that the applicants' arrests and detention cannot be considered as an instance of "interference" with their rights guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention, insofar as the demonstration of 25 April 2006 is concerned.'

**(c) Prescribed by law:**

The Court discusses the 'prescribed by law' element explicitly in the context of the notification requirement. It states: 'Article 11 establishes a three-tier test: an interference will constitute a breach of Article 11 unless it is "prescribed by law", pursues one or more legitimate aims under paragraph 2 and is "necessary in a democratic society" for the achievement of those aims.'

**(i) Foreseeable:** The Court explicitly discusses foreseeability of the law: 'The Court is not called upon to give a definitive interpretation of the Public Gatherings Act. It simply observes that a norm cannot be regarded as a "law" if it is not formulated with sufficient precision... The word "lodging" used in the Public Gatherings Act is ambiguous... the organisers must have been excused for misinterpreting the law since the law itself was ambiguous.' The Court concludes: 'Mr A.'s refusal to allow the demonstration with reference to the allegedly belated "lodging" of the notice did not have a firm basis in the domestic law.' This implies the law was not sufficiently foreseeable in its application. However, the Court does not make a definitive finding that the law fails the prescribed-by-law test overall; it focuses more on the proportionality analysis. The Government argued: 'section 7 of the Public Gatherings Act had been properly published, and established clear and foreseeable rules governing public gatherings.' The Court's analysis of the ambiguity of the word 'lodging' suggests the foreseeability requirement was not fully met with respect to the notification deadline.

**(ii) Accessible:** The Government stated that 'section 7 of the Public Gatherings Act had been properly published,' implying accessibility. The Court does not explicitly dispute this. Accessibility is implied but not directly addressed as a separate sub-element.

**(iii) Safeguards:** The Court does not explicitly discuss safeguards against abuse as a separate sub-element of the prescribed-by-law test.

**(d) Legitimate aim:**

The Court explicitly discusses legitimate aims. Regarding the notification requirement: 'the duty to notify the authorities about a public event is not, as such, contrary to Article 11 of the Convention and that such a requirement pursues the legitimate aims of securing public order and protecting the rights and freedoms of others.' Regarding the blockade: 'it is clear that the actions of the police on that day pursued the legitimate aim of preventing disorder and crime.' Regarding the first applicant's arrest: 'the applicant's arrest and detention had a lawful basis... and pursued a legitimate aim of "prevention disorder or crime".' The aims found include: prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, and protection of the rights and freedoms of others (public safety is also referenced).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:**

The Court extensively discusses proportionality/necessity. Regarding the ban on the demonstration (decision of 17 April 2006): The Court finds the three reasons given (belated notice, insufficient park capacity, content-based restriction) were each insufficient or unjustified. 'Having regard to the above the Court concludes that the decision of Mr A. not to allow the demonstration, in the light of the reasons adducted in his letter of 17 April 2006, was unjustified.' Regarding the blockade of Usukhchay: 'the total blockade of the village was an excessive measure... the response of the authorities was disproportionate.' Regarding the Miskindzha dispersal: 'the authorities' overall response to the blocking of the road and the aggressive behaviour of a big group of protesters was not disproportionate.' Regarding the first applicant's arrest: 'the two months' detention of the first applicant following the events of 25 April 2006 was not contrary to Article 11 of the Convention.'

**(f) Positive obligation:**

The Court does not explicitly frame any finding in terms of a positive obligation under Article 11. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of negative obligations (interference, justification). Positive obligations are not discussed.
