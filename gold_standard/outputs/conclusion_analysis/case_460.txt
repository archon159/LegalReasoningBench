**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated in all seven episodes. The final conclusion is stated in the 'Overall conclusions' section:

'Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in all seven episodes.'

This is preceded by the structured conclusion: 'In short, the Court concludes that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of the fifth and sixth episodes, as the restrictions at issue failed to pursue a legitimate aim (see paragraph 126 above), and also in respect of the remaining five episodes because of the respondent State's failure to show that the restrictions were necessary in a democratic society (see paragraph 146 above).'

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:**
The Court explicitly discusses whether each of the seven events constitutes an 'assembly' within the autonomous meaning of Article 11. Key passages:

- Episode 1 (5 March 2012): 'The political rally at which the applicant and other participants (about 500 people) refused to leave the venue after the end of the authorised time slot was a continuation of a meeting notified under the Public Events Act... It undoubtedly constituted an assembly within the meaning of Article 11.'

- Episodes 2-4 (walkabouts): 'The Court observes that the "walkabouts" in question involved groups of persons who acted in a coordinated and purposeful way, in this particular instance for political purposes; these gatherings numbered approximately 170, 50 and 50-100 people respectively, and they were peaceful... the Court considers that they fell within the notion of "assembly" contained in Article 11.'

- Episode 5 (27 October 2012): 'The Court finds that irrespective of whether such conduct could in principle amount to a public event within the meaning of Russian law, in the specific circumstances of this episode and for the purpose of deciding on the applicability of Article 11 of the Convention, it cannot be separated from the applicant's participation in the stationary demonstration.'

- Episode 6 (24 February 2014, noon): The Grand Chamber endorses the Chamber's reasoning that 'the applicant's intention and his actual conduct were covered by the notion of "peaceful assembly" in Article 11 § 1 and thus fell within the scope of protection afforded by this provision.'

- Episode 7 (24 February 2014, evening): 'The Court has no doubt that this event constituted an assembly within the meaning of Article 11, and considers that the applicant's conduct fell within the ambit of this provision.'

The general principle is also stated: 'the right to freedom of assembly covered both private meetings and meetings in public places, whether static or in the form of a procession; in addition, it can be exercised by individual participants and by the persons organising the gathering.'

**ii) Participation:**
The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation in each episode. For the walkabouts: 'The applicant intended to take part in those public gatherings and has never denied it.' For episode 7: 'The applicant has not denied his intention to participate in this gathering.' For episode 5, the Court notes the applicant's participation in the stationary demonstration even if he denied intending to lead a march. For episode 6, the Court finds the applicant's conduct fell within the scope of Article 11 even though he did not intend to hold a rally.

**iii) Peaceful:**
The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies. General principle: 'Article 11 of the Convention only protects the right to "peaceful assembly", a notion which does not cover gatherings where the organisers and participants have violent intentions.' In application: the walkabouts 'were peaceful'; episode 1 involved protestors whose 'behaviour remained non-violent'; episode 7 was a 'spontaneous stationary demonstration.' The Court repeatedly notes the absence of violence across all episodes.

**(b) Interference:**
The Court explicitly finds interference in all seven episodes. The Government itself accepted interference: 'The Government... accepted that the dispersal of the persons gathering and the law-enforcement measures against the applicant had constituted an interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly.'

For each episode the Court confirms: arrests, transfer to police station, detention and administrative convictions constitute interference. General principle: 'The term "restrictions" in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during a gathering and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards.'

For episodes 1-4 and 7: 'his arrests, transfer to a police station, detention and the ensuing sanctions constituted "a restriction", within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11, and thus an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly.'

For episodes 5-6: 'The Court therefore finds that the applicant's arrests, transfer to a police station and the ensuing sanctions on these two occasions constituted an interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 § 2.'

**(c) Prescribed by law:**

The Court explicitly discusses the 'prescribed by law' requirement but ultimately defers the full analysis to the necessity/proportionality assessment.

**i) Foreseeability:**
Explicitly discussed and doubted: 'the circumstances in the present case suggest that, because of the overly broad nature of the aforementioned legal provisions, the foreseeability of their application could be called into question.' Specifically for episodes 5 and 6: 'The requirement on the latter two occasions that such conduct should have been subject to advance notification cannot reasonably be viewed as having been foreseeable, even under the most extensive interpretation of the Public Events Act.' The Court finds 'reason to doubt that the manner of application of the relevant law was sufficiently foreseeable to meet the quality requirement inherent in the autonomous notion of lawfulness under paragraph 2 of Article 11.'

**ii) Accessibility:**
Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element. The Court notes the measures 'could therefore be said to have had a basis in domestic law' but does not separately analyze accessibility of the legal provisions.

**iii) Safeguards:**
Explicitly discussed: 'Stressing the importance of adequate safeguards against arbitrary interferences by public authorities with the right to freedom of assembly, the Court cannot but note the broad terms used in the relevant provisions... the broad scope of the attendant duty of notification... and the broad definition of the offence.' The Court finds 'reason to doubt' the law met quality requirements and notes 'the existing legal framework thus afforded the executive authorities wide discretion in deciding what behaviour constituted a public event.' In the overall conclusions: 'it cannot be said that the relevant national law provided effective safeguards against abuse.' However, the Court explicitly defers the full prescribed-by-law analysis: 'the Court finds it more appropriate to incorporate that analysis in the broader proportionality assessment to be carried out under the necessity test below, i.e. in an extended review of whether the discretion enjoyed by the authorities in this area was accompanied by adequate safeguards against abuse.'

Given this deferral, the Court does not reach a definitive standalone conclusion on 'prescribed by law' — it raises doubts but folds the analysis into necessity. The overall conclusion on safeguards is effectively 'no' (inadequate safeguards), but this is reached as part of the necessity/proportionality analysis rather than as a standalone prescribed-by-law finding.

**(d) Legitimate aim:**
Explicitly discussed. The Government claimed 'prevention of disorder or crime' and 'protection of the rights and freedoms of others.'

For episodes 5 and 6: The Court finds no legitimate aim was pursued. 'The Court is therefore not satisfied that on the fifth and the sixth occasions the impugned measures taken against the applicant pursued any legitimate aim for the purposes of Article 11 § 2. For this reason alone there has been a violation of Article 11 in respect of each of these two episodes.'

For the remaining five episodes: 'The Court seriously doubts that any legitimate aim provided for in Article 11 § 2 was pursued, but sees no need to reach a firm conclusion on this point, considering that the interference was in any event not "necessary" for the reasons set out below.'

So for episodes 1-4 and 7, the Court leaves the legitimate aim question open and proceeds to necessity. For episodes 5-6, it finds no legitimate aim.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:**
Explicitly and extensively discussed for the five remaining episodes (1-4 and 7). The Court applies the proportionality test from Kudrevičius and Others.

Key findings:
- Episode 1: 'this gathering was dispersed and the applicant arrested for the sole reason that the demonstration was no longer covered by the authorisation permit and was therefore considered unlawful... In exercising the discretion afforded to them by the domestic law the authorities did not act in the manner compatible with the essence of the right to freedom of assembly.'

- Episodes 2-4 (walkabouts): 'In an ambiguous situation, such as the three examples at hand, it was all the more important to adopt measures based on the degree of disturbance caused by the impugned conduct and not on formal grounds, such as non-compliance with the notification procedure.'

- Episode 7: 'The authorities' interference with the spontaneous demonstration at hand was also an example of such an automatic and inflexible application of formal requirements which were found to be incompatible with the essence of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly.'

Overall: 'the Court cannot find that the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly as protected by the Convention was outweighed by any interests on the part of the respondent State in restricting the exercise of that freedom... The reasons relied on by the respondent State did not correspond to a pressing social need... there was no reasonable relationship of proportionality between the restrictions placed on the applicants' right to freedom of assembly and any legitimate aim pursued.'

The necessity test is answered negatively (i.e., the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society) — meaning the government failed to justify the interference.

**(f) Positive obligation:**
The Court discusses positive obligations in the general principles section: 'States must not only refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon the right to assemble peacefully but also safeguard that right... there may in addition be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these rights.' And: 'The authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to lawful demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens.'

However, the Court's analysis in this case is primarily focused on negative obligations (unjustified interference by the state). The positive obligation framework is mentioned in the general principles but is not the primary basis for the violation findings. The overall conclusions do note a 'structural inadequacy in the regulatory framework' and a 'persistent failure by the national authorities to show tolerance towards unauthorised but peaceful gatherings,' which has elements of positive obligation reasoning, but the Court does not explicitly conclude that a positive obligation was violated as a distinct finding. The discussion is more in the context of the negative obligation analysis.
