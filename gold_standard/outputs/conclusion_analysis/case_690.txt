**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is at the end of the merits section:

"The foregoing considerations are sufficient for the Court to conclude that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention, seen in the light of Article 10."

This conclusion is reached after applying the findings from Novikova and Others, particularly §§ 127-131 (on prescribed by law/foreseeability) and §§ 197-201 (on necessity/proportionality).

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed in the Article 11 sense. However, the entire case revolves around whether the applicant's coordinated solo demonstrations constituted a 'group event' under domestic law. The Court implicitly treats the events as falling within the scope of Article 11 by proceeding to examine the interference and justification. The domestic authorities classified the event as a group assembly, and the Court does not dispute that Article 11 is engaged. This is implied rather than explicit.

*Participation:* The applicant organised the demonstrations. The Court notes: "the applicant submitted that he had organised solo demonstrations by his co-workers rather than a group event." His role as organiser is not disputed. This is implied by the Court's engagement with the merits.

*Peaceful:* The Court does not explicitly address whether the assembly was peaceful, but §201 states: "Undoubtedly, no such considerations were at stake before or during Mr Romakhin's demonstration," referring to public safety/security risks. §199 also notes the demonstrators "were merely standing in a peaceful and non-disruptive manner at a distance of some fifty metres from each other." This implies the assembly was peaceful.

**(b) Interference**

The interference is explicitly discussed and accepted by both parties: "It is common ground between the parties that the 'interference' in the present case consisted in the applicant being taken to the police station and held there for some time, and his prosecution for an administrative offence." The Government also "accepted that the circumstances of the case disclosed an 'interference' under both Articles 10 and 11 of the Convention." Conclusion: yes, explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element by reference to Novikova and Others §§ 127-131. The key passage: "The Court considers that the above considerations (in particular, in §§ 127-31) are applicable in the present case in so far as the impugned legislation was applied in 2009 for prosecuting the applicant for organising what the authorities classified as a group event rather than several solo demonstrations."

*Foreseeable:* Explicitly discussed. §131 (quoted in the text): "the legislation in force was not sufficiently foreseeable as to what conduct or omission could be classified as an offence on account of a breach of the notification requirement under the Public Assemblies Act, where there was a doubt as to whether the event in question was a group event... simultaneous solo demonstrations or merely one solo demonstration." The Court finds the law was NOT sufficiently foreseeable. Conclusion: no.

*Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed. The Court focuses on foreseeability rather than accessibility. Conclusion: null, not discussed.

*Safeguards:* Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element. The Court's concern about the 'reclassification rule' (§193) touches on the risk of abuse, but this is framed under necessity rather than as a safeguards analysis. Conclusion: null, not discussed as a distinct sub-element.

Overall prescribed by law: The Court finds the law was not sufficiently foreseeable (at least for the 2009 events, before the 2012 amendments). However, the Court does not formally conclude that the interference was NOT prescribed by law — it applies the Novikova findings and then moves to necessity. The overall conclusion on prescribed by law is effectively 'no' (not sufficiently foreseeable), though the Court reaches its violation finding primarily through the necessity analysis.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court addresses legitimate aim in the context of the necessity analysis. At §199: "the Court cannot see what legitimate aim, in terms of Article 10 of the Convention, the authorities genuinely sought to achieve." The Court references public safety, prevention of disorder, and protection of the rights of others as potential aims (§199: "no compelling consideration relating to public safety, prevention of disorder or protection of the rights of others was at stake") but finds none were genuinely pursued. The Court thus finds no legitimate aim was established. Conclusion: no, explicitly discussed. No aims found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed at length. The Court applies §§ 190-201 from Novikova and Others. Key passages include §198: "the intended primary purposes... would, normally, be fully attainable through the reasonable application of the distance requirement, without any 'pressing social need' for the 'reclassification rule'..." and §199: "the Court cannot see what legitimate aim... the authorities genuinely sought to achieve. It fails to discern sufficient reasons constituting a 'pressing social need' for convicting for non-observance of the notification requirement." The Court also notes at the end: "The domestic courts failed to provide reasons, which would be sufficient, for classifying the events on 12 December 2009 as a single group event under the PEA and, foremost, for punishing the applicant as its organiser." Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society), explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state to facilitate or protect the assembly. The entire analysis is framed as a negative obligation (unjustified interference). Conclusion: null, not discussed.
