**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraphs of the merits assessment:

'The foregoing considerations are sufficient to enable the Court to conclude that the ban on the events organised by the applicant did not correspond to a pressing social need and was thus not necessary in a democratic society. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the Pride March and picketing constitute an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11. However, this is implied by the Court's entire analysis, which proceeds on the assumption that Article 11 applies. The Court refers to 'the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly guaranteed by Article 11 § 1 of the Convention,' implying that the events fall within the scope of the right. The sub-element is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**ii) Participation:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicant participated or attempted to participate. However, it is implied throughout: 'The Court observes that the Moscow authorities imposed a ban on the Pride March and picketing in 2006, 2007 and 2008 and enforced the ban by dispersing events held without authorisation and by finding the applicant and other participants who had breached the ban guilty of an administrative offence.' The applicant's role as organiser is treated as given.

**iii) Peaceful:** The Court does not explicitly address whether the assembly was peaceful as a threshold matter, but it is implied. The Court refers to 'the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly' and notes that 'the purpose of the marches and picketing, as declared in the notices of the events, was to promote respect for human rights and freedoms and to call for tolerance towards sexual minorities.' It also notes that 'the participants had not intended to exhibit nudity, engage in sexually provocative behaviour or criticise public morals or religious views,' and that the events were intended to be 'strictly peaceful and orderly events.' The Court also reiterates that 'the guarantees of Article 11 of the Convention apply to all assemblies except those where the organisers and participants have violent intentions or otherwise deny the foundations of a democratic society,' implying the assembly here was peaceful. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a threshold question.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference: 'The Court observes that the Moscow authorities imposed a ban on the Pride March and picketing in 2006, 2007 and 2008 and enforced the ban by dispersing events held without authorisation and by finding the applicant and other participants who had breached the ban guilty of an administrative offence. There is accordingly no doubt that there has been an interference with the exercise of the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly guaranteed by Article 11 § 1 of the Convention. In fact, the existence of the interference in the present case is not in dispute between the parties.' This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court notes the dispute but explicitly declines to rule on it: 'The Court further notes that the parties disagreed as to whether the Moscow authorities' acts were prescribed by law. They also disagreed as to whether the interference served a legitimate aim. However, the Court may dispense with ruling on these points because, irrespective of the aim and the domestic lawfulness of the ban, it fell short of being necessary in a democratic society.' The Court does, however, touch on the lawfulness issue in the context of proportionality: 'The Court observes, however, that these reasons do not constitute grounds under domestic law for banning or otherwise restricting a public event.' This is discussed but left unresolved as a formal finding. The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are not individually discussed. The overall 'prescribed by law' question is raised but not decided, so the conclusion is null.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly notes the dispute and declines to rule definitively, but does engage with the two aims put forward by the Government in the context of proportionality. The Government argued: (1) public safety and prevention of disorder; (2) protection of morals; and (3) protection of the rights and freedoms of others. The Court states: 'the Court may dispense with ruling on these points because, irrespective of the aim and the domestic lawfulness of the ban, it fell short of being necessary in a democratic society.' However, it then addresses both alleged aims in the proportionality analysis. On public safety/prevention of disorder: 'the Government failed to carry out an adequate assessment of the risk to the safety of the participants in the events and to public order.' On protection of morals: 'The Court is not convinced that the Government may at this stage substitute one Convention-protected legitimate aim for another one which never formed part of the domestic balancing exercise. Moreover, it considers that in any event the ban was disproportionate to either of the two alleged aims.' The Court does not formally find a legitimate aim established; the conclusion is null (not decided). The aims discussed (though not formally accepted) are public safety, prevention of disorder, and protection of morals.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central and explicitly discussed element. The Court finds that the ban was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages:

- 'the Court cannot accept the Government's assertion that the threat was so great as to require such a drastic measure as banning the event altogether, let alone doing so repeatedly over a period of three years.'
- 'The Court is therefore unable to accept the Government's claim to a wide margin of appreciation in the present case. It reiterates that any decision restricting the exercise of freedom of assembly must be based on an acceptable assessment of the relevant facts... the Court cannot but conclude that the authorities' decisions to ban the events in question were not based on an acceptable assessment of the relevant facts.'
- 'The foregoing considerations are sufficient to enable the Court to conclude that the ban on the events organised by the applicant did not correspond to a pressing social need and was thus not necessary in a democratic society.'

The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the government's interference was not necessary), so the answer to 'was it necessary?' is 'no,' meaning the right was violated on this ground.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly discusses positive obligations in the context of the State's duty to protect peaceful assemblies from counter-demonstrators. It quotes from Bączkowski: 'Genuine and effective respect for freedom of association and assembly cannot be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere; a purely negative conception would not be compatible with the purpose of Article 11 nor with that of the Convention in general. There may thus be positive obligations to secure the effective enjoyment of these freedoms... This obligation is of particular importance for persons holding unpopular views or belonging to minorities, because they are more vulnerable to victimisation.'

The Court then applies this: 'It is thus the duty of Contracting States to take reasonable and appropriate measures to enable lawful demonstrations to proceed peacefully... the authorities effectively endorsed the intentions of persons and organisations that clearly and deliberately intended to disrupt a peaceful demonstration in breach of the law and public order.' And: 'the authorities had failed even to attempt to comply with their obligation under Article 11 to take reasonable and appropriate measures to enable lawful demonstrations to proceed peacefully. They had banned the events, which in their view were likely to be attacked, instead of protecting them.'

The Court finds that Russia violated its positive obligation by banning the events rather than taking measures to protect them. The conclusion is that the positive obligation was violated (i.e., the government did NOT fulfill it).
