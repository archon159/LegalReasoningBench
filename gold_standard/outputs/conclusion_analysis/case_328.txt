**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Analysis of canonical doctrine elements**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated (as "organisers and/or participants"), and the assembly must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 would not apply). These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: The introductory paragraph refers to "public assemblies" and the applicants as "organisers and/or participants," implying an assembly existed. Implied, conclusion: yes.
- *scope_participation*: The applicants are described as "organisers and/or participants of public assemblies." Implied, conclusion: yes.
- *scope_peaceful*: Not explicitly discussed, but implied by the Court's application of Article 11 protections. Implied, conclusion: yes.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the penultimate paragraph: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The introductory paragraph also describes the measures: "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Interference is explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). These are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its prior case-law. Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or assessed. Not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) without elaborating further. Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. Not discussed.
