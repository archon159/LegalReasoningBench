**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits assessment: 'The Court therefore concludes that the measures in question were not necessary in a democratic society (see, mutatis mutandis, Musegh Saghatelyan, cited above, §§ 249-254). Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The existence of an assembly is explicitly discussed. The Court refers to 'the demonstrations held after the presidential election of 19 February 2008 at Freedom Square and their dispersal in the early morning of 1 March 2008.' The assembly at Freedom Square is treated throughout as an established fact. This is explicit.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses the applicant's participation: 'The applicant in the present case was similarly an active supporter of the opposition and was also regularly present at the demonstrations, including during the police operation of 1 March 2008.' Furthermore, the Court notes that 'the actual charges against the applicant and his subsequent conviction concerned his behaviour and acts which he had allegedly committed during the assembly at Freedom Square when exercising his Article 11 rights.' This is explicit.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court explicitly addresses this: 'As regards the Government's claim that the assembly at Freedom Square was not peaceful and therefore did not fall within the scope of Article 11, the Court notes that it has already examined and dismissed a similar claim by the Government, finding that there was not sufficient and convincing evidence to conclude that the organisers and the participants of the assembly at Freedom Square had had violent intentions and that the assembly in question had not been peaceful (see Mushegh Saghatelyan, cited above, §§ 229-233).' The Court concludes the assembly was peaceful. This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and concludes that there was an interference: 'The Court therefore concludes that there has been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly on account of his prosecution and conviction.' The Court also reiterates the general principle: 'The term "restrictions" in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an assembly, such as a prior ban, dispersal of the rally or the arrest of participants, and those, such as punitive measures, taken afterwards, including penalties imposed for having taken part in a rally.' This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly declines to decide this question: 'In the present case, the Court does not consider it necessary to decide whether the interference was prescribed by law and pursued a legitimate aim having regard to its conclusions set out below, regarding the necessity of the interference.' Therefore, the overall 'prescribed by law' question is mentioned but left unresolved. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not discussed at all.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

As noted above, the Court explicitly states it does not consider it necessary to decide whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. However, the Government had argued the aim was 'the dispersal of the unlawful assembly and the confiscation of illegal arms and ammunition,' which could relate to public safety and prevention of disorder. The Court does not adopt or endorse any legitimate aim. The question is explicitly raised but left unresolved.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed and decided. The Court concludes the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages: 'The Court therefore considers that the domestic courts in the present case similarly failed to carry out a thorough and objective establishment of the facts underlying the charges against the applicant and to demonstrate the rigour and scrutiny which, in the particular circumstances of the case and given the overall context, were required of them in order to ensure an effective implementation of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly guaranteed by Article 11. In such circumstances, it cannot be said that the reasons adduced by the domestic courts to justify the interference were genuinely "relevant and sufficient" or that the courts based their decisions on an acceptable assessment of the relevant facts. The Court therefore concludes that the measures in question were not necessary in a democratic society.' This is explicit, with a negative conclusion (i.e., the interference was not necessary, so the answer to whether necessity was satisfied is 'no').

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of a negative interference with the applicant's rights. This element is not discussed.
