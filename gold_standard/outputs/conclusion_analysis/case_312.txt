**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits assessment:

- 'It finds that the proceedings against the applicants in the present cases and the ensuing administrative detention were arbitrary and unlawful measures aimed at punishing the applicants for their political activity, including their previous participation in opposition protests, and preventing them from participating in or organising such protests.'
- 'The applicants' arrests and administrative detention could not but have had the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies. Those measures undoubtedly had a chilling effect, which deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate.'
- 'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied throughout the reasoning. The entire analysis proceeds on the assumption that opposition protests and political rallies constitute assemblies within the meaning of Article 11. The Court references 'opposition protests,' 'demonstrations,' and 'political rallies' throughout, and the applicants are described as 'active participants in opposition protests.' This is implied rather than explicitly stated as a doctrinal step.

*ii) Participation:* The Court implies that the applicants participated (or sought to participate) in assemblies. It notes that 'all of them were members of the PFPA and had actively participated in various protests held by the opposition' and that the measures were aimed at 'preventing them from participating in or organising such protests.' This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate doctrinal element.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assemblies were peaceful. However, the entire framing of the case — treating the arrests as unlawful interference with Article 11 rights — implies that the assemblies were peaceful. There is no suggestion of violence. This is implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. The Government argued 'there had been no interference with the applicants' rights under Article 11 of the Convention since they had been arrested and convicted for various administrative offences in circumstances unrelated to any public assembly.' The Court rejects this, finding that 'the proceedings against the applicants in the present cases and the ensuing administrative detention were arbitrary and unlawful measures aimed at punishing the applicants for their political activity, including their previous participation in opposition protests, and preventing them from participating in or organising such protests.' The Court also found that 'the applicants' arrests and administrative detention could not but have had the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies.' Interference is thus explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law. It characterises the measures as 'arbitrary and unlawful,' which implies that the interference was NOT prescribed by law (or at least not lawfully applied). However, the Court does not go through the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) in any structured way. The finding of 'arbitrary and unlawful' interference suggests a negative conclusion on this element, but it is implied rather than explicitly discussed through the canonical sub-tests.

*Foreseeable:* Not explicitly discussed; implied negative (arbitrary and unlawful).
*Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed.
*Safeguards:* Not explicitly discussed, though the Court notes that 'the domestic courts failed to establish facts that were disputed between the parties... by carrying out an objective and thorough examination,' which touches on the absence of adequate safeguards. This is implied.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. Given that it found the measures to be 'arbitrary and unlawful' and aimed at punishing political activity, the implication is that no legitimate aim was served. However, the Court does not go through the list of legitimate aims in Article 11(2). This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court does not explicitly apply the necessity/proportionality test. It does not balance the intensity of the interference against a legitimate aim. This is consistent with its finding that the measures were arbitrary and unlawful — once that is established, there is no need to proceed to necessity. This element is not discussed as such, though the overall conclusion implies it.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the government. The case is framed entirely as one of negative interference. This element is not discussed.

**Summary of approach:** The Court takes a shortcut approach, relying heavily on its prior judgment in Huseynli and Others v. Azerbaijan. It finds that the facts are essentially the same, that the measures were arbitrary and unlawful interferences with Article 11 rights, and concludes there was a violation. It does not systematically work through the prescribed-by-law, legitimate aim, and necessity tests, instead treating the arbitrary and unlawful nature of the interference as sufficient to establish the violation. The scope elements (assembly, participation, peaceful) are implied by the framing of the case.
