**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and finds no reason to depart from those findings.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" being interfered with, and describes the applicants as "organisers and participants of public assemblies." This implies that there was an assembly, that the applicants participated (or tried to), and that the assemblies were within the scope of Article 11 protection. The peacefulness of the assemblies is not explicitly addressed but is implied by the fact that the Court proceeds to analyze the interference and necessity without questioning whether Article 11 applies at all.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied (yes) — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph.
- **scope_participation**: implied (yes) — the applicants are described as "organisers and participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied (yes) — the Court proceeds with the full Article 11 analysis without questioning peacefulness.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is explicitly discussed. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." The Court then confirms: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The use of the word "interferences" (plural) confirms the Court treats this element as established.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" requirement is not explicitly discussed. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its leading cases. There is no mention of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed, even impliedly, in the text provided.

- **prescribed_by_law**: not discussed (null)
- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not discussed (null)
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not discussed (null)
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not discussed (null)

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim element is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the Russian authorities. However, by proceeding to the necessity analysis, one might imply that a legitimate aim was assumed (as in the leading cases referenced), but the text itself does not state this. Given the absence of any explicit or clearly implied discussion, this is treated as not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the decisive finding, reached by reference to the leading cases of Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov and Others. The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is why there is a violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Positive obligations are not discussed in this reasoning section. The Court focuses entirely on negative obligations (unjustified interference by the state). There is no mention of any positive duty on the government to facilitate or protect the assemblies.
