**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. This is stated in the final two sentences of the merits section:

'The Court finds that there was no justification for the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of association. There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

The Court reaches this conclusion after finding that the domestic authorities acted arbitrarily in banning the applicant's meeting, relying on an unsubstantiated 'terrorist threat' while allowing the Moscow government's own festivities to proceed.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. In the interference section, it states: 'The applicant in the present case attempted to organise a meeting of local residents to protest against the Moscow government's town-planning policy.' The Court also recites general principles: 'the right to freedom of assembly covers both private meetings and meetings in public thoroughfares as well as static meetings and public processions.' The existence of an assembly (a planned public protest meeting) is explicitly acknowledged, though the Court does not dwell on it as a contested issue.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court explicitly notes the applicant's participation: 'the applicant was fined for having taken part in an unauthorised assembly.' The applicant also organised the meeting. This is explicitly discussed as part of the interference analysis.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The applicant's complaint is framed as concerning 'a peaceful assembly' and the Court's general principles section refers to 'the right of peaceful assembly enshrined in Article 11.' The peaceful character of the assembly is implied rather than explicitly contested or analyzed — the Court proceeds on the assumption that it was peaceful without making it a separate finding.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses and finds interference. In the section titled 'Whether there was interference,' it states: 'The Court considers that these measures – taken before, during and after the planned meeting – amounted to interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly. Accordingly, its task is to determine whether the interference was justified.' The measures identified were: withdrawal of permission on the eve of the meeting, dispersal by police, and a fine for participating in an unauthorised assembly.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The prescribed-by-law requirement is raised by the applicant ('the applicant pointed out that the interference was devoid of legal basis because the legislation in force at the material time did not allow the prefect to revoke a decision authorising a public assembly') and the Government responds by invoking the Suppression of Terrorism Act. However, the Court does not explicitly resolve this sub-question. Instead, it proceeds directly to assess whether the interference was justified on the merits (necessity/proportionality), finding a violation on those grounds without separately concluding on lawfulness. The Court's assessment section is titled 'Whether the interference was justified' and the Government's submissions address 'lawfulness, legitimate aim and necessity' together, but the Court's own assessment focuses entirely on the factual basis for the terrorist threat claim and the arbitrariness of the ban. The prescribed-by-law element is therefore raised but not explicitly resolved by the Court.

- **Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed by the Court in its own assessment.
- **Accessible:** Not explicitly discussed.
- **Safeguards:** Not explicitly discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government claimed the interference served the prevention of disorder and crime (specifically terrorist attacks) and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others. The Court notes: 'The Government justified the lawfulness, legitimate aim and necessity of the interference by reference to information about a possibility of a terrorist attack.' However, the Court does not separately conclude that a legitimate aim was established. Instead, it proceeds to find that the factual basis for the claimed aim was not substantiated, effectively collapsing the legitimate aim and necessity analyses. The aims invoked by the Government were prevention of disorder, prevention of crime (terrorism), and protection of rights of others. The Court does not formally accept or reject these aims as legitimate — it finds the interference unjustified because the factual basis was not proven.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central focus of the Court's assessment. The Court explicitly discusses necessity, applying the general principles that 'there must be convincing and compelling reasons to justify an interference with this right' and that the Court must determine whether the interference was 'proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued' and whether the reasons were 'relevant and sufficient.' The Court finds that: (1) the Government failed to produce any evidence substantiating the terrorist threat; (2) the domestic courts shifted the burden of proof improperly onto the applicant; (3) the fact that only the anti-government meeting was cancelled while the government's own festivities proceeded is a strong indication of arbitrariness. The Court concludes: 'the domestic authorities acted in an arbitrary manner' and 'there was no justification for the interference.' Necessity is explicitly discussed and found not to be satisfied.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does mention in its general principles that 'States must not only safeguard the right to assemble peacefully but also refrain from applying unreasonable indirect restrictions upon that right.' However, this is stated as a general principle and the case is analyzed as one of direct interference (negative obligation), not as a failure to fulfill a positive obligation. The positive obligation dimension is not separately discussed or concluded upon as a distinct issue in this case.
