**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope requirements are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for there to be an interference with their freedom of assembly. These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants and an organiser (application no. 39118/12) of public assemblies."
- *scope_participation*: implied yes — the applicants are described as "participants and an organiser" of the assemblies.
- *scope_peaceful*: implied yes — the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies were non-peaceful, and the leading cases cited (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly refers to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" in the necessity analysis: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." Interference is thus explicitly acknowledged, though not analyzed in depth as a separate step.

Conclusion: yes, explicitly discussed (implied by the necessity analysis referencing "the interferences").

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its leading case findings. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity conclusion. No aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the explicit conclusion on necessity, albeit reached by reference to leading cases rather than detailed analysis.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative interference with the right to freedom of assembly.
