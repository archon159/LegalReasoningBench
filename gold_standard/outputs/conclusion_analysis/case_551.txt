**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section: "Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court reaches this conclusion on the basis that the interference was not prescribed by law, following its earlier reasoning in Mkrtchyan v. Armenia.

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicant participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful as separate analytical steps. However, the Court implicitly affirms all three sub-elements by stating: "The Court considers that the applicant's conviction for participation in an unauthorised march undoubtedly interfered with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly." This sentence implies: (i) there was an assembly (a march), (ii) the applicant participated in it, and (iii) it was peaceful (the Court characterises it as falling within "freedom of peaceful assembly" without any suggestion to the contrary). These are implied rather than explicitly discussed as separate questions.

**(b) Interference**

The interference element is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "It was not in dispute between the parties whether there had been an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly. The Court considers that the applicant's conviction for participation in an unauthorised march undoubtedly interfered with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly." The conclusion is affirmative and explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

This element is explicitly and centrally discussed. The Court recalls the Mkrtchyan precedent and finds: "Article 180.1 of the CAO was not formulated with such precision as to enable the applicant to foresee, to a degree that was reasonable in the circumstances, the consequences of his actions, since there was no legal act applicable in Armenia which contained the 'prescribed rules' referred to in that provision. The USSR Law of 28 October 1988 was no longer applicable and a new law on assemblies and rallies was adopted only on 28 April 2004. The Court concluded that the interference was not prescribed by law." The Court then applies this to the present case: "It therefore does not see any reasons to depart from its finding reached in the case of Mkrtchyan. It follows that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly was not prescribed by law."

- **Foreseeability:** Explicitly discussed and found to be absent. The Court quotes from Mkrtchyan that the provision was "not formulated with such precision as to enable the applicant to foresee, to a degree that was reasonable in the circumstances, the consequences of his actions." Conclusion: no.

- **Accessibility:** The Government argued the rules "were accessible and formulated with sufficient precision," but the Court implicitly rejects this by finding the law not prescribed by law overall. The accessibility sub-element is mentioned by the Government but not separately resolved by the Court — the Court's overall finding of "not prescribed by law" subsumes it. The primary reason given is foreseeability/precision (no applicable legal act), so accessibility is at best implied as not satisfied. However, since the Court does not separately address accessibility and focuses on foreseeability/precision, this is best characterised as implied (the overall "not prescribed by law" conclusion covers it) with conclusion "no."

- **Safeguards:** Not discussed at all.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states it does not need to examine this element: "Having reached this conclusion, the Court does not need to verify whether the other two requirements (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) set forth in paragraph 2 of Article 11 have been complied with." Accordingly, no legitimate aim is identified and the element is not discussed on the merits.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Same as legitimate aim — explicitly stated to be unnecessary to examine. Not discussed on the merits.

**(f) Positive obligation**

No positive obligation is discussed anywhere in the judgment. This element is entirely absent.
