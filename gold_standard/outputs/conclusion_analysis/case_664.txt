**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final paragraph of the section:

"These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention in respect of each applicant."

Additionally, the penultimate paragraph states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements and were moreover not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope (assembly exists, participation, peaceful)**

The scope elements are not explicitly discussed. However, they are implied by the Court's reasoning. The Court proceeds directly to analyze interference and the justification tests, which logically presupposes that there was an assembly, that the applicants participated (or tried to), and that the assembly was peaceful. The reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and "restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events" implies all three scope sub-elements are satisfied.

- *scope_assembly_exists*: implied (yes) — the Court refers to "public events" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether an assembly existed.
- *scope_participation*: implied (yes) — the applicants are described as having been subject to restrictions on their participation in public events.
- *scope_peaceful*: implied (yes) — the Court applies Article 11 without any suggestion that the assemblies were non-peaceful; the leading case Lashmankin is applied wholesale.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements..."

Also: "The applicants complained principally of the restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events."

Conclusion: yes, interference is explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses the "quality of law" (prescribed by law) requirement: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements."

This is an explicit finding that the prescribed-by-law requirement was NOT satisfied. However, the Court does not separately discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards — it simply states the overall "quality of law" standard was not met, referencing the Lashmankin leading case. The sub-elements are therefore implied from the overall finding rather than individually addressed.

- *prescribed_by_law* (overall): explicitly discussed, conclusion: no (requirements not met).
- *prescribed_by_law_foreseeable*: implied (no) — follows from the overall quality of law finding.
- *prescribed_by_law_accessible*: implied (no) — follows from the overall quality of law finding.
- *prescribed_by_law_safeguards*: implied (no) — follows from the overall quality of law finding.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. Given that the Court found the interference failed the "prescribed by law" test and the necessity test, it did not need to separately address legitimate aim. The legitimate aim element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court explicitly addresses this: "were moreover not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

Conclusion: explicitly discussed, conclusion: no (not necessary in a democratic society).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this passage. This element is not discussed.
