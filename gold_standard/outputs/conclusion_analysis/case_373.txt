**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is established in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the measures applied to the applicants as participants in a peaceful public assembly did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society." It then concludes: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The overall scope question is addressed, primarily by reference to the leading case Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia: "in case Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, §§ 51-75, 4 December 2014, the Court established, inter alia, that the gathering in question fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention." This is an explicit reference to scope being established, though the Court does not independently re-examine each sub-element in detail — it relies on the prior case.

- **i) Assembly exists:** Implied affirmatively. The Court refers to "a peaceful public assembly" and to the Navalnyy and Yashin case establishing that "the gathering in question fell within the scope of Article 11." The assembly's existence is treated as given.

- **ii) Participation:** Implied affirmatively. The Court refers to "the applicants as participants in a peaceful public assembly" — participation is treated as established without separate discussion.

- **iii) Peaceful:** Implied affirmatively. The Court repeatedly describes the assembly as "a peaceful public assembly" (both in the introductory paragraph and in the necessity analysis), treating the peaceful character as given.

**(b) Interference**

Interference is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a standalone element. The Court describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants in a peaceful public assembly, namely their arrest followed by their conviction for an administrative offence" and refers to "the measures applied to the applicants." The existence of interference is treated as self-evident from the arrest and conviction, consistent with the approach in the cited leading cases (Frumkin, Kasparov, Navalnyy and Yashin). It is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate doctrinal step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

This element is not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to necessity without addressing whether the interference was prescribed by law, foreseeable, accessible, or accompanied by adequate safeguards. None of the three sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

This element is not explicitly discussed. The Court does not identify any legitimate aim pursued by the Russian authorities. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without first establishing a legitimate aim. The aims_found list is therefore empty.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only element explicitly and substantively discussed. The Court states: "the measures applied to the applicants as participants in a peaceful public assembly did not correspond to a pressing social need and were thus not necessary in a democratic society." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation finding.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Positive obligations are not discussed in this decision. The case is framed entirely as one of unjustified interference (negative obligation), not as a failure to protect or facilitate assembly (positive obligation).
