**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. This is stated in the final paragraph of the assessment section: "Consequently, there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court implicitly accepts that there was an assembly. It refers to "the applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating" and discusses "the demonstration" throughout. The Government also argued the demonstration was "outside the scope of Article 11" due to its violent character, which the Court addresses differently (under necessity rather than scope). The existence of an assembly is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court states: "The applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Kassianos school in Nicosia." The applicant's participation is implied by this statement, though not explicitly analyzed as a separate sub-element.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Government argued the demonstration was "clearly outside the scope of Article 11" due to its violent character. The Court does not exclude the assembly from the scope of Article 11 on grounds of violence; instead, it finds an interference and proceeds to the justification analysis. However, the Court does note the violent character of the demonstration in the necessity analysis: "the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent" and "the intervention of the Turkish and/or Turkish-Cypriot forces was not due to the political nature of the demonstration but was provoked by its violent character." The Court treats the violence as relevant to necessity/proportionality rather than as excluding the assembly from the scope of Article 11. The peaceful character is thus implicitly accepted for scope purposes (the Court proceeds to analyze interference), though the violence is noted in the necessity analysis.

**(b) Interference**
Explicitly discussed: "The Court considers that there has been an interference with the applicant's right of assembly (see Protopapa, cited above, § 104)." The Court finds interference affirmatively and explicitly.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
Explicitly discussed: "This interference had a legal basis, namely sections 70 and 71 of the Cypriot Criminal Code (see paragraphs 22-23 above) and section 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law (see paragraph 26 above), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." The Court also addresses the specific issue of whether TRNC laws can constitute a legal basis, referencing Foka v. Turkey and concluding that acts of TRNC authorities in compliance with laws in force in northern Cyprus should be regarded as having a legal basis in domestic law.

The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are NOT explicitly discussed. The Court simply concludes the interference was "prescribed by law" without separately analyzing these sub-elements. They are implied by the overall conclusion.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
Explicitly discussed: "There remain the questions whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society. The Government submitted that the interference pursued legitimate aims, including the protection of national security and/or public safety and the prevention of disorder and crime." The Court then proceeds to analyze the necessity question in light of these aims, implicitly accepting them as legitimate. The aims found are: national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, and prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Explicitly discussed at length. The Court references fundamental principles, notes the violent character of the demonstration, the breach of the UN buffer zone, and the violation of TRNC borders. It concludes: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2." The Court also notes the "wide margin of appreciation left to the States in this sphere." The necessity test is answered in favor of the government (i.e., the interference was necessary/proportionate), so the conclusion is "yes" (the interference was necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court briefly mentions positive obligations in passing: "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens (see Oya Ataman v. Turkey)." However, this is mentioned as a general principle in the context of the necessity analysis, not as a separate positive obligation claim. The Court does not analyze whether the government violated any positive obligation; it is mentioned only as background jurisprudence. This is not_discussed as a standalone element with a conclusion.
