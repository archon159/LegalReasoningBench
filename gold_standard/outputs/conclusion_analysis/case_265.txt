**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of the merits section: 'There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' This follows from two key findings: (i) 'the domestic courts did not give relevant and sufficient reasons for the conviction of the applicant under that provision and thus failed to afford him an important procedural safeguard arising from Article 11 of the Convention'; and (ii) 'the imposition of a prison sentence amounted to a disproportionate interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed, but it is implied by the reference to 'the demonstration in question' and by the Court's finding that the applicant's conviction amounted to an interference with his right to freedom of assembly. The existence of an assembly (the demonstration) is taken for granted.

*ii) Participation:* The Court states: 'the sole ground for the applicant's conviction under section 7(2) of Law no. 3713 for disseminating propaganda in support of the PKK was his participation in the demonstration in question and his alleged incitement of the group who was shouting slogans and threw stones at the police.' Participation is thus implied as established, not explicitly analyzed as a separate sub-element.

*iii) Peaceful assembly:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assembly was peaceful. The applicant was alleged to have incited stone-throwing at police, which could raise questions about peacefulness, but the Court does not address this sub-element directly. It is not discussed.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: 'The Court considers that the applicant's conviction amounted to an "interference" with the exercise of his right to freedom of assembly (see also Gülcü, cited above, §§ 93-97 and 102).' The conclusion is affirmative (yes, there was an interference).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court addresses this element but only in a summary, assumed manner: 'It is further prepared to assume that the interference was prescribed by law...' The Court does not separately analyze foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The overall prescribed-by-law element is discussed explicitly (though only assumed), but the three sub-elements are not individually discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: 'the national authorities have pursued the legitimate aims of protecting national security and preventing disorder and crime (compare also Faruk Temel v. Turkey, no. 16853/05, § 52, 1 February 2011).' The Court assumes (rather than firmly finds) these aims, but it is explicit. Aims found: national security, prevention of disorder, prevention of crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed at length: 'As regards the question whether the interference was necessary in a democratic society, the Court observes that the sole ground for the applicant's conviction under section 7(2) of Law no. 3713 for disseminating propaganda in support of the PKK was his participation in the demonstration in question and his alleged incitement of the group who was shouting slogans and threw stones at the police. However, the trial court did not demonstrate any connection or relevance between those actions and the scope and purpose of the offence of disseminating propaganda under section 7(2) of Law no. 3713. Therefore, and bearing in mind that in their reasoned judgments the domestic courts failed to indicate any other actions which could be regarded as disseminating propaganda in support of the PKK, the Court concludes that the domestic courts did not give relevant and sufficient reasons for the conviction of the applicant under that provision and thus failed to afford him an important procedural safeguard arising from Article 11 of the Convention.' Additionally: 'As regards the proportionality of the interference, the Court notes that, in the light of the foregoing, the imposition of a prison sentence amounted to a disproportionate interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly.' The conclusion is that the interference was NOT necessary (no).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. This element is not discussed.
