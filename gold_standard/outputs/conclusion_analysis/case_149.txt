**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**Question 2: Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied by the Court's reasoning. The applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies," and the Court proceeds to analyze interference and necessity, which logically presupposes that the assemblies fell within the scope of Article 11. The reference to "public assemblies" and "participants" implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated, and (iii) the assembly was peaceful (since the Court applies the full Article 11 framework without excluding the applicants from protection). These are implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" and the Court applies Article 11 without questioning whether an assembly existed.
- scope_participation: implied yes — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- scope_peaceful: implied yes — the Court applies the full Article 11 framework, which presupposes the assembly was peaceful; no suggestion is made that the applicants were violent.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the nature of the interference is described earlier: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis by reference to leading cases. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly identify or discuss any legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing whether the interference served a legitimate aim. This element is not discussed, and no specific aims are identified.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law, specifically the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, and by noting that "the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion." The necessity element is explicitly discussed and resolved in the negative (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
