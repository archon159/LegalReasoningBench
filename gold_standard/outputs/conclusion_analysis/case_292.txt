**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) and states it found no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss the three sub-elements of scope (existence of an assembly, participation, and peacefulness). However, scope is implied: the Court refers to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and describes them as "organisers or participants of public assemblies," which implies that (i) an assembly existed, (ii) the applicants participated (or tried to), and (iii) the assembly was peaceful enough to attract Article 11 protection. These conclusions are implied rather than explicitly reasoned.

- **(i) Assembly exists:** Implied by the reference to "public assemblies" in the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **(ii) Participation:** Implied by the same passage — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants."
- **(iii) Peaceful:** Implied — the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without excluding the assemblies from protection on grounds of violence.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The passage states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The opening paragraph also describes the measures: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." The existence of interference is thus explicitly acknowledged, though not separately analyzed as a distinct step.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. These elements are not discussed at all — the Court jumps directly to necessity. Given that the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it did not need to address prescribed by law. These sub-elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim. No specific aim from Article 11(2) is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and states it found no new fact or argument to depart from those findings.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. This element is not discussed.
