**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of the merits assessment: 'There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' This follows from the Court's finding that 'the refusal to approve the location of the applicant's public event by reference to the general ban on holding public events in the vicinity of court buildings or by reference to the ban, established by a regional law, on holding public events in Stefanovskaya Square in Syktyvkar, without any consideration to the specific circumstances of the case, could not therefore be regarded as being "necessary in a democratic society" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicant participated, or whether it was peaceful. However, these elements are implied by the structure of the analysis. The Court proceeds directly to discuss interference, which logically presupposes that the assembly falls within the scope of Article 11. The applicant's planned 'picket' is treated throughout as a public event protected by Article 11 without any question being raised about scope.

- **Assembly exists**: Implied. The applicant's planned public event (a 'picket') is treated as an assembly throughout. The Court states 'It has not been disputed between the parties that the proposal to change the location of the applicant's public event amounted to an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly,' which implies the existence of an assembly.
- **Participation**: Implied. The applicant is the organiser of the event; his attempt to hold it is the subject of the entire case.
- **Peaceful**: Implied. The Court refers to 'his right to freedom of peaceful assembly' without any suggestion that the assembly was not peaceful. No violence or disorder is attributed to the applicant.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: 'It has not been disputed between the parties that the proposal to change the location of the applicant's public event amounted to an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly (see Lashmankin and Others, cited above, §§ 404-09).' The conclusion is affirmative (yes, there was an interference).

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court frames the analysis by stating: 'It remains to be ascertained whether the interference was "prescribed by law", pursued one or more legitimate aims under paragraph 2 of Article 11, and was "necessary in a democratic society".' However, the Court does not explicitly resolve the 'prescribed by law' question. Instead, it moves directly to the necessity analysis, finding the interference not necessary in a democratic society. The Court notes the two legal provisions relied upon by the domestic authorities (the regional law and the town administration's decree), but does not separately conclude on foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The prescribed by law question is therefore raised but not resolved — the Court bypasses it by finding a violation on necessity grounds. This is a common approach in ECtHR judgments where the Court finds it unnecessary to resolve earlier elements once a violation is found on necessity. Accordingly:
- **Prescribed by law (overall)**: Not resolved — raised but bypassed.
- **Foreseeable**: Not discussed.
- **Accessible**: Not discussed.
- **Safeguards**: Not discussed explicitly, though the Court's criticism of the wide discretion of local executive authorities in determining 'immediate vicinity' (referencing Lashmankin) touches on the safeguards issue implicitly: 'the general ban under section 8 of the Public Events Act on holding public events in the vicinity of court buildings is incompatible with Article 11 § 2, taking into account its absolute nature coupled with the local executive authorities' wide discretion in determining what is considered to be "in the immediate vicinity".' This is framed as a necessity/proportionality point rather than a prescribed-by-law/safeguards point.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court raises the legitimate aim question as part of the standard formula ('It remains to be ascertained whether the interference was "prescribed by law", pursued one or more legitimate aims under paragraph 2 of Article 11, and was "necessary in a democratic society"'), but does not explicitly resolve it. The Government argued that the aim was to protect the independence of the judiciary and prevent pressure on judges (which would fall under 'rights of others' or 'prevention of disorder'). The Court does not make a finding on whether a legitimate aim was pursued, proceeding instead to find the interference not necessary. The legitimate aim is therefore not explicitly resolved. The Government's submissions reference public safety and protection of the judiciary, but the Court does not endorse or reject these as legitimate aims.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds that the interference was NOT necessary in a democratic society. Key passages:
- Regarding the vicinity-of-court ban: 'The Court has already found that the general ban under section 8 of the Public Events Act on holding public events in the vicinity of court buildings is incompatible with Article 11 § 2, taking into account its absolute nature coupled with the local executive authorities' wide discretion in determining what is considered to be "in the immediate vicinity" (see Lashmankin and Others, cited above, §§ 431-42). The Court does not see any reason to reach a different conclusion in the present case. The refusal to approve the location chosen by the applicant for his public event by reference to the fact that it was to be held in the vicinity of the Constitutional Court of the Komi Republic could not, therefore, be regarded as being "necessary in a democratic society" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.'
- Regarding the regional law ban on Stefanovskaya Square: 'No arguments regarding risk of public disorder or security considerations have ever been advanced to justify the general ban on holding public events in Stefanovskaya Square.' The Court further notes the ban is not limited in time, applies to all public events irrespective of numbers, and has not been subject to 'exacting parliamentary and judicial review.' 'It follows that the Government have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons to justify the general ban on holding public events in Stefanovskaya Square in Syktyvkar.'
- Final conclusion: 'the refusal to approve the location of the applicant's public event by reference to the general ban on holding public events in the vicinity of court buildings or by reference to the ban, established by a regional law, on holding public events in Stefanovskaya Square in Syktyvkar, without any consideration to the specific circumstances of the case, could not therefore be regarded as being "necessary in a democratic society" within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention.'

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by the state with the applicant's right). No positive obligation is mentioned or implied.
