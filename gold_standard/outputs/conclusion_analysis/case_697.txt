**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraph of section D.1, which reads: "Considering all of the above, the Court finds that there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention on account of the municipality's unlawful prohibition of the applicant organisation's public rally of 19 April 2000."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assembly falls within the scope of Article 11, but this is implied by the entire structure of the analysis. The case concerns a planned public rally, which is paradigmatically an assembly. The Court proceeds directly to discuss interference, implying that the scope conditions are met.

- *Assembly exists*: Implied. The Court refers throughout to "the applicant organisation's public rally of 19 April 2000" without questioning whether this constitutes an assembly. The parties agreed there was an interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly, which presupposes an assembly exists.

- *Participation*: Implied. The applicant organisation planned and attempted to hold the rally; the prohibition prevented it from doing so. This is implied by the finding of interference.

- *Peaceful*: Implied. The Court refers to the right to "freedom of peaceful assembly" and neither party disputes the peaceful nature of the planned rally. The complaint is framed as an "unlawful interference with its right to freedom of peaceful assembly" from the outset.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed. In section D.1(a), the Court states: "The parties agreed that there had been an interference with the exercise of the applicant organisation's right to freedom of peaceful assembly within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention (see paragraphs 30 and 31 above). That view is shared by the Court." Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed. In section D.1(b), the Court states: "The Court observes that the Plovdiv Regional Court established in its judgment of 28 March 2001 that the municipality's prohibition of the applicant organisation's public rally of 19 April 2000 was issued in violation of the Meetings and Marches Act (see paragraph 19 above). Accordingly, the said prohibition represented an interference with the exercise of the applicant organisation's right to freedom of peaceful assembly which was not 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention." Conclusion: no (the interference was NOT prescribed by law).

The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually discussed. The Court simply adopts the domestic court's finding that the prohibition violated the Meetings and Marches Act, concluding it was not prescribed by law, without breaking this down into the three sub-criteria. These sub-elements are therefore not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. The Court explicitly states: "In the light of this conclusion, the Court is not required to determine whether the interference pursued one or more legitimate aims under paragraph 2 and whether it was 'necessary in a democratic society' for the achievement of those aims." Since the interference was not prescribed by law, the Court did not proceed to examine legitimate aim. No aims are found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Not discussed, for the same reason as legitimate aim. The Court explicitly states it is not required to determine this question.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. The Court's analysis is entirely framed in terms of a negative obligation (the state's prohibition constituting an unjustified interference). No positive obligation is identified or discussed.
