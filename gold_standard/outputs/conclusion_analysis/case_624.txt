**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the necessity section states: 'There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.' This conclusion is reached after the Court finds that (a) the authorities persisted in impeding Ilinden's commemorative events with substantially the same justification as in Stankov, which was found insufficient; (b) the authorities failed to protect participants from counter-demonstrators, violating positive obligations; and (c) one ban was imposed with almost identical reasoning even after similar measures had been declared contrary to Article 11.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:** The Court explicitly discusses this under 'Applicability of Article 11.' The meetings organised by Ilinden are treated as assemblies throughout. The Court states: 'Article 11 only enshrines the right to "peaceful assembly". Its protection does not therefore extend to demonstrations whose organisers and participants have violent intentions.' The existence of assemblies is implicit throughout and explicitly assumed.

**ii) Participation:** The applicants' participation (or attempted participation) is discussed throughout the interference section. The Court notes that 'the mayors had imposed formal bans on these meetings... These measures doubtless had an inhibiting effect on at least some of the members and followers of Ilinden who intended to participate in the events.' This is implied rather than separately analysed as a standalone sub-element.

**iii) Peaceful character:** Explicitly discussed under 'Applicability of Article 11': 'the Government have not disputed the peaceful character of the meetings organised by Ilinden. The Court further notes that in that previous case it found that those involved in the organisation of the prohibited meetings... did not have violent intentions... Likewise, the Court finds no indication that the organisers of and the participants in the meetings under consideration in instant case were contemplating resorting to violence. Article 11 is therefore applicable.'

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed under section 3 ('Whether there have been interferences'). The Court concludes: 'the Court concludes that there have been interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly on all occasions under examination, except the events of April 2001, August and September 2002, and August 2003.' The Court analyses each occasion individually, noting formal bans, police actions diverting meetings, and inhibiting effects on participants.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed under section 4(a). The Court accepts that the mayoral bans were prescribed by law, relying on the Meetings and Marches Act and the prior holding in Stankov: 'the Court accepts that the prohibitions imposed by the mayors were prescribed by law.' Regarding prosecution authority decrees, the Court notes that 'former Article 185 § 1 of the CCP, was formulated extremely vaguely and did not circumscribe the situations where the prosecution authorities could act,' but is 'prepared to accept, for the purposes of the present case, that the interferences in these instances were also prescribed by law.'

**i) Foreseeability:** The Court implicitly addresses foreseeability when noting that Article 185 § 1 CCP 'was formulated extremely vaguely and did not circumscribe the situations where the prosecution authorities could act.' This raises foreseeability concerns but the Court ultimately accepts the law as prescribed by law for purposes of the case. The sub-element is discussed but the conclusion is a qualified yes.

**ii) Accessibility:** Not explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element. The Court's acceptance that the law was prescribed by law implies accessibility is satisfied.

**iii) Safeguards:** The Court notes the vagueness of Article 185 § 1 CCP and that 'Such a power must however be used sparingly and only when indeed warranted,' implying concern about safeguards, but ultimately accepts the law for purposes of the case. This is implied rather than explicitly resolved as a standalone sub-element.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed under section 4(b): 'The Government submitted that the interferences pursued the following aims: protecting national security and the territorial integrity of the country, guaranteeing public order in the local community, protecting the rights and freedoms of others and preventing disorder and crime.' The Court accepts these: 'The Court accepts that the bans were intended to safeguard one or more of the interests cited by the Government, as it did in Stankov and the United Macedonian Organisation Ilinden.' Aims found: national security, public safety/prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly discussed under section 4(c). The Court finds the interferences were NOT necessary in a democratic society. It states: 'the Court perceives no material difference in the case at hand. It notes that... the authorities persisted in their efforts to impede the holding of the commemorative events which Ilinden sought to organise... the authorities' justification for so doing was substantially the same as in Stankov and the United Macedonian Organisation Ilinden... and thus insufficient to make the impugned measures necessary in a democratic society.' The Court also notes with concern that 'one of the bans was imposed, with almost identical reasoning, even after similar measures had been declared contrary to Article 11.'

**(f) Positive obligation**

Explicitly discussed under section 4(c). The Court recalls: 'genuine, effective freedom of peaceful assembly cannot be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere; it is the duty of Contracting States to take reasonable and appropriate measures to enable lawful demonstrations to proceed peacefully (see Plattform "Ärzte für das Leben" v. Austria...).' The Court finds the positive obligation was violated: 'the authorities were therefore bound to take adequate measures to prevent violent acts directed against the participants in Ilinden's rally, or at least limit their extent. However, it seems that they, while embarking on certain steps to enable the organisation's commemorative event to proceed peacefully, did not take all the appropriate measures which could have reasonably be expected from them under the circumstances, and thus failed to discharge their positive obligations under Article 11.'
