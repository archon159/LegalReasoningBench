**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraphs of the merits section:

'In the light of the above, the Court considers the restriction of the applicant's right to hold an assembly was not based on legal provisions that met the Convention requirements of quality of the law.'

'Having reached this conclusion, the Court does not need to verify whether the other two requirements (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) set forth in Article 11 § 2 have been complied with.'

'Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed as a separate threshold question, but it is implied throughout the reasoning. The Court refers to 'the prohibition on the applicant's holding the peaceful assembly he planned to hold' and discusses the interference with 'his rights guaranteed by Article 11.' The existence of a planned assembly is treated as a given. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate element.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court notes that the applicant was the organiser: 'the prohibition on the applicant's holding the peaceful assembly he planned to hold for an indefinite period constituted an interference with his rights guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention, regardless of whether he actually attempted to commence it or not on the day before the ban was imposed.' This implies that the applicant's participation/organisation is accepted, though not discussed as a separate sub-element. It is implied.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court refers throughout to 'peaceful assembly' and 'peaceful demonstrations.' The applicant's assembly is described as 'peaceful' without any dispute on this point. The Court states: 'the prohibition on the applicant's holding the peaceful assembly he planned to hold.' This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element — the Court treats it as uncontested.

**(b) Interference**
This is explicitly discussed under the heading '1. Whether there was an interference.' The Court states: 'The Court considers that the prohibition on the applicant's holding the peaceful assembly he planned to hold for an indefinite period constituted an interference with his rights guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention, regardless of whether he actually attempted to commence it or not on the day before the ban was imposed.' Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
This is explicitly discussed under the heading '2. Whether the interference was based on law.' The Court concludes: 'the Court considers the restriction of the applicant's right to hold an assembly was not based on legal provisions that met the Convention requirements of quality of the law.' Overall conclusion: no, the interference was not prescribed by law.

**(i) Foreseeable?**
The Court explicitly discusses foreseeability. It quotes from Vyerentsov v. Ukraine: 'it cannot be concluded that the "procedure" referred to in Article 185-1 of the Code on Administrative Offences was formulated with sufficient precision to enable the applicant to foresee, to a degree that was reasonable in the circumstances, the consequences of his actions.' The Court also notes that the Temporary Regulations of the Kharkiv municipality lacked a proper legal basis, and that 'in absence of clarity regarding the criteria for imposing restrictions on the freedom of assembly, the local authorities and the courts made a number of assumptions... that could be seen as rendering practically impossible any decision allowing the holding of a peaceful assembly.' Conclusion: no, the law was not sufficiently foreseeable.

**(ii) Accessible?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss accessibility as a separate sub-element. The general 'prescribed by law' analysis focuses on foreseeability and quality of law, but accessibility is not separately addressed. However, the Court does note that the law must be 'accessible to those concerned and formulated with sufficient precision' as a general standard, without separately concluding on accessibility. This is not_discussed as a standalone sub-element (the general standard is mentioned but no separate conclusion is drawn on accessibility alone).

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse?**
The Court implicitly addresses this when it notes that the Temporary Regulations 'appear to leave the issue of permission for any assembly in the city at the complete and unrestrained discretion of the local authorities.' This touches on the absence of safeguards against abuse, but it is framed within the broader foreseeability/quality of law analysis rather than as a distinct safeguards sub-element. It is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element. Conclusion: no adequate safeguards.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court explicitly states: 'Having reached this conclusion, the Court does not need to verify whether the other two requirements (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) set forth in Article 11 § 2 have been complied with.' Therefore, legitimate aim is not discussed and no conclusion is reached. The Court does mention in passing that Article 182 of the Code of Administrative Justice provides for restrictions 'in the interests of national security and public order,' to avert 'a real risk of disturbances or crime' or the endangering of 'the health of the population or the rights and freedoms of other people,' but this is only to note that such wording merely confirms the right is not absolute — it is not a finding that a legitimate aim was actually pursued. No aims are formally found.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Explicitly not discussed: 'Having reached this conclusion, the Court does not need to verify whether the other two requirements (legitimate aim and necessity of the interference) set forth in Article 11 § 2 have been complied with.' Conclusion: null (not discussed).

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this case. The analysis is entirely framed in terms of a negative interference with the applicant's rights. Conclusion: not discussed.
