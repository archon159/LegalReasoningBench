**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits section:

"Having regard to the foregoing considerations, the Court finds that the dispersal of the applicants' peaceful assembly cannot be regarded as having been necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued."

"Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an assembly existed as a separate question, but it is implied throughout the reasoning. The text refers repeatedly to "the applicants' assembly," "their spontaneous demonstration," and "their peaceful demonstration." The Government did not dispute that the applicants could rely on Article 11 guarantees. This is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate sub-element.

*ii) Participation:* Similarly implied. The applicants are described as participants in the demonstration. The Government did not dispute that the applicants could rely on Article 11 guarantees: "The Government did not dispute that the applicants could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11." This is implied.

*iii) Peaceful:* The peaceful nature of the assembly is explicitly discussed, particularly in the necessity section. The Court states: "a decision to disband the ensuing, peaceful assembly solely because of the absence of the requisite prior notice, without any illegal conduct by the participants, amounts to a disproportionate restriction on freedom of peaceful assembly." Also: "there is no evidence to suggest that the applicants represented a danger to public order beyond the level of the minor disturbance which is inevitably caused by an assembly in a public place." The domestic courts' failure to consider the peaceful nature is also noted: "The courts based their decision to declare the police measures lawful solely on that argument and did not take into account other aspects of the case, in particular, the peaceful nature of the event." The assembly is found to be peaceful — conclusion: yes.

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed: "The Government did not dispute that the applicants could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the dispersal of the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of the applicants' rights under that provision. The Court sees no reason to hold otherwise." Conclusion: yes, there was an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed as a whole: "It must therefore be determined whether the sanction complained of was 'prescribed by law'..." The Court then addresses it: "There was no dispute between the parties that the restriction imposed on the applicants' freedom of peaceful assembly was based on section 14 of the Assembly Act, the wording of which is clear. Therefore, the requirement of foreseeability was satisfied. The Court sees no reason to depart from the parties' view." Conclusion: yes, prescribed by law.

*i) Foreseeability:* Explicitly discussed: "the wording of which is clear. Therefore, the requirement of foreseeability was satisfied." Conclusion: yes.

*ii) Accessibility:* Not explicitly discussed. The Court only mentions foreseeability. Accessibility is not addressed as a separate sub-element. Conclusion: null, not discussed.

*iii) Safeguards against abuse:* Not explicitly discussed. Conclusion: null, not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: "In the light of these considerations, the Court is satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others." The Government had submitted that restrictions served "to protect the rights of others, for example the right to freedom of movement or the orderly circulation of traffic" and that the three-day time-limit was necessary to enable police coordination. Aims found: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others. Conclusion: yes.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds: "a decision to disband the ensuing, peaceful assembly solely because of the absence of the requisite prior notice, without any illegal conduct by the participants, amounts to a disproportionate restriction on freedom of peaceful assembly." And: "the Court finds that the dispersal of the applicants' peaceful assembly cannot be regarded as having been necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued." The Court also notes the special circumstances of spontaneous assemblies and the failure to give sufficient advance notice of the Prime Minister's attendance. Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary in a democratic society — i.e., the necessity test was not satisfied by the government).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Government raised the concept of positive obligations in the context of the legitimate aim discussion: "freedom of peaceful assembly could not be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere. On certain occasions, positive measures had to be taken in order to ensure its peacefulness." However, the Court does not explicitly analyze or rule on a positive obligation as a separate element of its own analysis. The Court's reasoning focuses on the negative obligation (unjustified interference). The positive obligation point is raised by the Government but not adopted or developed by the Court as a distinct finding. Conclusion: null, not discussed as a Court finding (only raised by the Government in argument).
