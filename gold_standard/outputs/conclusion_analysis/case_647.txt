**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage states: "Having examined all the material before it, the Court concludes that they also disclose violations of the Convention in the light of its well-established case-law" and specifically references, among others, "Novikova and Others v. Russia, nos. 25501/07 and 4 others, §§ 106-225, 26 April 2016, related to disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against participants of solo manifestations, Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, §§ 84-97, 3 October 2013, and Frumkin, cited above, §§ 102-42, concerning disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against participants of public assemblies."

The Court does not spell out the violation of Article 11 in a separate, dedicated paragraph with detailed reasoning. Instead, it uses a well-established case-law shortcut, incorporating by reference the reasoning from Novikova, Kasparov, and Frumkin, all of which concern Article 11 violations related to disproportionate measures against assembly participants. The conclusion of a violation of Article 11 is thus explicit, though the reasoning is by reference.

**Question 2: Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

Because the Court reasons entirely by reference to well-established case-law and does not conduct a step-by-step analysis of the canonical elements, none of the individual doctrinal elements are explicitly discussed in the text of this decision. However, some elements can be treated as implied by the Court's conclusion and the cases it cites.

**(a) Scope**
- Assembly exists: The Court references cases about "participants of solo manifestations" and "participants of public assemblies," implying that assemblies existed. This is implied, not explicitly discussed.
- Participation: Similarly implied by the reference to "participants" — the applicants participated or attempted to participate. Implied, not explicit.
- Peaceful: The cases cited (Novikova, Kasparov, Frumkin) all concerned peaceful assemblies. This is implied by the nature of the referenced cases, but not explicitly discussed in this text.

**(b) Interference**
The Court refers to "disproportionate measures taken by the authorities," which implies that there was governmental interference with the right to assembly. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not discussed at all in this text. The Court jumps directly to the proportionality/necessity analysis by reference. Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
No legitimate aim is identified or discussed in this text. Not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
The Court's reference to "disproportionate measures" implies a finding that the necessity/proportionality test was not satisfied. This is implied by the language used, though not explicitly analyzed step by step.

**(f) Positive obligation**
No positive obligation is discussed in this text. Not discussed.

**Summary:** The Court finds a violation of Article 11 by reference to well-established case-law. The scope (assembly, participation, peaceful nature) and interference elements are implied by the references to assembly participants and governmental measures. The necessity element is implied (negatively — measures were disproportionate). Prescribed by law, legitimate aim, and positive obligation are not discussed.
