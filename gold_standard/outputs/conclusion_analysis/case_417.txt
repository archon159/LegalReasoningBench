**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The relevant passage states: "Having examined all the material before it, the Court concludes that they also disclose violations of the Convention in the light of its findings in Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, §§ 81-142, 5 January 2016, as regards disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies, and Tsvetkova and Others v. Russia, nos. 54381/08 and 5 others, §§ 84-138, 10 April 2018, as regards unlawful administrative arrest."

The Court does not cite a specific paragraph number in this excerpt (it is a summary section referring to an appended table), but the conclusion of violation is explicit.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

This is a well-established case-law section that disposes of complaints by reference to leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia and Tsvetkova and Others v. Russia) rather than conducting a full independent analysis. The Court does not walk through each element of the Article 11 doctrine explicitly. Instead, it incorporates by reference the reasoning in those leading cases. Below is an assessment of each element:

**(a) Scope (assembly exists, participation, peaceful)**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, by finding violations "as regards disproportionate measures taken by the authorities against organisers and participants of public assemblies," the Court implicitly confirms that: (i) assemblies existed (the word "public assemblies" is used), (ii) the applicants were organisers or participants, and (iii) the assemblies were within the scope of Article 11 (otherwise no violation could be found). These elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

**(b) Interference**
The Court does not explicitly discuss interference as a standalone element. However, by finding violations concerning "disproportionate measures taken by the authorities" and "unlawful administrative arrest," interference is clearly implied — governmental acts (measures against participants, administrative arrest) are identified as the basis of the violation.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The Court does not explicitly discuss the "prescribed by law" sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The reference to "unlawful administrative arrest" in the context of Tsvetkova and Others could imply that the interference was not lawful, but this is not spelled out in the excerpt. The sub-elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No aims are identified in this excerpt.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
The Court does not explicitly conduct a necessity analysis. However, the reference to "disproportionate measures" (from Frumkin) implies that the necessity/proportionality test was considered and found to be failed — i.e., the measures were not necessary in a democratic society. This is implied rather than explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this excerpt. This element is not discussed.

**Summary:** The reasoning section is extremely compressed, disposing of complaints by reference to leading cases. The violation of Article 11 is explicit. Scope, interference, and necessity are implied by the language used ("public assemblies," "organisers and participants," "disproportionate measures," "unlawful administrative arrest"). The prescribed-by-law sub-elements and legitimate aim are not discussed. Positive obligations are not discussed.
