**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court explicitly concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The key passage is in the final paragraph of Section B.2 (The Court's assessment):

'There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11, Article 13 (in conjunction with Article 11) and Article 14 (in conjunction with Article 11) of the Convention in respect of each applicant.'

The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its earlier finding in the leading case of Alekseyev, stating: 'Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities did not correspond to a pressing social need and was thus not necessary in a democratic society.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11 exists. However, it is implied throughout the judgment — the Court refers to 'LGBT public events,' 'LGBT public assemblies,' and 'public events organised by the applicants' without questioning whether these constitute assemblies. The entire analysis proceeds on the assumption that the events in question are assemblies protected by Article 11. This is implied.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicants participated or attempted to participate. However, it is implied — the applicants are described as organizers who submitted notifications/applications to hold events, and the Court treats them as having standing to complain under Article 11. This is implied.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the assemblies were peaceful. However, the Court refers to 'freedom of peaceful assembly' and proceeds to analyze the case under Article 11 without questioning the peaceful nature of the events. This is implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court implies that there was an interference by referring to 'the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities' and 'the decisions of local authorities to refuse to approve the dates and the venues of various LGBT public events.' The Government's own submissions acknowledge that 'local authorities had not banned any public events organised by the applicants but had warned them of the consequences of breaking the law by holding such events' — but the Court proceeds to analyze the case as one involving an interference with Article 11 rights. The interference is implied rather than explicitly analyzed as a separate step, but the Court's reference to the ban and its necessity analysis presupposes an interference.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly analyze the 'prescribed by law' requirement in the merits section. The Government submitted that 'the impugned measures had been lawful and proportionate.' The applicants agreed that 'after the entry into force of the Russian federal law outlawing the promotion of homosexual relationships to minors in July 2013, the ban on LGBT public events had become lawful.' The Court does not make an explicit finding on this element in the merits assessment — it jumps directly to necessity. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not discussed. Given that the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without resolving the prescribed-by-law question, this element is effectively not discussed in the merits (though the admissibility section references the notification procedure and domestic law in the context of the six-month rule).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government invoked the 'protection of rights and freedoms of minors, their parents and others' as the legitimate aim — specifically 'the protection of rights and freedoms of minors' in the context of 'promotion of homosexuality in public places.' The Court does not explicitly accept or reject this as a legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis, finding the ban was 'not necessary in a democratic society' without first confirming whether a legitimate aim was served. The legitimate aim is mentioned in the parties' submissions but not explicitly resolved by the Court. This is not_discussed from the Court's own assessment perspective, though the Government's aim of 'rights_of_others' (protection of minors) is referenced.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only element explicitly discussed by the Court in its own assessment: 'the Court considers that in the instant case the ban on holding LGBT public assemblies imposed by the domestic authorities did not correspond to a pressing social need and was thus not necessary in a democratic society.' The Court also references the proportionality principle by citing Bączkowski and Others v. Poland on 'the proportionality of interference with it.' The applicants also argued that 'the blanket refusals by local authorities on repeated occasions, without considering any alternative venues for the assemblies, had not been proportionate to the legitimate aims invoked by the Government.' The Court's conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary), which is the basis for the violation.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this judgment. The case is analyzed entirely as a negative obligation case (the state banned/refused to approve assemblies). No positive obligation framework is invoked.

**Summary of findings:**
- Violation: YES
- Scope (overall): implied yes
- Assembly exists: implied yes
- Participation: implied yes
- Peaceful: implied yes
- Interference: implied yes
- Prescribed by law: not discussed (no explicit finding)
- Prescribed by law - foreseeable: not discussed
- Prescribed by law - accessible: not discussed
- Prescribed by law - safeguards: not discussed
- Legitimate aim: not explicitly resolved by the Court (Government invoked rights_of_others/protection of minors, but Court did not confirm)
- Necessity: explicitly discussed, conclusion NO (not necessary = violation)
- Positive obligation: not discussed
