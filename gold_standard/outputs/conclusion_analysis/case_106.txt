**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" and its description of the applicants as "organisers or participants of public assemblies" implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied. The Court would not have proceeded to assess interference and necessity without implicitly accepting that the assemblies existed, that the applicants participated, and that the assemblies were peaceful (since the leading cases cited — Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov — all involved peaceful assemblies).

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the Court refers to "public assemblies" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether assemblies existed.
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the applicants are described as "organisers or participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court proceeds to assess the interference and necessity without suggesting the assemblies were violent; the leading cases cited all involved peaceful assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, and the opening paragraph describes "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." This confirms that interference is explicitly found.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The Court jumps directly to the necessity assessment. Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without reaching prescribed by law, this element is not discussed. It is not even implied, as the Court's reasoning does not require a finding on this element to reach its conclusion.

- **prescribed_by_law**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_foreseeable**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_accessible**: not discussed
- **prescribed_by_law_safeguards**: not discussed

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity assessment. The legitimate aim element is not discussed, and no specific aim is identified in the reasoning.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately justify the interference), leading to the violation. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law, particularly the leading cases of Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, and Kasparov.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning section. The case is framed entirely as one of negative interference (arrest, dispersal, conviction), not as a failure to protect assembly rights. Positive obligation is not discussed.
