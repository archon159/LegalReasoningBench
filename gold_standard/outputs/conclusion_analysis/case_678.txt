**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there must have been an assembly, the applicants must have participated in it, and it must have been peaceful (otherwise Article 11 protection would not apply). These are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- scope_assembly_exists: implied yes (the Court refers to "public assemblies" in the opening: "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies")
- scope_participation: implied yes (applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies")
- scope_peaceful: implied yes (the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 protection without questioning the peaceful nature)

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference. The opening paragraph states: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as the applicants' arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." Furthermore, the Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interferences" is used explicitly, confirming that interference is found. This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor any of its sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards). The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis. These elements are not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified. The Court proceeds directly to the necessity analysis without addressing legitimate aim. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the necessity requirement was not met (i.e., the government did not adequately balance the intensity of the intervention with the weight of the legitimate aim). The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its case-law, particularly the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation in this reasoning. This element is not discussed.
