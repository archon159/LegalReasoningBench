**Question 1: Violation of Article 11**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final paragraph of the reasoning section states: "Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court reaches this conclusion by finding that the interference with the applicants' right of assembly cannot be considered lawful under domestic law, because the protests were staged in accordance with the applicable laws, remained peaceful, and did not disturb public order in any way.

**Question 2: Canonical Elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court implicitly treats the applicants' protests as constituting an assembly. The text refers to "the applicants' protests" and their "right of assembly," implying that an assembly existed. This is not explicitly analyzed as a separate sub-question but is implied throughout.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court refers to the applicants' arrest and their staging of protests, implying they participated in the assembly. Again, this is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate element.

**(iii) Peaceful:** This sub-element is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the applicants' protests were staged in accordance with the old and new laws concerning assemblies... that they remained peaceful, and that they did not disturb public order in any way. This conclusion is supported by the videos attached to the case-file and by the conclusions of the domestic courts which acquitted the applicants."

**(b) Interference:**

Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "The Court considers that the applicants' arrest constituted 'interference by [a] public authority' with their right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11." The Government also "did not dispute the existence of an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly."

**(c) Prescribed by law:**

The Court addresses this element, though not by going through the sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) individually. Instead, the Court takes a shortcut: it finds that the interference was not lawful under domestic law at all, because the protests complied with the applicable laws. The Court states: "the interference with their right of assembly cannot be considered lawful under domestic law." This is an explicit discussion of the "prescribed by law" requirement, but the conclusion is negative (the interference was not prescribed by law), and the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards are not individually addressed — the Court simply finds the interference unlawful because the protests were conducted in compliance with the law.

**(i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed as a sub-element. The Court's finding that the interference was not lawful under domestic law implies a negative conclusion on "prescribed by law" overall, but foreseeability is not separately analyzed.

**(ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim:**

Not discussed. The Court short-circuits the analysis at the "prescribed by law" stage and does not proceed to examine whether the interference served a legitimate aim.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society:**

Not discussed. As with legitimate aim, the Court does not reach this stage of the analysis.

**(f) Positive obligation:**

Not discussed. The Court's reasoning is framed entirely in terms of a negative obligation (the State must not interfere unlawfully), and no positive obligation is spelled out.

**Summary:** The Court finds a violation of Article 11 on the basis that the interference (the applicants' arrest) was not "prescribed by law" — the protests were conducted in compliance with applicable assembly laws, were peaceful, and did not disturb public order. The Court does not proceed to examine legitimate aim or necessity. The peaceful nature of the assembly is explicitly confirmed. Interference is explicitly confirmed. The "prescribed by law" requirement is explicitly addressed and found not to be satisfied, without separately analyzing its sub-elements.
