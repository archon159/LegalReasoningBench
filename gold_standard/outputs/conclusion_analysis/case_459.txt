**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final paragraph of section (iv) Conclusion: "In view of the foregoing, the Court concludes that the suppression of the perceived march, and the applicants' arrest and conviction for an administrative offence were not justified by a pressing social need and therefore not necessary in a democratic society. There has accordingly been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention as regards both applicants."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses the existence of an assembly. It notes: "It is undisputed that on 5 December 2011 the applicants took part in an authorised and peaceful public demonstration at Chistyye Prudy." The Court also discusses the subsequent walk as a "perceived march" or spontaneous march. The assembly is treated as established throughout the judgment. This is explicit.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court explicitly notes that the applicants participated in the authorised demonstration and subsequently walked with a group of people. The applicants' participation is treated as undisputed: "It is undisputed that on 5 December 2011 the applicants took part in an authorised and peaceful public demonstration at Chistyye Prudy." This is explicit.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assembly. In section (i) on the interception of the march: "the march, or the perceived march (see paragraph 56 above), had only lasted for fifteen minutes, was peaceful, and the number of participants – one hundred at most – could not have been difficult for the riot police to protect and contain." Also in the conclusion: "the police and the courts expressly acknowledged that, ultimately, the applicants had been punished for holding a spontaneous peaceful demonstration and chanting anti-government slogans, acts protected by Articles 10 and 11 of the Convention." This is explicit.

**(b) Interference**
The Court explicitly discusses and confirms interference. In section (b): "The Court considers that under any interpretation there was a clear and acknowledged link between the exercise of the freedom of peaceful assembly by the applicants and the measures taken against them. Accordingly, their arrest, detention and the ensuing administrative charges constituted an interference with their right guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention." The Government also accepted that the measures constituted an interference: "The Government accepted that the applicants' arrest and their conviction for an administrative offence had constituted an interference with their freedom of expression and their freedom of assembly." This is explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
The Court does not fully resolve the "prescribed by law" question. It notes: "the Court cannot decide on the basis of the evidence at its disposal whether the authorities acted lawfully. In any event, it considers that in this case the issue of compliance with the law is indissociable from the question whether the interference was 'necessary in a democratic society'. It will therefore examine this issue below." The Court then proceeds to examine necessity without separately resolving the lawfulness question. The sub-elements (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are not individually discussed.

**i) Foreseeable:** Not explicitly discussed. The Court declines to resolve the lawfulness question separately. Not discussed as a standalone sub-element.

**ii) Accessible:** Not discussed.

**iii) Safeguards:** Not discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
The Court explicitly accepts a legitimate aim: "Turning to the existence of a legitimate aim, the Court will accept that the applicants' arrest and their conviction for an administrative offence pursued the legitimate aim of maintaining public order, as the Government claimed." The Government had also argued public safety: "the Government relied on the need to maintain public order and ensure road safety as a justification for the dispersal of the march." The Court accepts "maintaining public order" which maps to "prevention of disorder" and arguably "public safety." This is explicit.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
The Court explicitly and extensively discusses necessity. It finds the measures were not necessary:

- On interception: "the Government have failed to demonstrate that there existed a 'pressing social need' to interrupt their spontaneous march. In view of the above, the Court considers that in the instant case the police's forceful intervention was disproportionate and was not necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

- On arrest: "the Court...concludes that there existed no 'pressing social need' to arrest the applicants and to escort them to the police station. Those measures therefore fell short of being proportionate and necessary for the prevention of disorder within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11 of the Convention."

- On conviction: "The Court considers that the sanction imposed on the applicants was unwarranted by the circumstances of the case and disproportionate within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention."

- Overall conclusion: "the Court concludes that the suppression of the perceived march, and the applicants' arrest and conviction for an administrative offence were not justified by a pressing social need and therefore not necessary in a democratic society."

This is explicit, and the conclusion is that necessity was NOT satisfied (i.e., the interference was not necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court does not discuss any positive obligation on the part of the State. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (interference by State authorities). Not discussed.
