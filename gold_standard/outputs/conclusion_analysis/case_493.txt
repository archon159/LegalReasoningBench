**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The final conclusion is stated in the last two paragraphs of the necessity assessment:

'Having regard to the above considerations, the Court finds that the basis for the ban on the planned peaceful assemblies was neither relevant nor sufficient to meet any pressing social need. The ban has therefore not been shown to have been necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued. Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Analysis of canonical elements**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the planned demonstrations constitute an 'assembly' within the meaning of Article 11. However, this is implied throughout the judgment — the Court proceeds on the basis that the planned demonstrations are assemblies protected by Article 11. The admissibility section notes that 'freedom of assembly also protects the abstract possibility of holding an undisturbed peaceful assembly,' implying that the planned events qualify as assemblies.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
For Mr Patyi, the Court explicitly confirms his standing: 'Mr Patyi was the organiser of the demonstrations planned and that he signed all the documents submitted to the competent authorities. It follows that the latter's refusal to authorise these demonstrations undoubtedly concerned, in a personal and direct manner, his Convention rights.' This implies his participation/organisation is established. For the other 47 applicants, the Court explicitly finds that their intention to participate is not supported by evidence and rejects their complaints ratione personae.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court implies the assembly was peaceful. In the necessity section, it states: 'the Court notes that there is no evidence in the case file to suggest that the demonstrations would have been violent or would have represented a danger to public order.' This is an implied finding that the assembly was peaceful, though not framed as a formal scope analysis.

**(b) Interference**
Explicitly discussed: 'The Government did not dispute that Mr Patyi could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the ban on the demonstrations had interfered with the exercise of his rights under that provision. The Court sees no reason to hold otherwise.' The Court confirms interference explicitly.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
Explicitly discussed: 'There was no dispute between the parties that the restriction imposed on Mr Patyi's freedom of peaceful assembly was based on sections 6, 8 and 14 of the Assembly Act, the wording of which is clear (see paragraph 21 above). Therefore, the requirement of lawfulness was satisfied.'

The Court's analysis is brief and does not separately address foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards as distinct sub-elements. The reference to the wording being 'clear' implies foreseeability is satisfied. Accessibility and safeguards are not separately discussed — the overall conclusion that 'the requirement of lawfulness was satisfied' is stated without breaking down these sub-elements.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
Explicitly discussed: 'the Court is satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others.' The Government had submitted that restrictions served 'to protect the rights of others, for example the right to freedom of movement or the orderly circulation of traffic' and also referenced preventing disorder.

Aims found: prevention of disorder and protection of the rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court applies the standard test: 'The expression "necessary in a democratic society" implies that the interference corresponds to a "pressing social need" and, in particular, that it is proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued.' The Court finds that the authorities 'mechanically relying on the same reasons and not taking into account Mr Patyi's factual clarifications, failed to strike a fair balance between the rights of those wishing to exercise their freedom of assembly and those others whose freedom of movement may have been frustrated temporarily, if at all.' The Court concludes necessity is NOT satisfied: 'the basis for the ban on the planned peaceful assemblies was neither relevant nor sufficient to meet any pressing social need.'

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Government raised positive obligations in the context of legitimate aim: 'freedom of peaceful assembly could not be reduced to a mere duty on the part of the State not to interfere. On certain occasions, positive measures had to be taken in order to ensure that an assembly was peaceful.' However, the Court does not explicitly adopt or develop a positive obligation analysis in its own assessment. The Court's reasoning focuses on the negative obligation (unjustified interference). The mention of positive obligations is in the Government's submissions, not in the Court's own legal analysis. Therefore, positive obligations are not discussed by the Court itself as a distinct element of its reasoning.
