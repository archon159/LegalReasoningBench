**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court explicitly finds a violation of Article 11 in the final paragraph of the merits section: "In these circumstances the Court finds a violation of Article 11 of the Convention in both cases complained of by the applicant."

This conclusion is reached after the Court's analysis of both the first case (demonstration of 17 April 2011) and the second case (demonstration of 26 January 2013).

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

*i) Assembly exists:* The Court implicitly accepts that assemblies (demonstrations) took place. In the first case it refers to "the demonstration of 17 April 2011" and in the second to "the demonstration of 26 January 2013." The existence of assemblies is not explicitly debated but is treated as given throughout the reasoning. This is implied.

*ii) Participation:* The Court explicitly refers to the applicant's participation: "her arrest and conviction following her participation in the demonstration of 17 April 2011" and "her arrest and conviction following her participation in the demonstration of 26 January 2013." Participation is thus explicitly acknowledged, though not debated as a contested issue.

*iii) Peaceful:* The Court explicitly addresses the peaceful nature of the assemblies. In the first case, it notes (by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov) "the lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration, which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner." In the second case, it refers to "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention." The peaceful character is explicitly discussed and confirmed.

**(b) Interference**

The Court implicitly treats the dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions as constituting interference with her freedom of assembly. It states: "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging her from participating in political rallies." While the Court does not use the word "interference" in a formal doctrinal step, the entire analysis presupposes that the State's actions constituted an interference. This is implied.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses the "prescribed by law" requirement, particularly in the context of the first case. It notes (by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov) "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed." The applicant also argued that the domestic legislation did not comply with principles of foreseeability and precision.

However, the Court's treatment is nuanced: for the first case, it raises concerns about foreseeability and safeguards but does not make a definitive finding that the law fails the "prescribed by law" test (it proceeds to find a violation on necessity grounds). For the second case, the Court explicitly states it is "ready to accept that the dispersal of the demonstration of 26 January 2013 was lawful," which implies it accepts the "prescribed by law" requirement is satisfied for that case.

*i) Foreseeable:* Explicitly discussed. The Court notes "serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies" (first case, by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov). The applicant argued the law lacked foreseeability. The Government argued it was precise and foreseeable. The Court raises concerns but does not make a clean finding of "not foreseeable" — it proceeds to find a violation on other grounds. Conclusion: concerns raised but not definitively resolved (null for a clean yes/no, but discussed explicitly).

*ii) Accessible:* Not explicitly discussed. The Court does not separately address accessibility of the law.

*iii) Safeguards against abuse:* Explicitly discussed. The Court notes concerns about "the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed" and "doubts about the credibility of the formal ground... invoked by the authorities for arresting and convicting the participant." This addresses the adequacy of safeguards against abuse. Again, the Court raises concerns without making a definitive finding on this sub-element alone.

Overall on "prescribed by law": The Court does not make a clean finding that the interference was NOT prescribed by law. For the second case it accepts lawfulness. For the first case it raises concerns but moves on to necessity. The overall prescribed_by_law conclusion is thus ambiguous — the Court does not find a violation on this ground specifically, but raises concerns. I will treat the overall conclusion as "null" (unresolved/not definitively decided) for the first case, and "yes" for the second, but since the schema requires a single answer, I will treat it as "null" given the mixed picture, with explicit discussion.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Government argued that the dispersal was necessary "in the interests of national security, for protection of the rights and freedoms of others, and the prevention of disorder or crime." The Court does not explicitly accept or reject these aims — it proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. The legitimate aim is thus mentioned (through the Government's submissions) but the Court does not make an explicit finding accepting a legitimate aim. Given the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds, it implicitly assumes (without deciding) that a legitimate aim could be present. I will treat this as implied with the aims mentioned being national security, prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, and rights of others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly discussed. For the first case: the Court finds "the lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration" and "the lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11." For the second case: "the authorities in the present case have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration" and "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11." The Court also notes the chilling effect: "The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly." The necessity requirement was NOT met — the Court finds the interference was not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the State in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (unjustified interference). Positive obligations are not discussed.
