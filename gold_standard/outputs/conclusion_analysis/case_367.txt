**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that the scope conditions are satisfied — there must have been a (peaceful) assembly in which the applicants participated for Article 11 to be engaged at all. This is further supported by the introductory paragraph: "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." The scope elements are therefore implied rather than explicitly discussed.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the text refers to "public assemblies" and "these assemblies" without questioning their existence.
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the applicants are described as "participants of public assemblies."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court proceeds to analyze Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies were violent or outside the protection of Article 11.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly acknowledges interference: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The introductory paragraph also describes the measures: "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence." The existence of interference is explicitly confirmed, though the Court does not dwell on it as a contested issue.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. These sub-elements are not discussed at all. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, which is consistent with a repetitive-case procedure where the Court relies on prior leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and does not re-examine every element. Since the Court finds a violation at the necessity stage, it does not need to (and does not) address prescribed by law.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. No specific aim is identified or analyzed. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is the basis for the violation finding. The Court reaches this conclusion by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) and finds "no fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion."

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. The case is framed entirely in terms of negative obligations (unjustified interference). This element is not discussed.
