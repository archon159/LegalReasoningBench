**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that Article 11 has been violated. The final paragraph of the merits section states: "The above leads the Court to conclude that, with several exceptions, the authorities persisted with the practice of imposing bans on Ilinden's rallies, and that those bans were not necessary in a democratic society (see Stankov and the United Macedonian Organisation Ilinden, § 109, and United Macedonian Organisation Ilinden and Ivanov, § 114, both cited above). There has therefore been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the gatherings constitute "assemblies" within the meaning of Article 11. However, this is clearly implied throughout the judgment. The Court refers throughout to "rallies" planned by Ilinden, treats them as falling under Article 11 without question, and proceeds directly to interference analysis. The existence of assemblies is implied by the entire structure of the analysis.

**(ii) Participation:** The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the applicants participated or attempted to participate. However, it is implied: the applicants are described as organisers and participants in the rallies, and the Court discusses measures taken against them personally (arrests, fines, police orders directed at members and supporters). The participation element is implied.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly:** The Court does not explicitly address whether the assemblies were peaceful. However, it is implied: the Court refers throughout to the applicants' "right to freedom of peaceful assembly," and notably states that even if there was a risk of clashes with counter-demonstrators, "it was the task of the police to stand between the two groups and to ensure public order." This implies the assemblies themselves were peaceful. The peaceful character is implied.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly discusses interference. It states: "Those measures were clearly aimed at hindering or even altogether preventing the events planned by Ilinden. While in many cases not actually impeding the holding of those events, they undoubtedly had a chilling effect on the individuals concerned and on the other participants in the rallies and thus amounted, in themselves, to interferences with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly."

The Court also elaborates on the concept: "The Court observes in that connection that an interference does not need to amount to an outright ban, legal or de facto, but can consist in various other measures taken by the authorities. The term 'restrictions' in Article 11 § 2 must be interpreted as including both measures taken before or during an assembly, and those, such as punitive measures, taken after that."

Conclusion: interference found — yes, explicitly discussed.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly discusses this element, raising "certain misgivings" but ultimately declining to decide it: "The Court has certain misgivings as to whether the interferences were 'prescribed by law', for several reasons. First, no legal basis has been cited for the arrests and seizures made by the police during certain rallies... Secondly, on a number of occasions the Mayor of Blagoevgrad relied on the fact that Ilinden was not registered to ban its rallies... even though this was not an express requirement of the law... Thirdly... the 1990 Meetings and Marches Act envisages no role for regional governors in the policing of rallies... Fourthly, section 62(1) of the 1997 Ministry of Internal Affairs Act, which served as a basis for the orders issued by the police, was formulated very generally and gave no indication of the circumstances in which the police could use the power conferred on them... Lastly, the fines imposed by the mayor of Blagoevgrad were later annulled by the courts."

However: "the Court does not consider it necessary to determine whether the interferences were 'prescribed by law' or pursued a legitimate aim, as it finds... that they cannot be regarded as being 'necessary in a democratic society'."

So the overall prescribed-by-law question is raised but left unresolved (null conclusion).

**(i) Foreseeable:** The Court implicitly addresses foreseeability when it notes that section 62(1) of the 1997 Ministry of Internal Affairs Act "was formulated very generally and gave no indication of the circumstances in which the police could use the power conferred on them" — this is a foreseeability concern. This is explicitly discussed but left unresolved (the Court expresses misgivings but does not reach a final conclusion).

**(ii) Accessible:** The Court does not explicitly discuss accessibility of the legal provisions. Not discussed.

**(iii) Safeguards against abuse:** The Court's concern about the very general formulation of the police powers provision ("gave no indication of the circumstances in which the police could use the power conferred on them") also touches on the absence of safeguards. This is implied but not separately articulated as a safeguards analysis. Given the Court's explicit reference to the lack of guidance on when police powers could be used, this can be treated as implied.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court explicitly states it does not consider it necessary to determine whether the interferences "pursued a legitimate aim": "the Court does not consider it necessary to determine whether the interferences were 'prescribed by law' or pursued a legitimate aim, as it finds... that they cannot be regarded as being 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The legitimate aim element is explicitly mentioned but left unresolved. No specific aims are identified or confirmed. Conclusion: null; aims_found: [].

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central element explicitly and extensively discussed. The Court finds the interferences were not necessary in a democratic society:

"On that point, the Court starts by noting that in three consecutive judgments it has found interferences almost identical to those in the present case not to be necessary in a democratic society and thus to be in breach of Article 11."

The Court notes that the authorities relied on the same grounds already found inadequate: "The instances in which the authorities in effect banned the rallies which Ilinden wished to hold during the period 2004-09 present no material difference. The mayors of Blagoevgrad and Petrich relied on essentially the same grounds as those given to ban such rallies previously: the organisation's unregistered status, conjectural threats to public order, the holding of events of other organisations and/or the authorities at the same time and place, and lastly, Ilinden's controversial statements on issues perceived as sensitive."

On restrictions without reasons: "on those occasions when the mayors did not ban the rallies, but only imposed restrictions as to their time, duration, place and manner, they either did not give any reasons, or briefly referred to the holding of other events at the same time and place without specifying why the simultaneous holding of two events would be impossible."

On counter-demonstrators: "Even if there was a risk of clashes between participants in Ilinden's rallies and counter-demonstrators, it was the task of the police to stand between the two groups and to ensure public order... That reason for interfering with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly could not therefore be considered as relevant and sufficient."

Conclusion: necessity not satisfied — no (i.e., the interferences were NOT necessary), explicitly discussed.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Government raised positive obligations in its submissions: "The Government submitted that all rallies planned by Ilinden had in fact taken place... The authorities had complied with their positive obligations under Article 11 and had ensured that the events proceeded smoothly."

The Court implicitly addresses positive obligations when it discusses the duty of police to protect assemblies from counter-demonstrators: "Even if there was a risk of clashes between participants in Ilinden's rallies and counter-demonstrators, it was the task of the police to stand between the two groups and to ensure public order, as was shown in fact to be possible."

Also, in the admissibility section, the Court discusses the inadequacy of compensation-only remedies and states: "If States were able to confine their response to such incidents to the mere payment of compensation, without putting in place effective procedures ensuring the possibility of staging such rallies, it would be possible in some cases for the authorities arbitrarily to deprive groups of individuals and organisations of their right to freedom of peaceful assembly."

The Court implicitly rejects the Government's positive obligation argument by finding a violation. The positive obligation dimension is discussed but primarily in the context of rejecting the Government's framing — the Court finds the State violated Article 11 through active interference rather than through failure to fulfil positive obligations. The positive obligation is implied/discussed but the conclusion is that the government did not comply with its obligations (conclusion: no).
