**Question 1: Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final paragraph of the reasoning section:

"These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

Additionally, the Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the 'quality of law' requirements established by the Convention or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**Question 2: Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The scope is not explicitly discussed, but it is implied. The Court refers to "the applicants complained principally of the prohibition on holding peaceful assemblies or restrictions on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events" and speaks of "the applicants' freedom of assembly" being interfered with. This implies that there was an assembly, that the applicants participated (or tried to), and that the assembly was peaceful. The word "peaceful" is used explicitly in the complaint description: "prohibition on holding peaceful assemblies."

- *Assembly exists*: Implied by the reference to "peaceful assemblies" and "public events" in the complaint, and by the Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly."
- *Participation*: Implied — the applicants are described as complaining about prohibitions on holding assemblies, implying they sought to participate.
- *Peaceful*: Implied (with a hint of explicit mention in the complaint framing): "prohibition on holding peaceful assemblies" — the Court does not dispute this characterization, and the leading case reference confirms the peaceful nature is assumed.

**(b) Interference**

The interference is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the 'quality of law' requirements established by the Convention or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court thus explicitly acknowledges that there were interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The "prescribed by law" / "quality of law" requirement is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the 'quality of law' requirements established by the Convention or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit finding that the quality of law requirements were not met (at least in some of the cases). However, the Court does not separately discuss the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards — it uses the umbrella phrase "quality of law requirements" without breaking them down. The conclusion on prescribed_by_law is "no" (the requirements were not met), but the sub-elements are not individually discussed. They are implied by the overall "quality of law" finding.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The legitimate aim is not discussed. The Court does not identify any specific legitimate aim pursued by the interference. Given the disjunctive structure of the Court's conclusion ("either... quality of law... or... necessary in a democratic society"), it is possible that in some cases the Court found the interference not prescribed by law (making legitimate aim analysis unnecessary) and in others found it not necessary (which would require a legitimate aim to have been found, but the Court does not spell this out). The legitimate aim element is therefore not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Necessity is explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the 'quality of law' requirements established by the Convention or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'." This is an explicit (if brief) finding that at least some of the interferences were not necessary in a democratic society.

**(f) Positive obligation**

Positive obligations are not discussed in this reasoning section. The Court focuses entirely on negative obligations (i.e., unjustified interferences with the right). There is no mention of any positive obligation on the part of the government.
