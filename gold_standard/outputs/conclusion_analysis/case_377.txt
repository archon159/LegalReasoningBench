**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The conclusion is stated in the final two paragraphs of the merits section:

'Having regard to the above considerations, the Court finds that the basis for the ban on the planned peaceful assembly was, if at all relevant, not sufficient to meet any pressing social need. The ban has therefore not been shown to have been necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued.'

'Accordingly, there has been a violation of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**2. Canonical elements of Article 11 doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not engage in a detailed, structured discussion of scope sub-elements, but it is implied throughout the judgment that there was an assembly (a planned demonstration), that the applicant sought to participate (he was the organiser), and that it was peaceful. The Court notes: 'there is no evidence in the case file to suggest that the demonstration would have been violent or represented a danger to public order.' This makes the peaceful nature of the assembly explicit. The existence of the assembly and the applicant's participation are implied by the entire framing of the case.

- **i) Assembly exists:** The Court refers throughout to 'the demonstration' and 'the planned peaceful assembly.' This is implied rather than explicitly analysed as a doctrinal sub-element. The Court states: 'the applicant planned to organise a demonstration with an anticipated 200 participants.' Conclusion: yes, implied.

- **ii) Applicant's participation:** The applicant is the organiser of the demonstration. This is implied throughout. Conclusion: yes, implied.

- **iii) Peaceful assembly:** Explicitly discussed: 'the Court notes that there is no evidence in the case file to suggest that the demonstration would have been violent or represented a danger to public order.' And the Court refers to 'the ban on the planned peaceful assembly.' Conclusion: yes, explicit.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly addresses interference: 'The Court notes that the Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the ban on the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of his rights under that provision. The Court sees no reason to hold otherwise.' Conclusion: yes, explicit.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court explicitly addresses this: 'There was no dispute between the parties that the restriction imposed on the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly was based on the pertinent provisions of Act no. III of 1989 on the Right of Assembly; and, again, the Court sees no reason to hold otherwise. Therefore, the requirement of lawfulness was satisfied.'

The Court does not separately analyse the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards against abuse. It simply accepts that the lawfulness requirement is satisfied without breaking it down into sub-components.

- **i) Foreseeable:** Not separately discussed; the overall 'prescribed by law' conclusion is accepted without sub-element analysis. Conclusion: null, not_discussed (though implied yes by the overall finding).

- **ii) Accessible:** Not separately discussed. Conclusion: null, not_discussed.

- **iii) Safeguards:** Not separately discussed. Conclusion: null, not_discussed.

Overall prescribed by law: yes, explicit.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed: 'The Court is satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others.' The Government had argued the aim was 'securing the rights of others, that is, those of traffic users.' The Court accepts both 'preventing disorder' and 'protecting the rights of others.' Conclusion: yes, explicit. Aims found: prevention_of_disorder, rights_of_others.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central and most extensively discussed element. The Court applies the 'pressing social need' and proportionality test. Key passages:

'The Court finds that the Government have not demonstrated that the national authorities based their decisions on an acceptable assessment of the relevant facts.' The Court finds the traffic disruption argument unpersuasive given that Venyige Street is a dead end and the Government's speculation about blocking a major thoroughfare. The Court concludes: 'the authorities, when issuing the prohibition on the demonstration and relying on traffic considerations alone, failed to strike a fair balance between the rights of those wishing to exercise their freedom of assembly and those others whose freedom of movement may have been frustrated temporarily, if at all.' And: 'the basis for the ban on the planned peaceful assembly was, if at all relevant, not sufficient to meet any pressing social need. The ban has therefore not been shown to have been necessary in a democratic society in order to achieve the aims pursued.' Conclusion: no (the interference was not necessary), explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation of the state in this judgment. The case is framed entirely as a negative interference case. Conclusion: null, not_discussed.
