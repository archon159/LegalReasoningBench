**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. The relevant passage is in the final two paragraphs of the reasoning section:

"Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

And: "These complaints are therefore admissible and disclose a breach of Article 11 of the Convention."

These two sentences together confirm the violation finding.

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether there was an assembly, whether the applicants participated, or whether the assembly was peaceful. However, the Court's statement that "the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly" occurred implies that all three scope sub-elements are satisfied — there was an assembly, the applicants participated in it, and it was peaceful (otherwise Article 11 would not apply). The introductory paragraph also notes that the applicants were arrested and convicted "in view of their participation in public demonstrations," which implies the existence of an assembly and participation. The peaceful nature is implied by the Court's willingness to apply Article 11 without qualification.

- **scope_assembly_exists**: implied yes — the Court refers to "public demonstrations" and "freedom of assembly" without questioning whether an assembly existed.
- **scope_participation**: implied yes — the Court explicitly notes the applicants' "participation in public demonstrations" and "participation in the assembly."
- **scope_peaceful**: implied yes — the Court applies Article 11 without suggesting the assembly fell outside its protection due to violence.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly states: "the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The word "interference" is used explicitly, confirming that the Court found governmental interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly. This is explicit. The factual background (arrest and conviction for administrative offence) also supports this.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference was prescribed by law, nor does it address foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The Court jumps directly to the necessity analysis, relying on its leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). Since the Court finds a violation on necessity grounds without addressing prescribed by law, this element is not discussed. It is possible the Court implicitly assumed it was prescribed by law (since it proceeds to necessity), but this is not stated. Given the Court's approach of relying on prior case-law and finding the violation solely on necessity, prescribed by law and its sub-elements are not_discussed.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not explicitly discuss whether the interference served a legitimate aim. It proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. No specific aim is identified. This element is not discussed.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only substantive element explicitly discussed. The Court states: "the Court considers that in the instant case the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly was not 'necessary in a democratic society'." The Court finds that necessity is NOT satisfied, concluding there is a violation. This is explicit.

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not discuss any positive obligation. This element is not discussed.
