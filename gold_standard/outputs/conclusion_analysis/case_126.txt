**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

The Court concludes that there has been NO violation of Article 11 of the Convention. This is stated in the final paragraph of the reasoning section: "Consequently, there has been no violation of Article 11 of the Convention."

Additionally, the penultimate paragraph states: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2."

**2. Analysis of canonical elements:**

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court implicitly acknowledges an assembly existed by referring to "the applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating" and by proceeding to analyze the interference with the "applicant's right of assembly." The assembly is not explicitly questioned as a threshold matter, but it is implied throughout. The Government of Turkey/TRNC argued the demonstration was "outside the scope of Article 11" due to its violent character, which the Court does not fully accept as a scope exclusion — instead it proceeds to analyze the interference and justification. So the existence of an assembly is implied as "yes."

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court states: "The Court notes that the applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Georgios church in Nicosia." This implies the applicant participated in the demonstration. This is implied rather than explicitly discussed as a separate sub-element.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
This is explicitly discussed. The Government argued the demonstration was "outside the scope of Article 11" due to its violent character. The Court, however, does not exclude the assembly from the scope of Article 11 on grounds of violence. Instead, it proceeds to find an interference and then addresses the violence in the context of justification (necessity/legitimate aim). The Court notes: "the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order." The Court also states: "the intervention of the Turkish and/or Turkish-Cypriot forces was not due to the political nature of the demonstration but was provoked by its violent character." However, the Court does not explicitly exclude the assembly from Article 11 protection on grounds of lack of peacefulness — it treats the violent character as relevant to justification rather than scope. The Court implicitly treats the assembly as within scope (even if not fully peaceful) by proceeding to the interference and justification analysis. The peaceful sub-element is thus discussed explicitly (the violent character is addressed) but the conclusion is nuanced: the Court does not say the assembly falls outside scope; rather it uses the violence to justify the interference. For the purposes of this analysis, the Court implies the assembly was within scope (i.e., it does not exclude it), but the violent character is noted. I will mark scope_peaceful as "no" (not peaceful) but "implied" since the Court does not explicitly rule on this as a scope question — it uses the violence in the necessity analysis.

**(b) Interference**
Explicitly discussed: "Under these circumstances, the Court considers that there has been an interference with the applicant's right of assembly (see Protopapa, cited above, § 104)." Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**
Explicitly discussed: "This interference had a legal basis, namely sections 70 and 71 of the Cypriot Criminal Code (see paragraphs 29-30 above) and section 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law (see paragraph 33 above), and was thus 'prescribed by law' within the meaning of Article 11 § 2 of the Convention." Conclusion: yes.

The sub-elements (foreseeable, accessible, safeguards) are NOT individually discussed. The Court simply states the interference had a legal basis and was "prescribed by law" without breaking down the sub-requirements. These sub-elements are not_discussed individually.

**(d) Legitimate aim**
Explicitly discussed: "The Government submitted that the interference pursued legitimate aims, including the protection of national security and/or public safety and the prevention of disorder and crime." The Court then proceeds to analyze the violent character of the demonstration and finds the interference justified, implicitly accepting these aims. The Court states: "interferences with the right guaranteed by Article 11 of the Convention are in principle justified for the prevention of disorder or crime and for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others where, as in the instant case, demonstrators engage in acts of violence." The Court also notes: "There remain the questions whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society" — and then proceeds to address them together. The Court implicitly accepts the legitimate aims of national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, and prevention of crime. Conclusion: yes. Aims found: national_security, public_safety, prevention_of_disorder, prevention_of_crime.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**
Explicitly discussed. The Court states: "There remain the questions whether the interference pursued a legitimate aim and was necessary in a democratic society." It then analyzes the violent character of the demonstration, the violation of UN buffer zone, the wide margin of appreciation, and concludes: "the Court holds that the interference with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly was not, in the light of all the circumstances of the case, disproportionate for the purposes of Article 11 § 2." Conclusion: yes (the interference was necessary/proportionate, i.e., the government's action was justified).

**(f) Positive obligation**
The Court mentions positive obligations in passing: "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens." However, this is mentioned in the context of the general principles, not as a finding of a positive obligation violation against the respondent. The Court does not find a positive obligation violation. This is discussed but only as a general principle reference, not as a specific finding. I will mark it as "implied" with conclusion "no" (no positive obligation violation found) — actually, the Court mentions it as a general principle but does not apply it as a specific claim. The applicant's complaint is about interference (negative obligation), not failure to protect. I'll mark positive_obligation as discussed="implied" and conclusion="no" since the Court references the duty but does not find it violated and does not apply it as the main framework.
