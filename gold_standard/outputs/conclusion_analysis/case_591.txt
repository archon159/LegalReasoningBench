**1. Violation of Article 11 ECHR**

Yes, the Court concludes that Article 11 ECHR has been violated. This is stated in the final paragraphs of the necessity assessment:

- "That approach, in circumstances where there was no real foreseeable risk of violent action or of incitement to violence or any other form of rejection of democratic principles was in the Court's view not justified under paragraph 2 of Article 11 of the Convention."
- "In sum, the Court finds that the authorities overstepped their margin of appreciation and that the measures banning the applicants from holding commemorative meetings were not necessary in a democratic society, within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention. There has therefore been a violation of that provision."

**2. Canonical Elements of Article 11 Doctrine**

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court explicitly discusses this in the Applicability section. The case concerns planned commemorative meetings/demonstrations organised by the applicant association. The existence of assemblies is treated as given — the Court's applicability analysis focuses on whether those assemblies were peaceful, implying the existence of assemblies is not in dispute. This is explicit by implication from the applicability discussion.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The applicants are the organisers of the meetings. The Court notes: "on all occasions under examination the authorities prohibited the meetings planned by both applicants." The applicants' participation/organisation is treated as established and not disputed. This is implied.

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
This is explicitly discussed under "B. The Court's assessment > 1. Applicability": "The Government expressed doubts as to the peaceful character of the applicant association's meetings and on that basis disputed the applicability of Article 11 of the Convention." The Court states: "The Court reiterates that Article 11 of the Convention only protects the right to 'peaceful assembly'. That notion – according to the Commission's case-law – does not cover a demonstration where the organisers and participants have violent intentions." The Court then concludes: "In the present case, having carefully studied all the material before it, the Court does not find that those involved in the organisation of the prohibited meetings had violent intentions... Article 11 is thus applicable." Conclusion: yes (peaceful).

**(b) Interference**

Explicitly discussed under "B. The Court's assessment > 2. Whether there has been an interference": "The Court notes that on all occasions under examination the authorities prohibited the meetings planned by both applicants. That was, indeed, a practice that had been invariably followed ever since 1992." And: "On the basis of the above, the Court considers that there has undoubtedly been an interference with both applicants' freedom of assembly, within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention." Conclusion: yes.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Explicitly discussed under "B. The Court's assessment > 3. Whether the interference was 'prescribed by law'". The Court notes problems with the reasons given (fluctuating, reliance on lack of registration which was not a valid ground), but ultimately accepts: "the authorities referred to an alleged danger to public order which in accordance with domestic law was among the grounds justifying interference with the right to peaceful assembly" and "the prohibitions complained of were imposed by decisions of the competent mayors and courts in accordance with the procedure prescribed by the Meetings and Marches Act." Conclusion: "the Court accepts that the interference with the applicants' freedom of assembly may be regarded as being 'prescribed by law'." Conclusion: yes.

**i) Foreseeable?**
The Court does not separately and explicitly address foreseeability as a sub-element. It notes that the reasons given were not always clear ("fluctuated and were not elaborate") and that reliance on lack of registration "could not in itself, under the applicable law, serve as ground for a ban on a meeting." However, the Court ultimately accepts the prescribed-by-law requirement is met without separately breaking down foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards. Foreseeability is implied as satisfied given the overall conclusion. Conclusion: yes (implied).

**ii) Accessible?**
Not separately discussed. Implied as satisfied given the overall prescribed-by-law conclusion. Conclusion: yes (implied).

**iii) Sufficient safeguards against abuse?**
Not separately discussed. Implied as satisfied given the overall prescribed-by-law conclusion. Conclusion: yes (implied).

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Explicitly discussed under "B. The Court's assessment > 4. Legitimate aim". The Government cited: protection of national security and territorial integrity, protection of the rights and freedoms of others, guaranteeing public order in the local community, and prevention of disorder and crime. The Court states: "Having regard to all the material in the case the Court accepts that the interference was intended to safeguard one or more of the interests cited by the Government." The Court does not specify which particular aim(s) it accepts, but accepts that at least one was present. Conclusion: yes. Aims found: national security, public safety, prevention of disorder, prevention of crime, rights of others (all cited by the Government and accepted by the Court without specification).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Extensively and explicitly discussed under "B. The Court's assessment > 5. 'Necessary in a democratic society'". The Court applies the general principles (pressing social need, proportionality, margin of appreciation) and examines the specific grounds relied on (alleged possession of arms, alleged threat to public safety, separatist ideas, alleged propagation of violence, alleged 'conversion' of population, statements perceived as offensive). The Court concludes: "the Court finds that the authorities overstepped their margin of appreciation and that the measures banning the applicants from holding commemorative meetings were not necessary in a democratic society, within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention." Conclusion: no (not necessary — i.e., the interference was NOT justified as necessary).

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court does not explicitly discuss or spell out a positive obligation for the government in this case. The case is framed entirely as a negative obligation case (prohibition of meetings by authorities). There is a brief reference to Plattform "Ärzte für das Leben" v. Austria in the general principles section, but only in the context of the general protection afforded by Article 11, not to establish a positive obligation. No positive obligation finding is made. Conclusion: not discussed.
