LIST 2: Atomic facts strictly necessary to support each discussed element of law

Scope: F003 is strictly necessary. The Court's willingness to proceed with an Article 11 analysis — implying that an assembly existed, that the applicants participated, and that the assembly was within the scope of protection — rests entirely on the characterization in F003. Without F003, there is no factual basis for the scope finding.

Interference: F003 is strictly necessary. The specific governmental acts identified as interference (dispersal, arrest, conviction) are all embedded in the complaint characterization of F003. This is the only atomic fact that grounds the interference finding.

Prescribed by law: No atomic facts are strictly necessary, as this element is not discussed.

Legitimate aim: No atomic facts are strictly necessary, as this element is not discussed.

Necessity: F003 is strictly necessary. The necessity assessment, though thin, is directed at the complaint as characterized in F003 — the dispersal of assemblies and the arrest and conviction of participants. Without F003, there is no factual subject matter for the necessity analysis.

LIST 3: Irreducible factual core for the final conclusion

The Court's conclusion is that Article 11 has been violated because the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not necessary in a democratic society. The case turned on the existence of governmental acts (dispersal, arrest, conviction) directed at participants of public assemblies. The single atomic fact that is strictly necessary and sufficient to anchor this conclusion is F003. It establishes that the applicants were participants in public assemblies who suffered disproportionate measures — which is the factual predicate for the entire Article 11 analysis and the violation finding. F002 (organisers) is not strictly necessary because the reasoning text uses the language of participants, not organisers. F004 is irrelevant to the Article 11 conclusion. F001 is procedural and not substantive.
