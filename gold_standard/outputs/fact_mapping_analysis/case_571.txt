LIST 1: Factual grounding quotes mapped to atomic facts

--- SCOPE (implied) ---
Quote: "the applicant's punishment for organising and holding a peaceful assembly constituted an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly"
- F005: The applicant notified the Mayor of the intention to hold a peaceful assembly (picket) on 19 March 2009 — establishes that an assembly was planned and the applicant was the organiser.
- F015: The picket took place as planned on 19 March 2009 — establishes that the assembly actually occurred.
- F016: According to the applicant, the picket on 19 March 2009 was peaceful — establishes the peaceful character.
- F019: The police report noted there had been no violation of public order during the picket — corroborates the peaceful character.
- F034: The Prymorsky District Court found the applicant guilty of having violated the procedure for organising and holding meetings and demonstrations — confirms the applicant's participation as organiser.

--- INTERFERENCE ---
Quote: "the applicant's punishment for organising and holding a peaceful assembly constituted an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly"
- F034: The Prymorsky District Court found the applicant guilty of having violated the procedure for organising and holding meetings and demonstrations.
- F035: The Prymorsky District Court fined the applicant 170 UAH (approximately EUR 17).
- F050: The Odessa Regional Court of Appeal upheld the decision of the Prymorsky District Court.

--- PRESCRIBED BY LAW ---

Sub-element: Legal basis in domestic law (accessibility)
Quote: "The Court notes that the fine was imposed on the applicant under Article 185-1 of the CAO, which prescribed a penalty for violations of the procedure for organising and holding demonstrations. Therefore, the interference had a basis in domestic law. It has not been disputed that the Code was accessible and the Court does not have reason to doubt that."
- F062: Article 185-1 of the CAO provides that a breach of the procedure for organising and holding meetings, rallies, street marches and demonstrations shall be punishable by a reprimand or fine.
- F036: The Prymorsky District Court held that the applicant was in breach of Article 185-1 of the CAO because he had not complied with the ten-day notification requirement.

Sub-element: Foreseeability — inconsistency of domestic court practice
Quote: "there is no single view on the applicability of the 1988 Decree and the existence of a clear and foreseeable procedure for organising and holding peaceful demonstrations. The practice of the domestic courts also reveals inconsistencies in this sphere"
- F078: The Information Note of April 2012 of the Higher Administrative Court stated that judicial practice contained instances of cases being decided on the basis of the 1988 Decree and that this approach is incorrect.
- F081: The Babushkinsky District Court held that the procedures for exercising the right to freedom of assembly were not regulated by Ukrainian legislation.
- F083: The Kyiv Administrative Court restricted the right to hold a demonstration on account of failure to notify ten days in advance, referring to the 1988 Decree.
- F086: The Kyiv Administrative Court of Appeal quashed that judgment.
- F037: The Prymorsky District Court based its findings on the 1988 Decree.
- F050: The Odessa Regional Court of Appeal upheld the Prymorsky District Court's decision.

Sub-element: Foreseeability — insufficiency of constitutional provisions alone
Quote: "the Constitution of Ukraine provides for some general rules as to the possible restrictions on the freedom of assembly, but those rules require further elaboration in the domestic law"
- F058: Article 39 of the Constitution provides that citizens have the right to assemble peacefully after notifying the authorities beforehand.
- F059: Article 39 of the Constitution provides that restrictions may be established by a court in accordance with the law.
- F073: The Constitutional Court's decision of 19 April 2001 noted that current legislation does not provide for a specific time-limit for notification.
- F076: The Constitutional Court held that specifying exact deadlines is a matter for legislative regulation.

Sub-element: Foreseeability — conflict between 1988 Decree and Ukrainian Constitution
Quote: "the only existing document establishing such a procedure is the 1988 Decree, whose provisions are not generally accepted as the valid procedure for holding demonstrations and which provides, as is confirmed in the practice of the domestic courts, for a different procedure from the one outlined in the Constitution. Indeed, whilst the Ukrainian Constitution requires advance notification to the authorities of an intention to hold a demonstration and stipulates that any restriction thereon can be imposed only by a court, the 1988 Decree, drafted in accordance with the Constitution of the USSR of 1978, provides that persons wishing to hold a peaceful demonstration have to seek permission from the local administration which is also entitled to ban any such demonstration."
- F058: Article 39 of the Constitution requires advance notification and court-imposed restrictions only.
- F059: Article 39 of the Constitution provides that restrictions may be established by a court in accordance with the law.
- F066: The 1988 Decree lays down the procedure for seeking and granting permission to organise and hold assemblies.
- F067: The 1988 Decree provides that an application shall be submitted to the executive committee of the appropriate local Soviet.
- F068: The 1988 Decree provides that an application shall be submitted no later than ten days before the planned date.
- F070: The 1988 Decree provides that the executive committee shall ban a meeting if the goal is contrary to the Constitution of the USSR or poses a threat to public order.
- F079: The Information Note of April 2012 stated that the 1988 Decree establishes a procedure for authorising (registering) peaceful assembly and empowers authorities to ban such events, whereas the Constitution provides for notification only and empowers only courts to ban gatherings.
- F087: The Kyiv Administrative Court of Appeal noted that the 1988 Decree conflicted with the Constitution as it required permission-seeking and authorised executive authorities to ban events.

Sub-element: Foreseeability — original purpose of the 1988 Decree
Quote: "From the preamble of the Decree it is clear that it had been intended for a very different purpose, namely for only certain categories of individuals to be provided by the administration with facilities to express their views in favour of a particular ideology, this in itself being incompatible with the very essence of the freedom of assembly guaranteed by the Ukrainian Constitution and the Convention."
- F066: The 1988 Decree lays down the procedure for seeking and granting permission — its permission-based structure reflects its Soviet-era purpose.
- F070: The 1988 Decree provides that the executive committee shall ban a meeting if the goal is contrary to the Constitution of the USSR — reflects ideological gatekeeping.

Sub-element: Foreseeability — domestic court's characterisation of the Decree
Quote: "As found by a domestic court, demonstrations under the 1988 Decree were considered on the basis of their compatibility with 'non-existent constitutions of non-existent subjects'."
- F094: The Kyiv Administrative Court of Appeal stated that the 1988 Decree concerned the holding of demonstrations in a non-existent country (the USSR) and considered demonstrations on the basis of their compatibility with non-existent constitutions of non-existent subjects.

Sub-element: Foreseeability — legislative gap and its duration
Quote: "no law has yet been enacted by the Ukrainian Parliament regulating the procedure for holding peaceful demonstrations, although Articles 39 and 92 of the Constitution clearly require that such a procedure be established by law, that is, by an Act of the Ukrainian Parliament. Whilst the Court accepts that it may take some time for a country to establish its legislative framework during a transitional period, it cannot agree that a delay of more than twenty years is justifiable, especially when such a fundamental right as freedom of peaceful assembly is at stake."
- F058: Article 39 of the Constitution requires advance notification — implies a procedure must be established by law.
- F059: Article 39 of the Constitution provides that restrictions may be established by a court in accordance with the law.
- F060: Article 92(1) of the Constitution provides that human rights and freedoms are determined exclusively by the laws of Ukraine.
- F071: The Resolution of the Verkhovna Rada of 12 September 1991 provides for temporary application of Soviet legislation only in so far as it does not contravene the Constitution and legislation of Ukraine.
- F073: The Constitutional Court's decision of 19 April 2001 noted that current legislation does not provide for a specific time-limit for notification.
- F076: The Constitutional Court held that specifying exact deadlines is a matter for legislative regulation.
- F011: The Odessa City Council's letter of 17 March 2009 mentioned that no implementing law in respect of the right to peaceful assembly had yet been enacted in Ukraine.

LIST 3: Irreducible factual core for the final conclusion (Article 11 violated)

The case turned on the 'prescribed by law' requirement, specifically foreseeability. The Court found that the Ukrainian legal framework for peaceful assembly was not sufficiently clear and foreseeable because: (a) the only existing procedural instrument (the 1988 Decree) conflicted with the Constitution; (b) no parliamentary law had been enacted despite a constitutional requirement to do so; and (c) domestic court practice was inconsistent. The applicant was fined under Article 185-1 of the CAO for failing to give ten days' notice under the 1988 Decree. The irreducible facts are:

- F015: The picket took place — the assembly existed (necessary to trigger Article 11 at all).
- F035: The applicant was fined — the concrete interference.
- F034: The fine was imposed for violating the procedure for organising and holding meetings under Article 185-1 of the CAO — links the interference to the legal framework under scrutiny.
- F062: Article 185-1 of the CAO prescribes a penalty for breach of the procedure — the legal basis for the fine.
- F068: The 1988 Decree requires ten-day advance notification — the specific rule the applicant was found to have breached.
- F058: Article 39 of the Constitution requires advance notification and court-imposed restrictions only — establishes the constitutional standard that the 1988 Decree conflicts with.
- F060: Article 92(1) of the Constitution provides that human rights are determined exclusively by laws of Ukraine — establishes that the 1988 Decree (a Soviet decree, not a Ukrainian law) cannot satisfy the constitutional requirement.
- F070: The 1988 Decree empowers the executive committee to ban assemblies — the core conflict with the Constitution's court-only model.
- F071: The Verkhovna Rada Resolution provides for temporary application of Soviet legislation only where it does not contravene the Constitution — the conditionality that undermines the 1988 Decree's validity.
- F073: The Constitutional Court's 2001 decision noted that current legislation does not provide a specific time-limit — confirms the legislative gap.
- F076: The Constitutional Court held that specifying exact deadlines is a matter for legislative regulation — confirms that a parliamentary act was required but absent.
- F078: The Higher Administrative Court's Information Note stated that applying the 1988 Decree is incorrect — establishes the inconsistency of domestic practice that undermines foreseeability.
