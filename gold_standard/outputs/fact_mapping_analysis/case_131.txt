LIST 1: Factual grounding for each element as identified in the reasoning section

As established in the prior analysis, the Article 11 reasoning section contains no independently stated facts. The Court's entire reasoning is a cross-reference to its Article 10 analysis: "largely identical considerations arise under this provision as were examined above under Article 10 of the Convention" and "[f]or the same reasons, it also finds no failure to protect the applicants' freedom of assembly."

The only element explicitly discussed is the positive obligation ("no failure to protect the applicants' freedom of assembly"), and the only element implied is scope (the reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" implies an assembly existed and the applicants participated).

For the positive obligation element, the factual grounding — to the extent it can be reconstructed from the atomic facts — concerns: (a) the nature of the Galleries as a privately owned but publicly accessible space (F008, F011, F012, F013); (b) the actions of Postel's security guards in removing the applicants (F029, F030, F031); (c) the applicants' attempts to exercise expressive activity there (F026, F027, F028, F035, F036); (d) the refusal of access by the Galleries manager (F037, F038, F039, F040); (e) the alternative means available to the applicants (F043, F044, F045); (f) the fact that the applicants ultimately succeeded in submitting 3,200 representations (F047); and (g) the domestic legal framework governing private property rights (F062, F065, F066).

For the scope element (implied), the relevant atomic facts are those establishing that the applicants attempted to engage in collective expressive/campaigning activity in a public-facing space: F024, F025, F026, F027, F028, F035.

LIST 2: Atomic facts strictly necessary to support each element of the Court's reasoning

Scope (implied — assembly/participation): The Court's reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" requires at minimum that there was a collective activity and that the applicants participated. The strictly necessary facts are: F024 (formation of group to campaign), F026 (first applicant set up stands at the Galleries entrance), F028 (collecting signatures as collective activity), F035 (third applicant formally requested permission to set up a stand and canvass views).

Positive obligation (explicitly discussed — "no failure to protect"): The Court found no failure by the State to protect the applicants' freedom of assembly. This conclusion rests on the following strictly necessary facts: (1) the interference came from a private party, not the State directly (F008, F029, F037); (2) the applicants had alternative means of reaching the public (F043, F044, F047); (3) the domestic legal framework gave the private owner the right to exclude (F062, F065, F066); and (4) the applicants were able to achieve their communicative purpose to a meaningful degree (F047).

LIST 3: Irreducible factual core for the final conclusion (no violation of Article 11)

The case turned on whether the State had a positive obligation to ensure the applicants could access privately owned but publicly used space for expressive/assembly purposes. The Court found no violation. The irreducible facts for this conclusion are:

- F008: The Galleries is privately owned by Postel Properties Limited — this is the foundational fact explaining why there is no direct State interference and why the case is framed as a positive obligation question.
- F029: Security guards employed by Postel removed the applicants — this is the act that triggered the Article 11 issue.
- F043, F044: The applicants had alternative means of reaching the public (roadside stands, old town centre).
- F047: The applicants ultimately submitted 3,200 representations — demonstrating that their communicative purpose was not entirely frustrated.
- F062, F065, F066: The domestic legal framework gave the private owner an unfettered right to exclude — explaining why the State's non-intervention was lawful under domestic law and why there was no positive obligation to override it.
