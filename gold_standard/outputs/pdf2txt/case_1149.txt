DECISION

Application no. 35139/14
Temur Damyrovych YULDASHEV and Violeta Viktorivna 
YULDASHEVA against Ukraine and Russia
and 326 other applications
(see list appended)

The European Court of Human Rights (First Section), sitting on 5 May 
2020 as a Committee composed of:

Aleš Pejchal, President,
Tim Eicke,
Jovan Ilievski, judges,
and Renata Degener, Deputy Section Registrar,

Having regard to the above applications lodged on the various dates 
indicated in the appended table,

Having deliberated, decides as follows:

THE FACTS

1.  A list of the applicants is set out in the appendix. They were 
represented before the Court by Ms N.Y. Tselovalnichenko, a lawyer 
practising in Kyiv.

The circumstances of the case

1. Incidents giving rise to the applications

2.  The facts of the cases, as submitted by the applicants, may be 
summarised as follows.

3.  The applicants argued that from the beginning of April 2014 onwards 
armed separatist groups had started to seize official buildings and territory 
in the east of Ukraine and announced the creation of two so-called “People’s 
Republics”; namely the “Donetsk People’s Republic” and the “Lugansk 
People’s Republic”. In response, on 14 April 2014 the Ukrainian

Government had begun what they termed an “anti-terrorist operation”, 
involving the use of armed military force.

4.  Some of the applicants also argued that Crimea had been “occupied” 
and “annexed” by the Russian Federation in February and March 2014.

5.  The incidents which, according to the applicants, had taken place in 
the course of and subsequent to the developments in the preceding 
paragraphs and which formed the basis of their applications, can be 
summarised as follows:

-a number of houses belonging to some of the applicants in Eastern 
Ukraine had been damaged and, in some cases, completely destroyed during 
“shelling by pro-Russian militants or by Russian or Ukrainian armed 
forces”. As a result, some of the applicants had been forced to live in 
destitute conditions without running water, gas, electricity, heating, food 
supplies or medication. The constant shelling by heavy weapons had also 
endangered their lives. Some applicants had had to abandon their villages, 
towns and cities and fled to other parts of Ukraine. The applicants had also 
been unable to have access to Ukrainian courts to claim compensation for 
the damage caused to their properties because the jurisdictions of the courts 
in the areas in Eastern Ukraine which were not under the control of the 
Ukrainian Government had been transferred to the relevant courts in the 
neighbouring regions that were controlled by the Ukrainian Government;

-some applicants and their relatives had been injured during the shelling 
and some of their relatives had been killed;

-some applicants and their relatives had been arrested and detained 
unlawfully by members of armed separatist groups who also carried out a 
number of abductions, robberies, and perpetrated ill-treatment and killings;

-some of the applicants’ pension payments had been stopped by the 
Ukrainian Government;

-some applicants who lived in areas of Eastern Ukraine which were not 
controlled by the Ukrainian Government had been unable to vote during the 
Presidential and Parliamentary elections. They had also been afraid to speak 
the Ukrainian language and to express pro-Ukrainian views for fear of 
recriminations from the militants;

-there had existed no effective remedies within the meaning of Article 13 
of the Convention for their complaints summarised above. Moreover, since 
the beginning of April 2014 official buildings in some parts of Eastern 
Ukraine had been seized and the activities carried out therein suspended and 
it had therefore been impossible for the applicants to have their Convention 
rights guaranteed effectively;

-a number of applicants, who are Crimean Tatars, had been forced to flee 
Crimea;

-some of the applicants who were living in various parts of Ukraine had 
been unable to freely reside and move through the territory of Crimea; and, 
finally,

-the applicants in fifty-seven of the applications directed their 
applications against the United Kingdom and alleged that, as well as the 
Russian Federation and Ukraine, the United Kingdom had also been 
responsible for their grievances. In that connection they alleged that the 
United Kingdom, by signing the Memorandum on security assurances in 
connection with Ukraine’s accession to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation 
of Nuclear Weapons in Budapest on 5 December 1994, had become a 
guarantor of Ukrainian independence and security and had thus become 
duty-bound to take effective measures against the Russian Federation for 
“its military intervention in Ukraine”. As the United Kingdom had not taken 
any such measures, it bore partial responsibility for the above-summarised 
incidents.

2. The Court’s decision to bar Ms N. Tselovalnichenko

6.  On 4 December 2018, in its examination of a number of applications 
in which the applicants – like the applicants in the present applications – 
were represented by Ms Tselovalnichenko, the Court decided, under 
Rule 36 § 4 (b) of the Rules of Court, that in view of her fraudulent and 
abusive behaviour in a number of cases lodged with the Court 
Ms Tselovalnichenko should be permanently barred from representing or 
otherwise assisting applicants in both pending and future applications.

7.  In order to inform all applicants represented by her, notably those 
residing in areas that could not be reached by post, the Court issued a press 
release on 12 December 2018 (ECHR 433 (2018)) and invited the media to 
inform the public about its decision to bar Ms Tselovalnichenko (see 
Chepelenko and Others v. Ukraine (dec.), nos. 15117/17, 18635/17, 
18655/17, 251/18 and 3633/18, §§ 17-18, 28 January 2020).

3. Attempts made to contact the applicants in the present applications

8.  According to the information contained in the application forms 
submitted to the Court by the applicants in the present applications, the vast 
majority of them were living in the Donetsk and Luhansk regions of Eastern 
Ukraine at the time of the introduction of their applications. After issuing 
the above-mentioned press release on 12 December 2018, the Court sent 
letters to the applicants on 17 December 2018 with a view to notifying them 
that Ms Tselovalnichenko could no longer act as their representative. The 
letters also stated that it was open to the applicants to appoint new 
representatives to replace her at any time. Almost all of those letters were 
returned to the Court undelivered.

9.  On 30 September 2019 a second attempt was made to contact the 
applicants and letters were sent to them once more. In those letters the 
applicants were asked to inform the Court by 25 October 2019 whether they 
wished to maintain their applications and, if so, to provide information

about any important factual developments that might have taken place since 
their last correspondence to the Court. The applicants were warned that 
failure to reply to the Court’s letter by the above-mentioned deadline could 
lead the Court to conclude that they were no longer interested in pursuing 
their applications and to strike them out of its list of cases. Replies were 
received in a small number of cases only – in some of which the applicants 
informed the Court that they had appointed new legal representatives – and 
the Court will continue to examine those cases. The letters sent to the 
applicants in the present 327 applications were either returned to the Court 
undelivered or no replies were received from the applicants.

COMPLAINTS

10.  In respect of their allegations summarised in paragraph 5 above, the 
applicants relied on, inter alia, Articles 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13 and 14 of 
the Convention and Article 1 of Protocol No. 1.

THE LAW

11.  Having regard to the similar subject matter of the applications, the 
Court finds it appropriate to examine them jointly in a single decision.

12.  The Court notes that, at the time of the introduction of their 
applications, the vast majority of applicants were living in the territories of 
the Donetsk and Luhansk regions which were outside the control of the 
Ukrainian Government and in which the Ukrainian postal service Ukrposhta 
had suspended its operations because of frequent attacks on its vehicles and 
employees by armed groups (see Chepelenko and Others, cited above, §§ 21 
and 23; see also Tsezar and Others v. Ukraine, nos. 73590/14 and 6 others, 
§ 9, 13 February 2018). As detailed above, in addition to issuing the press 
release on 12 December 2018, the Court also made two attempts to establish 
direct contact with the applicants by sending them letters, but those attempts 
were unsuccessful.

13.  As the application forms provided by the applicants did not indicate 
any other contact details ̶ such as alternative postal addresses, e-mails, 
telephone numbers etc. ̶ the Court was also unable to contact the applicants 
by such means.

14.  Moreover, the Court has not received any correspondence directly 
from the applicants since the introduction of the applications. It must be 
stressed in this connection that Rule 47 § 7 of the Rules of Court requires 
applicants to keep the Court informed of any change of their addresses and 
of all circumstances relevant to their application. The Court considers that 
the fluid nature of the situation surrounding the applicants’ cases makes it 
all the more important for the Court to receive regular information and 
updates from the applicants.

5

15.  The Court also reiterates that, on account of the independence of the 
legal profession from the State, the conduct of the legal representation is 
essentially a matter between the applicants and their lawyer (see Chepelenko 
and Others, cited above, § 33 and the case cited therein). Therefore, 
following the Court’s decision to bar the applicants’ representative, it was 
for that representative to inform her clients of such a pertinent development 
in their cases.

16.  The Court is thus faced with a situation in which it is not being 
provided with any information by the applicants nor is it in a position to 
contact them, which makes it impossible for the Court to continue to 
examine these applications.

17.  The Court considers that, in these circumstances, the applicants may 
be regarded as no longer wishing to pursue their applications, within the 
meaning of Article 37 § 1 (a) of the Convention. Furthermore, in accordance 
with Article 37 § 1 in fine, the Court finds no special circumstances 
regarding respect for human rights as defined in the Convention and its 
Protocols which require the continued examination of the case.

18.  Finally, the Court reiterates that under Article 37 § 2 of the 
Convention it may decide to restore an application to its list of cases if it 
considers that the circumstances justify such a course.

19.  In view of the above, it is appropriate to strike the cases out of the 
list.

For these reasons, the Court, unanimously,

Decides to join the applications;

Decides to strike the application out of its list of cases.
