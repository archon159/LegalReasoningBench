This is an important question, and the honest answer is that **the reasoning section provided contains almost no factual grounding** for any of the doctrinal elements. The Court's reasoning is extremely compressed and operates almost entirely by reference to the leading case of *Lashmankin and Others v. Russia*. Let me go through each element:

---

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding offered is the following characterization of the complaints:

> "The applicants complained principally of the restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events."

This is the sole factual anchor from which the existence of assemblies and the applicants' participation can be inferred. The Court does not itself establish these facts; it merely records what the applicants complained about. The Court does not explicitly adopt or verify these facts as established. No facts are stated regarding the peaceful nature of the assemblies.

---

**(b) Interference (explicit)**

The only factual grounding is the same sentence quoted above:

> "The applicants complained principally of the restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events."

And the Court's own characterization:

> "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions..."

The Court uses the word "interferences" as an established fact, but provides no underlying factual detail about what specific acts or omissions by which authorities constituted those interferences.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law (explicit)**

The Court states:

> "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements"

This is the entirety of the factual and legal grounding offered. No specific legal provisions are identified, no analysis of their wording, accessibility, or safeguards is provided. The Court grounds its conclusion entirely by reference to *Lashmankin*:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion on the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This is a methodological statement, not a factual finding. **No specific facts are quoted or established** in support of the quality-of-law conclusion.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

The Court states the interferences were:

> "moreover not 'necessary in a democratic society'"

Again, **no facts are provided** to ground this conclusion. The Court offers no balancing analysis, no assessment of the weight of any legitimate aim, and no examination of the proportionality of the specific measures taken. The conclusion rests entirely on the reference to *Lashmankin*.

---

**Overall observation**

The reasoning section is a repetition/clone judgment. The Court explicitly signals this methodology:

> "In the leading case of Lashmankin and Others v. Russia, no. 57818/09 and 14 others, §§ 402-78, 7 February 2017, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion..."

The factual grounding for all doctrinal conclusions is located in *Lashmankin*, not in this judgment. This text, standing alone, does not provide sufficient factual grounding for any of the doctrinal elements it invokes.
