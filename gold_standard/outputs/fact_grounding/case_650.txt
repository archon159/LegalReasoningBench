I will go through each element that was found to be either explicitly discussed or implied, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**
The only factual grounding is the following phrase from the opening paragraph:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and participants of **public assemblies**, namely the **dispersal of these assemblies**"

The Court itself uses the word "assemblies" and refers to their dispersal, which constitutes the Court's own characterisation of the events.

**ii) Participation**
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers and participants** of public assemblies"

The Court itself describes the applicants as organisers and participants.

**iii) Peaceful character**
No factual grounding can be identified. The Court does not mention any facts relating to the peaceful or non-peaceful nature of the assemblies. This element is implied only by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis, not by any stated facts.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds the interference finding in the following concrete facts stated in the opening paragraph:
> "namely the **dispersal of these assemblies**, as well as their **arrest** followed by their **conviction for administrative offences**"

These three factual elements — dispersal, arrest, and conviction — are the factual basis for the interference finding.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

No factual grounding can be identified, as this element is neither discussed nor implied.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

No factual grounding can be identified, as this element is neither discussed nor implied.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court's necessity assessment is notably thin on specific facts. The only factual grounding offered is the same set of facts underlying the interference finding:
> "namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences"

Beyond this, the Court does not ground its necessity assessment in any additional specific facts about the circumstances of the assemblies or the reasons given by the government for the measures taken. Instead, the Court relies entirely on its prior case-law:
> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject..."

The reference to "all the material submitted" suggests the Court considered factual submissions, but it does not identify or adopt any specific facts from those submissions. The necessity conclusion is therefore grounded in the prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) rather than in independently stated facts.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

No factual grounding can be identified, as this element is not discussed.
