The Court's analysis is conducted at a very high level of abstraction, relying on references to prior case-law rather than on facts established in the present case. Let me go through each element:

---

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding available comes from the Court's own characterization in the opening paragraph:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This passage establishes:
- That there were **public assemblies**
- That the applicants were **organisers or participants**
- That the assemblies were **dispersed**
- That the applicants were **arrested and convicted of administrative offences**

However, it should be noted that this reads more as a summary of the applicants' complaints than as the Court's own verified factual findings. The Court does not explicitly state "we find as a fact that..." It simply describes what the applicants complained of. No further factual detail is provided.

As to whether the assemblies were **peaceful**, there is **no factual grounding whatsoever** in this reasoning section.

---

**(b) Interference (explicitly discussed)**

The factual grounding is the same passage quoted above:

> "namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

These are the only concrete acts identified as constituting the interference. No further facts are provided about the circumstances of the arrests, the nature of the dispersals, or the content of the convictions.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicitly discussed)**

This is the most striking gap. The Court's necessity finding is **not grounded in any specific facts from the present case**. Instead, the Court relies entirely on:

1. A reference to prior leading cases:
> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014, and Kasparov and Others v. 2. A statement that no new material was found:

3. A general reference to case-law:
> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject..."

The Court does **not** identify which specific facts of the present case make the interference disproportionate. It does not weigh any particular government action against any particular legitimate aim on the facts. The necessity conclusion is reached purely by analogy to prior cases, with no case-specific factual analysis.

---

**Overall observation**

This reasoning section appears to be a **repetitive case judgment** (sometimes called a "clone case" or "well-established case-law" judgment), where the Court dispenses with detailed factual analysis because the situation is considered legally identical or closely analogous to already-decided leading cases. The Court explicitly signals this approach:

> "the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion"

This means the factual record of the present case is effectively treated as a given, with the legal outcome determined by reference to the leading cases rather than by independent factual reasoning.
