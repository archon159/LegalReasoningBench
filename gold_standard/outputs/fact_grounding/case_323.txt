**Factual grounding for each element discussed or implied:**

---

**(a) Scope**

- **Assembly exists / Participation:** The only factual grounding in the text is the following phrase from the opening paragraph: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the Court's own characterisation of the complaints, not merely a party submission. It establishes that there were public assemblies and that the applicants were organisers or participants. No further factual detail is provided.

- **Peaceful nature:** No factual grounding whatsoever. The Court does not mention any facts relating to the character or conduct of the assemblies. The peaceful nature is implied solely by the Court's willingness to proceed under Article 11, not by any stated fact.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is the same passage as above: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." These are the concrete governmental acts identified by the Court as constituting the interference. No further factual elaboration is provided.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

No specific facts are identified by the Court to ground its necessity assessment. The Court states: "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints." This is a negative statement — the Court found nothing to distinguish the case from its prior case-law — but it does not identify any particular facts that affirmatively support the necessity finding. The assessment rests entirely on the reference to prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others), not on any facts quoted or adopted from the record.

---

**Summary observation:**

The reasoning is strikingly thin on factual grounding. The only facts the Court explicitly states are: (i) there were public assemblies; (ii) the applicants were organisers or participants; (iii) they were arrested in connection with the dispersal of those assemblies; and (iv) they were convicted of administrative offences. Beyond these four bare facts, the Court's conclusions — including the central finding of a violation — rest on analogy with prior case-law rather than on any articulated factual analysis.
