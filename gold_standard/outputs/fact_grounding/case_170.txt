**Factual grounding for each element discussed:**

The only factual grounding available in the text is the following description:

and

These phrases establish that assemblies existed and that the applicants participated in them. There is no factual grounding for the peacefulness finding — the Court implies it by proceeding with the analysis but states no facts to support it.

**(b) Interference (explicit)**

The factual grounding is:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence"

This is the only factual description of what the government actually did. No further factual detail is provided in this reasoning section.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

Here the factual grounding is notably absent. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion"

This is a reference to an unspecified body of material, but no specific facts are quoted or identified in this reasoning section to ground the necessity assessment. The Court instead relies entirely on its prior case-law:

> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014 and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

The necessity conclusion is thus grounded in precedent rather than in any facts explicitly stated in this section of the judgment.

**Summary observation:**

The reasoning section is strikingly thin on explicit factual grounding. The Court relies primarily on precedent and a general reference to "all the material submitted," without articulating the specific facts that drive its conclusions on any of the canonical elements. This appears consistent with a repetitive-case or "clone case" approach, where the Court treats the matter as sufficiently resolved by prior leading judgments.
