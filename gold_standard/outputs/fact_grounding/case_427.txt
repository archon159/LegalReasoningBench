## Factual Grounding for Each Discussed Doctrinal Element

---

### (a) Scope

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**

The Court does not ground this finding in any newly stated facts in this reasoning section. It relies entirely on a cross-reference to prior case-law: *'The Court refers to its finding that the gathering in question fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Navalnyy v. Russia [GC], nos. 29580/12 and 4 others, §§ 110-12, 15 November 2018).'* No independent factual grounding is provided in this text.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**

The Court states: *'the applicants' intentions and conduct were also covered by the notion of peaceful assembly, even though the participants' initial purpose was to attend a court hearing and not to hold an assembly.'* The factual grounding is the applicants' purpose of attending a court hearing, which the Court treats as sufficient to bring their conduct within the scope of Article 11. However, the Court does not elaborate further on the specific facts underlying this characterisation within this reasoning section.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**

The Court states: *'the applicants' intentions and conduct were also covered by the notion of peaceful assembly.'* The only factual grounding offered is the implicit characterisation of the applicants as *'peaceful participants in the public assembly'*, repeated later: *'the measures applied in respect of the nine applicants as peaceful participants in the public assembly.'* No specific facts about the conduct of the assembly (e.g., absence of violence) are spelled out. The peaceful character appears to be inferred from the nature of the gathering — attending a court hearing — rather than from any explicitly stated factual findings about behaviour.

---

### (b) Interference

The Court grounds its finding of interference in the following explicitly stated facts:

*'the applicants' arrests, transfer to the police station and the ensuing sanctions constituted an interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 § 2.'*

Additionally, the Court accepts as established the following factual account, drawn from the parties' submissions but adopted by the Court itself as a finding:

*'It follows from the parties' submissions that the applicants were arrested, transferred to the police station and charged with administrative offences for the sole reason that their gathering as such had not been authorised.'*

The factual grounding is therefore: (1) arrest; (2) transfer to the police station; (3) being charged with administrative offences; and (4) the reason for all of this being solely the unauthorised nature of the gathering. The Court explicitly adopts this last point as its own conclusion ('it follows'), making it a Court finding rather than merely a party assertion.

---

### (c) Prescribed by law

As noted in the prior analysis, this element is not discussed. No factual grounding can be identified.

---

### (d) Legitimate aim

As noted in the prior analysis, this element is not discussed. No factual grounding can be identified.

---

### (e) Necessity in a democratic society

The Court's finding that the measures were not necessary is grounded in the following facts:

First, the nature of the measures applied, as already established under interference: arrest, transfer to the police station, and administrative sanctions — and crucially, that these were imposed *'for the sole reason that their gathering as such had not been authorised.'*

Second, the characterisation of the applicants as peaceful: *'the measures applied in respect of the nine applicants as peaceful participants in the public assembly did not correspond to a pressing social need.'*

Beyond these two factual anchors, the necessity assessment is not grounded in any further case-specific facts in this reasoning section. The Court relies heavily on precedent rather than on a detailed factual proportionality analysis: *'Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion.'* This formulation suggests the Court considered the facts but found nothing to distinguish the case from prior findings — without spelling out what those facts were.

---
