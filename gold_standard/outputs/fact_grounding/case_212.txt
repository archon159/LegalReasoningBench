This is an important question that requires careful attention to what the Court actually established as fact, versus what was merely asserted or referenced procedurally.

---

**(a) Scope — implied**

**Assembly exists / Participation:**
The only factual grounding is the Court's own characterization in the opening paragraph:
> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as **participants of public assemblies**, namely the **dispersal of these assemblies**, as well as their **arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence**."

This is the Court's own framing of the complaints, not merely a party submission. It establishes that assemblies existed and that the applicants were participants.

**Peaceful character:**
No factual grounding can be identified. The Court does not mention any facts relating to the conduct of the assemblies, the behavior of participants, or the presence or absence of violence. The peacefulness of the assemblies is implied structurally (by the Court proceeding to analyze the interference) but is not grounded in any stated facts.

---

**(b) Interference — explicitly discussed**

The factual grounding is the same passage as above:
> "namely the **dispersal of these assemblies**, as well as their **arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence**."

These are the concrete governmental acts the Court identifies as constituting the interference. No further factual detail is provided — the Court does not describe the circumstances of the dispersal, the arrests, or the convictions beyond this bare characterization.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society — explicitly discussed**

This is the most significant finding, yet it has **no independent factual grounding** in this reasoning section. The Court states:
> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity conclusion is grounded entirely by reference to prior case law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others), not by any factual analysis of the specific circumstances of the present case. The Court does not identify which facts made the interference disproportionate, nor does it weigh any specific governmental justification against the impact on the applicants. The factual basis for the necessity assessment is, in effect, absent from this text — the Court relies on the similarity to leading cases without spelling out the factual parallel.

---

**Overall observation:**
The reasoning section is strikingly sparse in its factual grounding. It reads as a repetitive case judgment (a so-called "clone case") where the Court disposes of the matter by reference to established precedent rather than through independent factual analysis. The only facts explicitly stated are the bare descriptions of the interference (dispersal, arrest, conviction). No facts are provided to support the scope elements or the necessity assessment beyond the structural logic of the judgment itself.
