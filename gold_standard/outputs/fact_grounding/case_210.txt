I will go through each canonical element and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists**
The Court grounds this in the nature of the planned events as described in the proceedings. The Court states: "the Moscow authorities rejected Mr Frolov's notifications of a public religious event." The factual basis for treating this as an assembly is implicit in the Court's application of the Barankevich precedent and the description of the planned events as public gatherings requiring notification under the Public Events Act. No single sentence explicitly states the factual characteristics of the assembly beyond this.

**(ii) Participation**
The factual grounding is: "There is no dispute in the instant case that Mr Frolov did send the required notification within the time-limits established by law." This establishes that Mr Frolov actively attempted to organise and participate in the event.

**(iii) Peaceful**
The Court grounds this in the following facts it itself states: "The Court notes the indisputably peaceful character of the planned religious events. Participants intended to assemble in support of their religion, Vaishnavism, and a particular lifestyle associated with it which in their view offered health benefits. There was no reason to presume a risk of any disturbance of public order or breach of peace on their part."

And further: "It has not been shown that unlawful means of conversion, infringing the rights of others, have been or were likely to be employed by Mr Frolov or other participants."

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds the finding of interference in the following fact: "the Moscow authorities rejected Mr Frolov's notifications of a public religious event."

It also grounds it in the legal framework: "under Russian law, religious events in the public space were to be conducted in accordance with the procedure established for public assemblies," and the authorities' stated reasons: "The authorities however held that the planned event could not proceed because missionary activities – which the promotion of Vaishnavism was taken to be – were inconsistent with the purposes of a public event as defined in the Public Events Act and also incompatible with the respect for the religious beliefs of others."

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

**(i) Foreseeability — first ground (purpose of the event)**
The Court grounds its finding of unforeseeability in the following facts:

On the content of the law: "The Act does not contain a list of permissible purposes or, for that matter, a requirement that a public event should pursue only permissible purposes. The Act does not specify how the purpose of the event should be assessed or provide for the authorities' discretion in determining which purposes are permissible and which are not. The provision to which the domestic courts referred, section 2(1) of the Act, provides a general definition of a 'public event' which may be held for the purposes of 'free expression and shaping of opinions ... on various issues related to ... social and cultural life'."

On the domestic courts' reasoning: "The Russian courts gave no reasons whatsoever for their finding that the promotion of Vaishnavism and a healthy lifestyle fell outside the scope of that broad definition."

**(i) Foreseeability — second ground (type of gathering)**
The Court grounds this in: "they failed to specify what type would have been appropriate in the circumstances. The Moscow City Court's finding that the forms of religious events were limited to 'worship, rites and ceremonies' does not sit well with the provisions of the Religions Act which established that religious events outside places of worship were to be conducted under the rules of the Public Events Act and, accordingly, be cast in one of the forms specified in it."

And: "confronted with the domestic courts' failure to specify the nature of the alleged incompatibility of the planned event with the concept of a 'meeting'."

**(ii) Accessibility**
No factual grounding identified. The Court does not discuss this sub-element.

**(iii) Safeguards**
The only factual grounding is implied in the passage already quoted under foreseeability: "The Act does not specify how the purpose of the event should be assessed or provide for the authorities' discretion in determining which purposes are permissible and which are not." The Court does not develop this into a standalone safeguards analysis, so no additional factual grounding can be identified beyond this.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not make an affirmative factual finding that a legitimate aim was served. It references the Government's submission that the interference pursued "a legitimate aim" for "the protection of public order and the rights of others," but the Court does not adopt this as established. The only factual grounding the Court itself provides in this vicinity is negative — it notes: "There was no reason to presume a risk of any disturbance of public order or breach of peace on their part," and "It has not been shown that unlawful means of conversion, infringing the rights of others, have been or were likely to be employed by Mr Frolov or other participants." These facts are deployed under necessity, not legitimate aim. No factual grounding for an affirmative legitimate aim finding can be identified.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court grounds its finding of lack of necessity in the following facts:

On the content-based nature of the restriction: "The Court notes that the domestic authorities did not have any objections to the planned events being held at a specific location or time... Rather, their objections related to the religious nature of planned events and accordingly amounted to content-based restrictions on freedom of assembly."

On the peaceful character and absence of disorder risk: "The Court notes the indisputably peaceful character of the planned religious events. Participants intended to assemble in support of their religion, Vaishnavism, and a particular lifestyle associated with it which in their view offered health benefits. There was no reason to presume a risk of any disturbance of public order or breach of peace on their part."

On the absence of unlawful means: "It has not been shown that unlawful means of conversion, infringing the rights of others, have been or were likely to be employed by Mr Frolov or other participants."

On the comparable events in another region: "it is also significant that the textually identical notifications filed in another Russian region were not met with any objections. There is no evidence of any disturbances during those events which appear to have been able to proceed peacefully. This reinforces the Court's finding that, in rejecting Mr Frolov's notifications of the planned religious events, the Moscow authorities acted in an arbitrary manner."

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

No factual grounding to identify. The Court does not discuss positive obligations in this judgment.
