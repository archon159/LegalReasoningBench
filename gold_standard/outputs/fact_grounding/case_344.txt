**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**
The only factual grounding is the phrase "public assemblies" and "dispersal of these assemblies" from the introductory paragraph:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is drawn from the applicants' complaints. The Court does not independently establish or verify the existence of assemblies — it does not explicitly adopt this as a Court-established fact. **No factual grounding can be identified that the Court itself established.**

**ii) Participation**
Same passage:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies."

Same caveat applies — this characterisation originates in the applicants' complaints. **No factual grounding can be identified that the Court itself established.**

**iii) Peaceful character**
**No factual grounding can be identified.** The Court says nothing about the character of the assemblies.

**(b) Interference**

The Court's own language explicitly acknowledges interference:
> "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The factual grounding for what the interference consisted of comes from the introductory paragraph, which the Court appears to adopt as the factual backdrop for its analysis:
> "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

However, it must be noted that this description originates in the applicants' complaints. The Court does not separately and explicitly verify these facts as established. That said, by proceeding to assess necessity on this basis, the Court implicitly treats these facts as given.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court's conclusion is:
> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The factual grounding here is notably **absent in any specific sense**. The Court does not cite particular facts about the circumstances of the dispersals, the conduct of the applicants, or the reasons given by the authorities. Instead, the necessity finding is grounded entirely in:
1. A reference to prior leading cases (*Frumkin*, *Navalnyy and Yashin*, *Kasparov and Others*) involving "issues similar to those in the present case."
2. The general statement that the Court examined "all the material submitted to it" without specifying what that material showed.

No specific facts from the case record are quoted or adopted by the Court in support of the necessity finding.

---

**Overall observation:** This reasoning section is a highly compressed, repetitive-case-style judgment. The Court relies almost entirely on precedent rather than case-specific factual analysis. As a result, the factual grounding for most doctrinal elements — even those implied or explicitly discussed — is either absent or traceable only to the applicants' unverified characterisation of events in their complaints.
