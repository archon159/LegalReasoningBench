# Factual Grounding for Each Doctrinal Element

---

## (a) Scope

### (i) Assembly exists
The Court does not explicitly identify specific facts grounding the existence of an assembly. The existence of a demonstration is treated as uncontested and the Court simply states: 'The Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11.' No independent factual grounding is articulated by the Court itself.

### (ii) Participation
The Court grounds the applicant's participation in one factual observation it makes directly: 'the applicant joined them at about 7 p.m.' This is the only fact the Court itself states regarding her participation.

### (iii) Peaceful character
The Court grounds the peaceful character of the assembly in one factual observation embedded in its legal analysis: 'to disperse the ensuing demonstration solely because of the absence of the requisite prior notice, **without any illegal conduct by the participants**.' This is the Court's own characterisation of the facts, not merely a party submission.

---

## (b) Interference

No specific facts are independently identified by the Court. The Court simply states: 'The Government did not dispute that the applicant could rely on the guarantees contained in Article 11; nor did they deny that the dispersal of the demonstration had interfered with the exercise of the applicant's rights under that provision. The Court sees no reason to hold otherwise.' The factual grounding is the dispersal itself, but the Court does not elaborate on the specific facts of the dispersal beyond accepting it as uncontested.

---

## (c) Prescribed by law

### (i) Foreseeability
The Court grounds this solely in: 'the restriction imposed on the applicant's freedom of peaceful assembly was based on section 14 of the Assembly Act, **the wording of which is clear**.' No further factual elaboration is provided.

### (ii) Accessibility
No factual grounding identified. The Court does not address this separately.

### (iii) Safeguards against abuse
The Court grounds this in the following facts it explicitly states: 'at the material time, no authorisation was required in Hungary for the holding of public demonstrations; however, the notification of the police was required seventy-two hours prior to the event. If the police decided to ban a demonstration, the organisers could seek judicial review within three days.' These are presented as established features of the Hungarian legal framework that the Court itself relies upon.

---

## (d) Legitimate aim

The Court does not independently identify specific facts grounding the legitimate aims. It accepts the aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others, but the factual basis is not separately articulated beyond the general context of a public demonstration. The Court's conclusion — 'the Court is satisfied that the measure complained of pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others' — is stated without explicit factual elaboration beyond what the Government submitted. Since the Court does not explicitly adopt specific factual assertions from the Government's submissions on this point as established facts, no verbatim factual grounding can be identified here without risk of misattribution.

---

## (e) Necessity in a democratic society

This is the most factually grounded element. The Court relies on the following facts, stated in its own voice:

**On the absence of special circumstances warranting immediate response:**
'the official results of the elections had been made public on 4 May 2002, two months before the impugned demonstration, and that the outcome of those elections had been objectively established.'

'the Court is not persuaded that this matter would have become obsolete had the demonstrators respected the notification rule.' (This is a legal conclusion, but it is grounded in the above temporal fact.)

**On the disorderly character of the events:**
'the impugned events originated in an illegal demonstration (see paragraphs 10 and 14 above) blocking a main bridge in central Budapest.'

'the declared objective of this latter gathering, in which the applicant participated, was to support those who had illegally demonstrated at the Erzsébet Bridge.'

'The essentially disorderly character of this combination of events is therefore so manifest that the decision of the police to disband the gathering cannot be said to be at variance with the object and purpose of Article 11 of the Convention.'

**On the duration of the demonstration before dispersal:**
'the demonstrators gathered at Kossuth Square at about 1 p.m. and the applicant joined them at about 7 p.m. However, the police did not break up the demonstration until about 9 p.m., with the result that the demonstrators had had several hours at their disposal to manifest their views.'

**On the tolerance shown by police:**
'the Court is satisfied that the police showed the necessary tolerance towards the demonstration, although they had had no prior knowledge of the event.'

**On the disruption caused:**
'which, in the Court's view, inevitably disrupted the circulation of the traffic and caused a certain disturbance to public order.'

**On the ability to exercise assembly rights:**
'the demonstrators were able to exercise, for several hours, their right to peaceful assembly as guaranteed by the Convention.'

---

## (f) Positive obligation

The Court does not ground any positive obligation analysis in independently stated facts. The positive obligation framing appears only in the context of justifying the notification requirement as a tool serving the State's obligations toward others. The Court does not reach a separate factual conclusion on this element. No factual grounding can be identified without misattributing party submissions to the Court.
