I will go through each element that was found to be either implied or explicitly discussed, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**

The only factual grounding in the reasoning text is: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of **public assemblies**, namely their arrest in relation to the **dispersal of these assemblies**."

This is thin — the Court names "public assemblies" and refers to their "dispersal," but provides no further factual detail about the nature, location, or content of the assemblies.

**ii) Participation**

The only factual grounding is: "disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers or participants of public assemblies**."

Again, this is a bare characterization rather than a developed factual finding.

**iii) Peaceful character**

There is **no factual grounding** in the reasoning text for the peaceful character of the assemblies. The Court does not mention this at all. The implication that the assemblies were peaceful is purely structural (the Court applies Article 11 without excluding the applicants from its protection), but no fact is cited to support it.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their **arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies** and their **conviction for administrative offences**."

These two concrete state acts — arrest and criminal conviction — are the only facts identified as constituting the interference. No further factual detail is provided in the reasoning text.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central finding, yet the factual grounding in the reasoning text is notably sparse. The Court states:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has **not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion** as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And: "Having regard to its **case-law on the subject**, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity conclusion is grounded not in specific facts from the instant case, but in:
1. A reference to prior leading cases (Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, Kasparov and Others v. Russia) involving "issues similar to those in the present case."
2. The absence of any new fact or argument that would distinguish the present case from those precedents.

In other words, the Court's factual grounding for the necessity assessment is essentially **negative** — no distinguishing facts were found — rather than positive. The reasoning text does not quote or adopt any specific facts about the proportionality of the government's response beyond what is captured in the bare description of arrest and conviction noted under interference above.

---

**Summary observation**

Across all elements, the factual grounding in this reasoning text is exceptionally thin. The Court is operating in a repetitive-case mode, relying on the similarity to established leading cases rather than conducting an independent factual analysis. The only concrete facts stated are: (1) the applicants were organisers or participants of public assemblies; (2) those assemblies were dispersed; (3) the applicants were arrested; and (4) they were convicted of administrative offences. All doctrinal conclusions rest on these four bare facts plus the reference to prior case-law.
