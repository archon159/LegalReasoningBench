This is an important question, and the honest answer is that **the reasoning text is almost entirely devoid of factual grounding** for any of the canonical elements. The Court's reasoning is conducted at a very high level of abstraction, relying on prior case-law rather than on facts specific to the instant case. Here is the element-by-element assessment:

---

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding offered is the following sentence describing the applicants' situation:

> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as **participants or organisers of public assemblies**, namely their arrest in relation to the **dispersal of these assemblies** and their **conviction for administrative offences**."

This is the sole factual basis from which the Court implicitly derives that there were assemblies, that the applicants participated in or organised them, and that the assemblies were dispersed. However, this sentence characterises the applicants' *complaints* rather than the Court's own findings of fact. The Court does not independently verify or adopt these facts as established — it simply proceeds on this basis without explicit endorsement.

No factual grounding is provided for the peacefulness of the assemblies.

---

**(b) Interference (implied)**

The same sentence provides the only factual basis:

> "...their **arrest** in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their **conviction for administrative offences**."

Again, this is drawn from the characterisation of the complaints, not from an independent factual finding by the Court.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicitly discussed)**

This is the only element the Court explicitly addresses, yet it provides **no case-specific factual grounding whatsoever**. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And:

> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity assessment is grounded entirely in the prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and the absence of distinguishing facts, not in any affirmative factual findings about what happened in the instant case.

---

**Overall observation:** The reasoning text contains no independent judicial fact-finding. The Court does not quote evidence, describe events, or make explicit findings of fact. Its entire analysis rests on the characterisation of the applicants' complaints and a reference back to leading cases. This is characteristic of a repetitive-case procedure (likely a well-established violation case handled under a streamlined approach), where the Court treats the facts as sufficiently analogous to prior cases without restating them.
