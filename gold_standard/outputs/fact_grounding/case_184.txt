I will go through each element that was found to be either explicitly discussed or implied, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**
The factual grounding is: "dispersal of these assemblies" and "organisers or participants of public assemblies." These phrases establish as fact that assemblies took place. Verbatim: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies."

**ii) Participation**
The factual grounding is the same passage: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies." The arrest of the applicants in connection with the dispersal establishes their physical presence and participation. Verbatim: "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

**iii) Peaceful character**
No factual grounding can be identified. The Court says nothing about the conduct of the assemblies, the behaviour of participants, or any absence of violence. The peaceful character is implied only by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis, not by any stated fact.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is explicit and specific. Verbatim: "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences." These two concrete state actions — arrest and criminal conviction — are the facts on which the finding of interference rests.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central finding, but the factual grounding in this particular reasoning section is notably thin. The Court does not conduct an independent factual analysis. Instead, it grounds its necessity assessment on two things:

First, a reference to prior case-law: "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

Second, a negative finding on new material: "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

The only concrete facts stated — arrest and conviction for administrative offences in connection with the dispersal of assemblies — are thus the entire factual basis on which the necessity assessment rests, by reference to the similarity with the leading cases. No additional or more granular facts are stated in this reasoning section.

---

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.
