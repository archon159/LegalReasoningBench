I will go through each element that was found to be either implied or explicitly discussed, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope (implied)**

**i) Existence of an assembly:**
The only factual grounding is the following phrase from the opening paragraph:
> "organisers or participants of public assemblies"

No further facts about the nature, size, location, or organisation of the assemblies are provided in this reasoning section.

**ii) Participation of the applicants:**
Again, the only factual grounding is:
> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies"

**iii) Peaceful nature of the assembly:**
There is no factual grounding for this element. The Court does not mention any facts relating to the conduct of the assemblies or the behaviour of participants. This element is implied purely by the Court's legal logic (proceeding to assess interference under Article 11 without questioning peacefulness), but is not grounded in any stated facts.

---

**(b) Interference (explicit)**

The factual grounding for the interference finding is:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences"

These are the only concrete facts stated: arrest, dispersal of assemblies, and conviction for administrative offences. No further factual detail is provided in this section.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

This is the most significant finding, yet it has the weakest explicit factual grounding in the text. The Court does not set out any specific facts about the proportionality of the measures, the conduct of authorities, or the weight of the legitimate aim. Instead, the Court grounds its necessity assessment entirely by reference to prior case law:

> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

And:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And:

> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

So the necessity finding is grounded not in specific facts about this case, but in the similarity to prior cases and the absence of any distinguishing facts or arguments. The only case-specific factual element that feeds into this assessment is the same as under interference: arrest, dispersal, and conviction for administrative offences.

There is one additional factual element mentioned in the context of victim status, which is relevant to the overall assessment:
> "mere discontinuation of the administrative-offence proceedings in respect of the applicants, without acknowledgment and sufficient redress of violation of their conventional rights do not deprive them of a victim status"

This tells us that the administrative proceedings were discontinued, but the Court treats this as a procedural/victim-status fact rather than a substantive fact bearing on necessity.

---

**Summary observation:**
The reasoning section is notably sparse in its factual grounding. The Court relies heavily on the precedent of prior Russian assembly cases and the absence of distinguishing material, rather than on a detailed factual analysis of the specific circumstances of each applicant's case.
