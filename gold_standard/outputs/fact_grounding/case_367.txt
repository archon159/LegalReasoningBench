I will go through each element where I found explicit discussion or implication, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text itself.

---

**(a) Scope**

- **Assembly exists / Participation:** The only factual grounding is the following sentence from the introductory paragraph:

> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as **participants of public assemblies**, namely the **dispersal of these assemblies**, as well as their **arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence**."

This is the Court's own framing of the complaint, not a party submission. However, it must be noted that the Court is here describing what the applicants complained of, not making an independent factual finding. The Court does not separately and explicitly establish as fact that assemblies took place or that the applicants participated. The implication of scope being satisfied rests on the Court proceeding to analyze Article 11 at all, rather than on any explicit factual finding.

- **Peaceful character:** No factual grounding whatsoever. The Court says nothing about the character of the assemblies. The peaceful nature is implied solely by the Court's willingness to engage with Article 11, not by any stated fact.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is the same sentence quoted above:

> "the **dispersal of these assemblies**, as well as their **arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence**."

These are the concrete governmental acts identified. Again, this appears in the Court's description of the complaint rather than in a separate section of established facts. No further factual detail is provided in the reasoning text.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the element most explicitly discussed, yet the factual grounding is notably thin. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it and having taken into account the issue of compliance with the six-month time-limit under Article 35 § 1 of the Convention [...] the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And:

> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity assessment is grounded not in specific facts about what happened at the assemblies, but in:
1. A reference to "all the material submitted," without identifying any of it;
2. A reference to prior leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov) as establishing the relevant conclusion for "issues similar to those in the present case."

No specific facts — such as the conduct of police, the behavior of participants, the proportionality of the sentences imposed, or the reasons given by domestic courts — are quoted or identified in the reasoning text as the factual basis for the necessity finding. The Court's reasoning is essentially comparative and deferential to its own prior case-law, not grounded in independently stated facts from the present case.

---

**Overall observation**

The reasoning text is extremely sparse in its factual grounding. This is characteristic of a repetitive-case judgment, where the Court treats the case as materially identical to prior leading cases and does not re-examine the facts in detail. For most canonical elements, no factual grounding can be identified in the reasoning text beyond the bare description of the complaint in the opening paragraph.
