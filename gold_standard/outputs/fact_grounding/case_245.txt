This is an important methodological question. Let me work through each element carefully, looking only for facts that the Court itself relied upon — not merely asserted by parties.

---

**(a) Scope**

**Assembly exists / Participation:**
The only factual grounding is the Court's own framing in the opening paragraph:
> "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence"

And:
> "participants of public assemblies"

These are the Court's own characterisations, not party submissions. They establish that assemblies took place and that the applicants participated in them.

**Peaceful:**
There is no factual grounding whatsoever. The Court states no facts — and adopts no party submissions — that speak to the peaceful or non-peaceful character of the assemblies. The inference that the assemblies were peaceful is purely structural (the Court applies Article 11 without qualification), but it rests on no identified facts in this text.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is the same passage as above:
> "the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence"

These are the concrete governmental acts the Court identifies as constituting the interference. No further factual detail is provided.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central finding, yet it is striking that **the Court provides no independent factual grounding** for its necessity assessment in this text. The Court does not describe the circumstances of the dispersal, the conduct of authorities, the proportionality of the response, or any other concrete facts bearing on necessity. Instead, it grounds its conclusion entirely in:

1. A reference to prior case-law:
> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014 and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

2. A general statement of having reviewed the material:
> "Having examined all the material submitted to it... the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

The necessity conclusion is thus grounded in the similarity to prior cases, not in any facts from the present case that are quoted or adopted in this text.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**Overall observation:**
The reasoning text is notably sparse in its factual grounding. The Court operates almost entirely by reference to precedent and a general assertion of having reviewed the material, without setting out the specific facts of the present case that it found relevant. This is characteristic of a repetitive-case procedure where the Court treats the matter as essentially resolved by prior leading judgments.
