This is an important question that requires careful reading of the reasoning text. The honest answer is that **the reasoning text is remarkably thin on factual grounding**. The Court does not narrate or rely upon specific facts in its reasoning. Let me go through each element:

---

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding comes from the introductory characterisation of the complaints:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the sole factual basis from which the Court's implied conclusions on scope (existence of assembly, participation, and peacefulness) could be drawn. However, it should be noted that this sentence is a description of the applicants' *complaints*, not an independent factual finding by the Court. The Court does not separately and explicitly establish these facts as proven.

---

**(b) Interference (explicit)**

The factual grounding is the same passage:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

Again, this is drawn from the characterisation of the complaints rather than from an independent factual assessment by the Court.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

There is **no identifiable factual grounding** for the necessity finding. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The Court references "all the material submitted to it" but does not identify, quote, or summarise any specific facts from that material. The necessity conclusion is grounded entirely in a reference to prior case-law (*Frumkin*, *Navalnyy and Yashin*, *Kasparov and Others*), not in any factual analysis of the specific circumstances of the instant case.

---

**Overall observation**

This reasoning section appears to be a **repetitive case judgment** (sometimes called a "clone" or "repetitive case" in ECtHR practice), where the Court disposes of cases that are substantially similar to already-decided leading cases by cross-reference, without conducting a fresh factual analysis. The factual grounding for the legal conclusions is therefore either absent or only minimally present in this excerpt.
