I will go through each element where the Court made an explicit or implied finding, and identify only the facts the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a)(i) Scope: Assembly exists**

The Court does not explicitly state facts establishing the existence of assemblies as a separate finding. However, it treats the assemblies as factual givens throughout. The closest factual grounding is the Court's own references to the events:

> 'The Court observes that the organisers did not submit a prior notice about the demonstration of 29 December 2013 to the BCEA.'

> 'the Court is ready to accept that the dispersal of the demonstrations on 12 January, 26 January and 10 March 2013 was lawful'

These references presuppose that demonstrations took place, but the Court does not separately ground this in identified facts.

---

**(a)(iii) Scope: Peaceful assembly**

The Court's only explicit factual grounding for the peaceful character of the assemblies is its own statement:

> 'the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention'

The Court characterises the demonstrations as peaceful here, but does not cite specific facts establishing this. The applicants' submission that the demonstrations 'had been intended to be peaceful and had been held in a peaceful manner' is noted in the parties' submissions section, but the Court does not explicitly adopt this as an established fact. The peaceful character appears to be assumed rather than grounded in identified facts.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

For the **29 December 2013** demonstration, the Court grounds its doubts about lawfulness in two facts it explicitly accepts:

> 'it is ready to accept that that demonstration was an immediate response to the death of a disabled war veteran who had set himself on fire as a protest against the bureaucratic injustices that he had allegedly faced. The fact that the funeral was held on 29 December constituted a special circumstance warranting a spontaneous demonstration on that particular date, overriding the obligation to submit a prior notice of an assembly.'

> 'according to Article 5 § IV of the Law on Freedom of Assembly, prior written notification was indeed not required for "spontaneous assemblies"'

For the **12 January, 26 January and 10 March 2013** demonstrations, the Court grounds its acceptance of lawfulness in the failure of the applicants to substantiate spontaneity:

> 'the Court considers that the applicants failed to sufficiently substantiate their argument that those demonstrations were spontaneous. In particular, considering that the death of soldiers in the army had already been a widespread problem, as submitted by the applicant in application no. 20589/13, it is not clear why there was an urgent need to hold a demonstration on a specific date, 12 January 2013, without submitting five days' written notice to the BCEA.'

Note: the Court here uses the applicant's own submission ('death of soldiers in the army had already been a widespread problem') against the applicant's argument, not to establish a fact in the applicant's favour. It is adopted as a conceded fact that undermines the spontaneity claim.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court's factual grounding for the necessity finding is notably thin in this judgment — it relies primarily on a reference to a prior case rather than independently identified facts:

> 'the Court notes that the issues raised by the present complaints are essentially the same as those examined in Gafgaz Mammadov (cited above). The facts of that case and the present cases are similar to a significant degree. Consequently, for the same reasons as those outlined in the Gafgaz Mammadov judgment, the Court concludes that the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations.'

The Court does identify one concrete factual element it relies upon:

> 'The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention.'

And it grounds the chilling effect finding in the following facts:

> 'The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicants' arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies.'

> 'The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of the sanctions that could potentially be applied against the participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly had a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly. Such a chilling effect deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate.'

The specific nature of the sanctions is noted in the parties' submissions (the Government acknowledged that 'the sanction applied to the applicants had been administrative detention'), but the Court does not explicitly adopt this as a factual finding in its own assessment — it refers only generally to 'arrests and convictions' and 'measures applied.'

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not separately ground this in identified facts beyond treating the following as self-evident from the complaints:

> 'the dispersal of the demonstrations by the police and their arrest and conviction for an administrative offence'

No further factual elaboration is provided by the Court itself on this element.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

As noted previously, the Court makes no explicit finding on legitimate aim and provides no factual grounding for this element. No facts can be mapped here.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.
