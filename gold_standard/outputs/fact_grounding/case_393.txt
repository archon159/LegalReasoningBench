# Factual Grounding for Each Doctrinal Element

---

## (a) Scope

### i) Assembly exists

The Court does not explicitly articulate a factual finding that "an assembly existed" as a discrete step. However, the existence of a gathering is treated as uncontested and is implicit in the Court's framing. The closest factual grounding is the Court's acceptance of the following:

> "The Court is prepared to agree with the Government that impeding the scheduled eviction of the Schijnheilig squat was the foreseeable result of intentional action by the organisers and participants, including the applicants."

No further discrete factual finding is made on this element. The existence of a gathering is assumed rather than argued.

---

### ii) Participation

The Court grounds its finding on the applicants' participation in the following factual observation:

> "The Court notes that the applicants were not amongst the group of protesters who were arrested and prosecuted on suspicion of publicly committing concerted acts of violence against persons or property."

And:

> "Since it does not appear from the materials in the case file that the applicants – who must be presumed to have had peaceful intentions in the absence of sufficient and convincing evidence to the contrary – personally set off smoke bombs, threw objects or kicked out in the direction of the police, or otherwise resorted or incited to violence..."

These passages confirm the applicants were present and participating, while distinguishing their conduct from that of violent participants.

---

### iii) Peaceful

This is the most extensively fact-grounded element. The Court makes the following factual findings:

**On the online calls and slogans:**
> "The Court further considers that no violent intentions or behaviour can be inferred from the calls posted online or the slogans chanted. On the face of it and given the context, they should be understood as expressions of dissatisfaction and protest rather than deliberate and unambiguous calls for violence."

**On air mattresses and balaclavas:**
> "Nor can any such intentions or behaviour be inferred in itself from the fact that several participants brought air mattresses or wore balaclavas or other disguises."

**On the applicants' personal conduct:**
> "Since it does not appear from the materials in the case file that the applicants – who must be presumed to have had peaceful intentions in the absence of sufficient and convincing evidence to the contrary – personally set off smoke bombs, threw objects or kicked out in the direction of the police, or otherwise resorted or incited to violence, the Court finds that the conduct during the gathering for which they were held responsible was not of such a nature and degree as to remove their participation in it from the scope of protection of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention."

**On the obstructive (but not violent) purpose of the gathering:**
> "The Court is prepared to agree with the Government that impeding the scheduled eviction of the Schijnheilig squat was the foreseeable result of intentional action by the organisers and participants, including the applicants. However...such obstructive or disruptive conduct might still be protected by that provision."

And:

> "Even if the purpose of the gathering went beyond conveying disapproval of the eviction of the Schijnheilig squat and the participants also sought to prevent the lawful eviction of the squat (potentially amounting to 'a form of coercion'...), this did not, of itself, remove the applicants' participation in it from the scope of protection of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention."

**On the applicants' non-membership in the violent group:**
> "The Court notes that the applicants were not amongst the group of protesters who were arrested and prosecuted on suspicion of publicly committing concerted acts of violence against persons or property."

---

## (b) Interference

The Court grounds the existence of an interference in the following factual finding:

> "the Court finds that the applicants are entitled to invoke the guarantees of Article 11, which is therefore applicable ratione materiae in the present case, and that their arrest, prosecution and conviction amounted to an interference with their right to freedom of peaceful assembly."

The specific acts constituting the interference — arrest, prosecution, and conviction — are identified as facts. No further elaboration of the underlying factual circumstances of the arrest etc. is provided in the reasoning section quoted; the Court treats these as established.

---

## (c) Prescribed by law

The Court explicitly declines to make any factual findings on this element:

> "The Court notes that the parties disagreed as to whether the interference was prescribed by law. However, the Court decides to dispense with ruling on the issue of lawfulness because, in any event, the interference cannot be said to have been 'necessary in a democratic society', for the reasons set out below."

**No factual grounding can be identified for this element**, as the Court made no findings on it.

---

## (d) Legitimate aim

The Court's acceptance of a legitimate aim is stated without reference to specific underlying facts beyond the nature of the interference itself:

> "The Court further accepts that the interference may be regarded as having pursued the 'prevention of disorder or crime' and the 'protection of the rights and freedoms of others' within the meaning of paragraph 2 of Article 11."

The Court does not articulate specific facts grounding this acceptance beyond the general nature of the situation (a gathering involving some violence, directed at preventing an eviction). No discrete factual findings are cited in support of this conclusion. The acceptance appears to follow from the general circumstances already described rather than from any new factual determination.

---

## (e) Necessity in a democratic society

The Court's finding on necessity is grounded not in facts about the proportionality of the measures as such, but in a procedural/analytical fact about what the domestic courts did or failed to do:

> "The Court notes that the Supreme Court found that the appellate court did not err in law and was not incomprehensible in its ruling that there had been no demonstration within the meaning of the Public Assemblies Act as the aim of the gathering had been to seek confrontation and to (physically) prevent the eviction, adopting the position that Article 11 of the Convention was inapplicable as well."

And:

> "The Supreme Court essentially stopped its assessment at that point; it did not examine whether the applicants' role in the gathering was in fact 'peaceful' within the autonomous meaning given to that concept in the Court's case-law."

These are the factual findings on which the necessity conclusion rests — namely, that the Supreme Court terminated its analysis prematurely and did not conduct the required balancing exercise. The Court then concludes:

> "By reaching such a conclusion and not exercising the balancing test required under Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, the Supreme Court failed to give relevant and sufficient reasons for the interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly, and thus failed to convincingly establish the necessity for such restrictions."

And:

> "the Court finds that the requirements under Article 11 of the Convention were not met because the analysis of applicability of that provision – and, consequently, the assessment of the justification of the interference – were not carried out at the domestic level in a manner consistent with the Convention and the Court's case-law."

---

## (f) Positive obligation

As noted previously, the Court does not discuss positive obligations. **No factual grounding can be identified**, as this element is entirely absent from the reasoning.
