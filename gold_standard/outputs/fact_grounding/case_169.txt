I'll work through each canonical element where I found explicit discussion or implication, identifying only the facts the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:**
The Court's own text refers to the events as "demonstrations" and "assemblies" throughout. The only direct factual grounding in the Court's own words is:
> "the organisers did not give the BCEA prior notice about the demonstrations of 12 and 26 January 2013"

The Court treats these as assemblies without further factual elaboration.

**ii) Participation:**
The Court grounds this in:
> "the demonstrations in which he participated"

and

> "the applicant's arrests and convictions"

**iii) Peaceful:**
The Court itself states:
> "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention"

This is the Court's own characterisation. However, the Court does not explicitly state which facts led it to conclude the demonstrations were peaceful. No specific factual grounding is offered by the Court for this characterisation — it is asserted rather than derived from stated facts. I cannot identify a factual grounding beyond the Court's bare assertion.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds the interference finding in:
> "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies."

and

> "The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly."

The underlying facts the Court relies on are therefore: the dispersal of the demonstrations, the applicant's arrests, and his convictions for an administrative offence. These are treated as established facts throughout the judgment.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court's factual grounding for its lawfulness finding is:

First, the absence of prior notification:
> "the organisers did not give the BCEA prior notice about the demonstrations of 12 and 26 January 2013"

Second, the legal framework:
> "under Article 5 § IV of the Law on Freedom of Assembly, no prior written notification was required for 'spontaneous assemblies'"

Third, the Court's own factual reasoning on why the spontaneity exception did not apply:
> "given that, as submitted by the applicant, the death of soldiers in the army had already been a widespread problem in the country, it is not clear why it was urgent to hold a demonstration on a specific date, 12 January 2013, without giving five days' written notice"

Note: the Court here adopts the applicant's own submission about the widespread nature of soldier deaths as an established fact, but uses it against the applicant's spontaneity argument. The Court does not adopt the applicant's characterisation that the demonstration was spontaneous.

Fourth, the conclusion:
> "the applicant has failed to sufficiently substantiate his allegation that the demonstrations in which he participated were spontaneous ones"

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

As noted in my prior analysis, the Court does not make an explicit finding on legitimate aim. I cannot identify any factual grounding offered by the Court for this element, because the Court does not address it. No mapping is possible.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court's factual grounding here is notably thin in this specific judgment, as it relies heavily on a cross-reference to a prior case:

> "the issues raised by the applicant and the facts of the present cases closely resemble those of the Gafgaz Mammadov case. Therefore, for the same reasons as those outlined in the Gafgaz Mammadov judgment, the Court concludes that the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations"

The Court then adds two factual conclusions of its own:

> "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention"

and the chilling effect reasoning:

> "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies. The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly. This deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate."

The concrete facts underpinning the necessity analysis in this judgment are therefore: the dispersal of the demonstrations, the arrests, and the convictions. The substantive reasoning is largely borrowed from *Gafgaz Mammadov* rather than developed from the specific facts of this case.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

As noted previously, the Court does not address positive obligations. No mapping is possible.
