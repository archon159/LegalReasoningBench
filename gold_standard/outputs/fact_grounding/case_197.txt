**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court's necessity assessment is notably thin on independent factual grounding. The reasoning text states: "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This is a negative formulation — the Court does not cite specific facts from the present case to ground its necessity finding. Instead, it grounds its conclusion entirely by reference to prior case-law: "Having regard to its case-law on the subject" and the leading cases of *Frumkin v. Russia*, *Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia*, and *Kasparov and Others v. Russia*, which are described as cases where "the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

In other words, the factual grounding for the necessity assessment is borrowed from those prior cases rather than independently established in this reasoning section. No specific facts from the present case are cited to support the necessity conclusion beyond the bare description of arrest and conviction already noted under interference.
