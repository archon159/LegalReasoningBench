Here is the analysis of the factual grounding for each element that was found to be either explicitly discussed or implied:

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:**
The only factual grounding is the following phrase from the opening paragraph:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of **public assemblies**"

This is the Court's own characterisation, not merely a party submission. However, it is extremely thin — the Court does not describe the nature, location, size, or purpose of any specific assembly.

**ii) Participation:**
The same passage provides the only factual grounding:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers or participants** of public assemblies"

Again, the Court itself uses this characterisation, but provides no further factual detail about the applicants' individual roles or conduct.

**iii) Peaceful:**
No factual grounding can be identified. The Court says nothing about the character or conduct of the assemblies. The peacefulness of the assemblies is implied structurally by the Court's proceeding to assess necessity, but there is no fact stated in the reasoning text that grounds this conclusion.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court identifies the following concrete governmental acts as the interference:
> "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences"

This is the Court's own description of the measures complained of. No further factual detail is provided about the circumstances of the arrests, the dispersals, or the nature of the convictions.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the element most explicitly discussed, yet paradoxically it has the least independent factual grounding in the reasoning text itself. The Court's conclusion rests entirely on a reference to prior case law rather than on any facts specific to the present case:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The Court references the leading cases of *Frumkin v. Russia*, *Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia*, and *Kasparov and Others v. Russia* as involving "issues similar to those in the present case," but does not specify which facts of the present case correspond to those prior cases. No specific facts from the present case are cited to ground the necessity assessment.

---

**Overall observation:**

The reasoning text is strikingly sparse in its factual grounding across all elements. The Court is operating in a repetitive-case mode (a so-called "clone case" or pilot-procedure-adjacent approach), relying on the similarity to established leading cases rather than conducting an independent fact-specific analysis. The only concrete facts stated are: (1) the applicants were organisers or participants of public assemblies; and (2) they were arrested in connection with the dispersal of those assemblies and convicted of administrative offences. All doctrinal elements beyond interference and necessity are left without any explicit factual grounding whatsoever.
