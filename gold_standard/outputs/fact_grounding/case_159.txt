I will go through each canonical element and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court grounds this entirely by reference to prior case law, without restating the underlying facts: "The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Yaroslav Belousov, cited above, §§ 168-71)." No independent factual grounding is provided in this text beyond the reference.

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly state facts establishing participation as such. It is implied by the entire framing of the case — the applicant was prosecuted and convicted for acts committed at the assembly — but no discrete factual finding is quoted by the Court to establish participation. No quotable factual grounding can be identified beyond what is implied by the prosecution itself.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful (as to the applicant personally)?**
The Court grounds this on the following finding: "it does not transpire from the description of his offences that he was among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character." Additionally, in the necessity section, the Court reinforces this: "there is nothing in the case file to suggest that the applicant was among those responsible for the onset of clashes between the protesters and the police." And: "The Court concludes that the applicant was not regarded as one of the individuals responsible for the disruption of the assembly or prompting the use of force by the police."

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court's finding of interference is stated as a legal conclusion — "the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for participation in mass disorder constituted an interference with the exercise of the freedom of assembly" — without separately enumerating the underlying facts beyond the fact of prosecution and conviction itself. The factual grounding is the existence of the prosecution and criminal conviction, which is established throughout the text. The most concrete statement of what happened to the applicant is: "he was found guilty of two criminal offences, namely participation in acts of mass disorder (Article 212 of the Criminal Code) and committing violent acts against a police officer (Article 318 of the Criminal Code). He was sentenced to three years and seven months' imprisonment under those provisions."

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court makes no independent factual findings on this element. It simply states: "The Court sees no reason to reach a different conclusion in this case (ibid., §§ 174-75). It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful." The factual grounding is entirely delegated to the Yaroslav Belousov judgment. No facts are independently stated or quoted in this text to support the lawfulness finding.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

As with prescribed by law, the Court states the conclusion without independent factual elaboration: "It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution were lawful, and pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others." No discrete facts are identified by the Court in this text to ground the legitimate aim finding. The finding is stated as a bare legal conclusion by reference to the prior judgment.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the most factually grounded element. The Court relies on the following facts, which it either establishes from the case file or explicitly accepts as established:

**Nature and severity of the applicant's acts:**
"he was found guilty of two criminal offences, namely participation in acts of mass disorder (Article 212 of the Criminal Code) and committing violent acts against a police officer (Article 318 of the Criminal Code). He was sentenced to three years and seven months' imprisonment under those provisions for hitting a police officer, once on the head and once on the body, which did not endanger the latter's life or health, while participating in an authorised public assembly."

**Disproportionate weight of the mass disorder sentence relative to the assault sentence:**
"the sentence of one year and eight months' imprisonment under Article 318 of the Criminal Code for using violence against a public official, namely hitting him, although severe, was more lenient than the partly concurrent three-year prison sentence for participation in acts of mass disorder."

**The Government's stated justification and its implications:**
"The main justification advanced by the Government for such a harsh sentence was the serious risk of civil unrest at the time, in particular the threat to political stability and public order. The gravity of the punishment was thus attributable to the general context of the applicant's assault on the policeman, not to the harm he had thus inflicted."

**The additional prison time attributable to the mass disorder conviction specifically:**
"his staying at the site of the rally accounted for an additional one year and eleven months to his prison term."

**Failure of domestic courts to specify acts constituting mass disorder separately from the assault:**
"the domestic courts have not specified the applicant's acts attributable to participation in the mass disorder under Article 212 of the Criminal Code, as opposed to the assault on the policeman under Article 318 of the Code. In describing his participation in the mass disorder, they merely referred to his use of violence against a police officer, also classified as a separate criminal offence."

**Absence of pre-existing intention:**
"the criminal file did not suggest any pre-existing intention by the applicant to participate in mass disorder."

**Absence of responsibility for onset of clashes:**
"there is nothing in the case file to suggest that the applicant was among those responsible for the onset of clashes between the protesters and the police."

**Timing and context of the applicant's act:**
"As far as can be discerned from the indictment, he hit the police officer at the height of the clashes, when the police were already arresting protesters."

**Failure of domestic courts to examine circumstances or rebut the applicant's allegations:**
"The domestic courts did not establish the particular circumstances in which that behaviour occurred and dismissed the applicant's arguments about excessive use of force by the police as unfounded while leaving his allegations unrebutted."

**The Court's characterisation of the applicant's conduct:**
"The applicant's conduct therefore, although involving violence, was sporadic in nature and, in the light of the domestic courts' findings, did not attain the degree of aggression which the Court has found in other cases to amount to violence calling for resolute measures, in particular the subsequent sentencing of people to lengthy prison terms."

**Chilling effect:**
"the applicant's criminal conviction, and especially the severity of his sentence, must have had the effect of discouraging him and other opposition supporters, and the public at large, from attending demonstrations and, more generally, from participating in open political debate. The chilling effect of the sanction imposed on him was further amplified by the large-scale proceedings in the case, which attracted widespread media coverage."

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

The Court explicitly declines to resolve this as a separate issue. It does, however, state a factual reason for folding the positive obligation material into the main analysis: "Attribution of responsibility for those acts was therefore a central question in the determination of the applicant's criminal charges. In those circumstances, his complaint about the authorities' role in the occurrence of the disorder is inseparable from that concerning the lack of justification for his criminal liability." No separate factual findings are made on the positive obligation question itself.
