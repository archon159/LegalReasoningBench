This is an important question, because it tests whether the Court's reasoning is actually grounded in specific facts or whether it is purely abstract and doctrinal. Working through each element:

---

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding offered is the following sentence from the opening paragraph:

> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers and/or participants of public assemblies**, namely the **dispersal of these assemblies**, as well as the applicants' **arrest followed by their conviction for an administrative offence**."

This is the sole factual basis from which the Court implies the existence of assemblies, the applicants' participation, and (by proceeding under Article 11) their peaceful character. Notably, this sentence describes what the applicants *complained of*, not what the Court has independently established as fact. The Court does not separately verify or adopt these facts as proven — it simply proceeds on this basis without explicit fact-finding on scope.

---

**(b) Interference (implied)**

The factual grounding is the same sentence as above:

> "the **dispersal of these assemblies**, as well as the applicants' **arrest followed by their conviction for an administrative offence**."

Again, this is drawn from the characterisation of the applicants' complaints, not from an independent factual finding by the Court. The Court treats these acts as constituting the interference without explicitly saying so.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

No factual grounding can be identified, because this element is not discussed at all.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

No factual grounding can be identified, because this element is not discussed at all.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central finding, yet it is strikingly thin in factual grounding. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And:

> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity finding is grounded not in specific facts from the present case, but entirely by reference to prior case-law (*Frumkin*, *Navalnyy and Yashin*, *Kasparov and Others*). No concrete facts about the proportionality of the dispersal, the conduct of authorities, the behaviour of participants, or the reasons given by the government are cited. The Court's reasoning is essentially: the leading cases found a violation in similar circumstances, nothing here distinguishes this case, therefore the same conclusion follows.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

No factual grounding can be identified, because this element is not discussed at all.

---

**Overall observation:** The reasoning section is remarkably sparse in its factual grounding across all elements. The Court relies almost entirely on a brief characterisation of the applicants' complaints and on cross-references to prior judgments, without independently establishing or citing specific facts to support its conclusions on any of the canonical elements.
