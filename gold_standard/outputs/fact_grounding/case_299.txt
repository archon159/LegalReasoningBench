I will work through each element where I found explicit discussion or implication, and identify only facts that the Court itself states or explicitly accepts.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**
The Court does not explicitly state facts establishing that an assembly occurred. It refers to "the demonstrations at issue" and "the demonstrations on 12 January, 26 January and 10 March 2013" as given, but does not articulate factual findings establishing their existence. No quotable factual grounding from the Court itself — the existence of the assemblies is simply assumed.

**ii) Participation**
The Court does not make an explicit factual finding about participation. The closest is the reference to "the applicants' arrests and convictions" in connection with the demonstrations, but this is framed as a legal consequence rather than a factual finding about participation. No quotable factual grounding from the Court itself.

**iii) Peaceful**
The Court accepts the peaceful character of the assemblies. The factual grounding is found in the Court's own language when it refers to "an unauthorised peaceful demonstration" and "unauthorised peaceful assemblies." These are the Court's own characterizations, not merely party submissions:

> "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention."

> "The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly."

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court does not make an explicit, labeled finding of interference, but the following facts are stated by the Court itself and ground the implied finding:

> "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicants' arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies."

This is the Court's own statement of fact and legal consequence, establishing that dispersal, arrest, and conviction occurred. These are the factual acts constituting interference.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court's lawfulness finding is grounded in the following facts that it itself states or accepts:

First, the Court states as fact that no prior notice was given:

> "The Court notes from the outset that the organisers did not give the BCEA prior notice of the demonstrations at issue."

Second, the Court notes the legal framework as an established fact:

> "under Article 5 § IV of the Law on Freedom of Assembly, no prior written notification was required for 'spontaneous assemblies'."

Third, the Court makes its own factual assessment that the applicants failed to substantiate spontaneity:

> "the applicants have failed to sufficiently substantiate their allegations that the demonstrations in which they participated were spontaneous."

Fourth, the Court provides its own factual reasoning for why spontaneity was not established:

> "given that, as submitted by the first applicant, the death of soldiers in the army had already been a widespread problem in the country, it is not clear why it was urgent to hold demonstrations on specific dates – 12 January and 10 March 2013 – without giving five days' written notice. Similarly, it is not clear why a demonstration in solidarity with the Ismayilli protests could not have been organised in conformity with the five-day written-notice requirement."

Note: the Court here partially draws on the first applicant's own submission ("as submitted by the first applicant") but uses it against the applicants' spontaneity claim — it is therefore a fact the Court explicitly engages with and reasons from, not merely a party assertion left unexamined.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court's necessity finding is grounded in the following facts it states itself:

First, the Court finds as a matter of fact that no sufficient justification was provided by the authorities:

> "the authorities in the present cases have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstrations."

Second, the Court states as fact that the authorities failed to recognize the protected status of participation:

> "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention."

Third, the Court states the factual consequences of the measures applied:

> "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicants' arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies."

Fourth, the Court states the broader factual impact:

> "The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly. This deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate."

---

**(d) Legitimate aim and (f) Positive obligation**

As noted in the prior analysis, legitimate aim is not explicitly assessed by the Court as a finding, and positive obligations are not discussed. Accordingly, no factual grounding can be identified for either element — the Court simply does not make factual findings in service of these elements.
