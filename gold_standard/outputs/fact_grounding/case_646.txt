I will go through each element that was found to be either implied or explicitly discussed, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text.

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding available in the text is the following sentence from the opening paragraph:

"The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers and participants of public assemblies**, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies, and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the sole factual basis from which the Court could imply the scope elements (existence of an assembly, participation, peaceful nature). However, it must be noted that this sentence describes the applicants' complaints, not the Court's own findings of fact. The Court does not independently verify or adopt these facts as established — it simply records what the applicants complained of. Therefore, strictly speaking, there is **no factual grounding explicitly adopted by the Court** for the scope elements. The implication is structural/logical rather than grounded in Court-verified facts.

---

**(b) Interference (explicit)**

The factual grounding is again the opening paragraph:

"The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and participants of public assemblies, namely **their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies, and their conviction for administrative offences**."

The same caveat applies: this describes the applicants' complaints. However, the Court subsequently uses the word "interferences" as an established fact ("the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly"), which suggests the Court has accepted these acts — arrest, dispersal, and conviction — as constituting interferences. This is the closest the Court comes to adopting facts as established.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

The Court's necessity finding is notably **not grounded in any case-specific facts** stated in this reasoning section. The Court states:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it... the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And: "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity conclusion is grounded entirely in the Court's prior case-law — specifically Frumkin v. Russia, Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia — rather than in any independently stated facts about the specific circumstances of the present case. The Court references "all the material submitted to it" but does not quote, summarise, or adopt any specific facts from that material. There is therefore **no identifiable specific factual grounding** for the necessity assessment beyond the general description of the measures (arrest, dispersal, conviction) noted under interference above.

---

**Overall observation**

The reasoning section is strikingly thin on explicit factual findings. The Court operates almost entirely by reference to its prior case-law and a general reference to "all the material submitted to it," without setting out the specific facts of the case that ground its conclusions. This is characteristic of a repetitive-case procedure where the Court treats the matter as essentially resolved by its leading judgments.
