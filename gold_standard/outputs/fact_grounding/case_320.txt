I will go through each canonical element that was found to be explicitly discussed or implied, and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists**

The Court does not separately set out a list of facts establishing the existence of an assembly. It simply affirms the fact as undisputed. The factual grounding is contained in the Court's own characterisation: 'the disruption of the applicants' participation in the peaceful march of 17 May 2012, organised to mark the International Day Against Homophobia.' No further independent factual elaboration is provided by the Court in this section beyond this description.

**(ii) Participation**

Again, the Court grounds this in the same undisputed characterisation: 'the march of 17 May 2012, which was organised by the first applicant and attended by the thirteen individual applicants (from the second to the fourteenth).' No additional independent factual findings are made in this section.

**(iii) Peaceful assembly**

The Court grounds the peaceful character of the assembly in its own characterisation: 'the disruption of the applicants' participation in the peaceful march of 17 May 2012' and 'the domestic authorities failed to ensure that the march of 17 May 2012 ... could take place peacefully.' The Court does not separately set out facts to establish peacefulness in this section; it treats it as self-evident and undisputed. It is worth noting that the violence came from counter-demonstrators, not from the applicants themselves, which is implicit in the Court's framing throughout but not separately articulated as a factual finding in this section.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds the interference finding in the following facts, which it treats as undisputed: 'the disruption of the applicants' participation in the peaceful march of 17 May 2012, organised to mark the International Day Against Homophobia, undoubtedly constituted an interference under Article 11 of the Convention.' The Court notes this 'is not even in dispute between the parties.' Beyond this, the Court cross-references its earlier factual findings under Article 3: 'the applicants' complaints that the State failed to protect their freedom to participate in the march of 17 May 2012 from the bias-motivated violence stem from exactly the same factual circumstances as those it has already examined under Article 3 of the Convention taken in conjunction with Article 14 ... Consequently, the Court's findings under the latter provisions are equally pertinent to the examination of the complaints under Articles 11 and 14 of the Convention.' The specific facts underpinning the interference (the attacks by counter-demonstrators and police inaction) are thus incorporated by reference to the Article 3 findings rather than restated here.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Not discussed in the traditional sense. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(f) Positive obligation — existence and breach**

This is the most factually grounded element. The Court relies on the following specific facts:

On the State's advance knowledge and failure to prepare:

'despite the fact that the domestic authorities were given prior notice on 8 May 2012 about the intention to organise a peaceful march on 17 May 2012, they did not manage to use that generous period of nine days for careful preparatory work.'

On the foreseeability of the risk:

'given the attitudes in parts of Georgian society towards the sexual minorities, the authorities knew or should have known of the risk of tensions associated with the applicant organisation's street march to mark the International Day Against Homophobia.'

On the failure to take preventive communicative measures:

'They were thus under an obligation to use any means possible, for instance by making public statements in advance of the demonstration to advocate, without any ambiguity, a tolerant, conciliatory stance ... as well as to warn potential law-breakers of the nature of possible sanctions.'

On the insufficiency of police deployment:

'it was apparent from the outcome of the LGBT procession, that the number of police patrol officers dispatched to the scene of the demonstration was not sufficient, and it would have been only prudent if the domestic authorities, given the likelihood of street clashes, had ensured more police manpower by mobilising, for instance, a squad of anti-riot police.'

On the overall conclusion of breach:

'the domestic authorities failed to ensure that the march of 17 May 2012, which was organised by the first applicant and attended by the thirteen individual applicants (from the second to the fourteenth), could take place peacefully by sufficiently containing homophobic and violent counter-demonstrators. In view of those omissions, the authorities fell short of their positive obligations under Article 11 taken in conjunction with Article 14 of the Convention.'

It should be noted that the Court explicitly incorporates by reference the factual findings made under Article 3: 'the Court's findings under the latter provisions are equally pertinent to the examination of the complaints under Articles 11 and 14 of the Convention.' The detailed factual record underpinning the positive obligation analysis — including the specific nature of the attacks and the specific conduct of the police — is thus grounded in those earlier findings rather than restated in this section.
