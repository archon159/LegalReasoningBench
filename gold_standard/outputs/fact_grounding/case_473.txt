I will work through each canonical element in turn, identifying only the facts the Court itself relied upon (not merely party assertions), quoting verbatim from the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists**

The Court grounds this on the format of the gathering as described by the applicant:

> 'Given the format of the gathering that the applicant has described as a flash mob, the Court considers that it fell within the notion of "peaceful assembly" contained in Article 11.'

The Court does not elaborate further on the specific factual characteristics of the flash mob in this passage, but later in the necessity analysis it describes the gathering in concrete terms:

> 'seven people standing in silence with their mouths sealed with adhesive tape and holding blank sheets of paper'

**(ii) Participation**

The Court grounds this on the applicant's own acknowledged intention and conduct:

> 'The applicant intended to take part in this assembly and never denied it; even if he did not consider it a "public event" or a "static demonstration" subject to notification under the applicable national law, he had been exercising his right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 of the Convention.'

**(iii) Peaceful**

The Court grounds this on the nature of the participants' conduct and the absence of any violence or disruption:

> 'nothing in the actions of the applicant and the other participants in the flash mob could be described as incitement to violence or rejection of democratic principles. They did not do anything capable of causing disorder or disruption to ordinary life. Indeed, seven people standing in silence with their mouths sealed with adhesive tape and holding blank sheets of paper hardly represent a threat to public order.'

And further:

> 'the domestic authorities did not show the requisite degree of tolerance towards their peaceful gathering despite the absence of any risk of insecurity or disturbance'

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds the existence of interference on two concrete governmental acts:

> 'the dispersal of the assembly and the ensuing sanctions constituted "a restriction", within the meaning of the second paragraph of Article 11, and thus an interference with his right to freedom of peaceful assembly as protected by the first paragraph of this Article.'

More specifically, the nature of those sanctions is grounded in the following facts:

> 'the police dispersed the assembly of seven people and a fine was imposed on the applicant following the administrative-offence proceedings on the sole grounds that no prior notification of a static demonstration had been given to the authorities.'

And at the admissibility stage, the Court also notes:

> 'he complained before it about the administrative-offence proceedings against him that had been instituted following his participation, together with six other people, in a flash mob and had resulted in a fine of EUR 22.'

---

**(c) Prescribed by law (implied concerns, not formally decided)**

The Court grounds its observations about the legal framework on the following factual/legal characterisation of the Public Events Act:

> 'the definition of a static demonstration under the Public Events Act... is broad to the extent that a vast array of social situations may fall under it. Any stationary gathering in public – no matter how small and short, irrespective of its purpose or context, and regardless of its potential to cause disruption to ordinary life – of two or more people (solitary static demonstrations being exempt from the prior-notification requirement) holding any object that could be regarded as "a means of visual expression" may be declared unlawful unless a document containing a lengthy list of elements has been submitted to the authorities no later than three days before the gathering.'

And:

> 'the Russian regulatory framework governing public gatherings provides for a broad interpretation of what constitutes a gathering subject to notification and allocates to the authorities excessively wide discretion in imposing restrictions on such gatherings through rigid enforcement.'

No further specific facts are identified by the Court to ground the prescribed-by-law analysis, consistent with its decision not to formally resolve this element.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim (not formally decided)**

The Court does not itself establish any facts grounding a formal finding on legitimate aim. It proceeds on the assumption of the Government's invoked aim for the purposes of the necessity analysis only:

> 'the Court will examine... whether the measures taken against the applicant were proportionate to legitimate aim invoked by the Government, namely "the prevention of disorder"'

No factual grounding for a formal finding can be identified here, consistent with the Court's explicit decision not to resolve this element.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the most extensively factually grounded element. The Court relies on the following facts:

On the nature of the assembly and absence of disorder:

> 'nothing in the actions of the applicant and the other participants in the flash mob could be described as incitement to violence or rejection of democratic principles. They did not do anything capable of causing disorder or disruption to ordinary life. Indeed, seven people standing in silence with their mouths sealed with adhesive tape and holding blank sheets of paper hardly represent a threat to public order.'

> 'the domestic authorities did not show the requisite degree of tolerance towards their peaceful gathering despite the absence of any risk of insecurity or disturbance'

On the sole basis for the sanction:

> 'the police dispersed the assembly of seven people and a fine was imposed on the applicant following the administrative-offence proceedings on the sole grounds that no prior notification of a static demonstration had been given to the authorities.'

On the failure of domestic courts to conduct a proportionality assessment:

> 'When finding the applicant guilty of the administrative offence under Article 20.2 § 2 of the CAO, the justice of the peace and the Presnenskiy District Court did not assess the level of disturbance the assembly had caused, if any. They merely observed that the applicant had failed to comply with the prior-notification requirement in respect of the social situation that, in their view, had doubtlessly amounted to a static demonstration.'

> 'the domestic judicial bodies in the course of the administrative-offence proceedings against the applicant did not seek to strike this balance giving the preponderant weight to the formal unlawfulness of the presumed static demonstration.'

On the criminal nature of the sanction:

> 'the Court has previously held that the offence set out in Article 20.2 § 2 of the CAO should be classified as "criminal", regard being had to the general nature of the offence, and given that the purpose of the sanction is punitive and deterrent in nature, all of which is a characteristic of the criminal sphere... the applicant was subject to sanctions which, although classified as administrative under domestic law, were "criminal" within the autonomous meaning of Article 6 § 1.'

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

As noted previously, this element is not discussed. No factual grounding can be identified because the Court does not engage with it at all.
