**Factual grounding for each element discussed:**

---

**(a) Scope**

The only factual grounding comes from the Court's own characterization in the opening paragraph:

> "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies"

> "organisers or participants of public assemblies"

These phrases establish that assemblies existed and that the applicants participated in them. There is no factual grounding for the peacefulness of the assemblies — the Court implies it by proceeding with the analysis but states no facts to support it.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is the same passage as above, plus:

> "their conviction for administrative offences"

These are the only facts the Court identifies as constituting the interference — arrest, dispersal, and conviction. No further factual detail is provided.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central finding, but it is notably **not grounded in any specific facts** from the case at hand. The Court's reasoning rests entirely on:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject"

The necessity finding is thus grounded in the absence of distinguishing facts and in prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov), not in any affirmative factual findings about the specific circumstances of these assemblies. No concrete facts about the conduct of authorities, the nature of the dispersal, or the proportionality of the sanctions are stated.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.

---

**Overall observation:** The reasoning is strikingly thin in its factual grounding. The Court relies almost entirely on precedent and a generic reference to "all the material submitted," without articulating the specific facts that drive its conclusions on any of the canonical elements. This is characteristic of a repetitive-case judgment where the Court applies an established finding by reference to leading cases rather than conducting a fresh factual analysis.
