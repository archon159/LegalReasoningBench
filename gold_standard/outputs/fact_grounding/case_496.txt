I will go through each element that was found to be either implied or explicitly discussed, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**

The only factual grounding is the following phrase from the opening paragraph:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of **public assemblies**, namely the **dispersal of these assemblies**"

The Court itself uses the word "assemblies" and refers to their dispersal, treating the existence of assemblies as an established fact. However, no further factual detail is provided — no description of where, when, how many people, or what kind of assembly.

**ii) Participation**

The only factual grounding is again from the opening paragraph:

> "as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies"

The Court itself characterizes the applicants in this way, treating their participation (or organisational role) as established. Again, no further factual detail is provided.

**iii) Peaceful character**

There is **no factual grounding** in the reasoning text for the peaceful character of the assemblies. The Court does not mention any facts bearing on this question. The peaceful character is implied only by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis, not by any explicit factual finding.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding for interference is found in the opening paragraph, where the Court describes the measures taken:

> "the **dispersal of these assemblies**, as well as the applicants' **arrest** followed by their **conviction for administrative offence**"

These three concrete governmental acts — dispersal, arrest, and conviction — are the factual basis for the finding of interference. The Court itself presents these as the measures complained of, treating them as established facts.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is where the absence of factual grounding is most striking. The Court's conclusion that the interference was not necessary in a democratic society is grounded not in any specific facts from the instant case, but exclusively in a reference to prior case-law:

> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014 and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of **issues similar to those in the present case**."

And:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found **any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion**"

The Court thus acknowledges that it examined the material but found nothing to distinguish the present case from the leading cases. The necessity finding is grounded in the similarity to prior cases, not in any independently stated facts about what happened in the instant case. **No specific facts from the present case are quoted or described** as the basis for the necessity assessment.

---

**Summary observation**

Across all elements, the factual grounding in this reasoning text is extremely thin. The Court operates almost entirely by reference to prior case-law and by brief, generic characterizations of the applicants and the measures taken. No facts about the circumstances of the assemblies, the reasons given by authorities for dispersal, the conduct of participants, or the nature of the convictions are spelled out in the reasoning section provided.
