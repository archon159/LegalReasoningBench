**Factual grounding for each element discussed:**

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding is the following description of the complaints:

"the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences."

This single sentence is the entirety of the factual grounding for all three scope sub-elements (existence of assembly, participation, peacefulness). The Court does not provide any further factual detail. Notably, there is no explicit factual finding on peacefulness — it is simply assumed by the Court proceeding to analyze the case under Article 11 without exclusion.

**(b) Interference (implied)**

The factual grounding is the same passage as for scope:

"the applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences."

The Court does not make any independent factual findings on interference beyond this description. The interference is treated as self-evident from the described measures (dispersal, arrest, conviction).

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

The Court makes no independent factual findings to ground its necessity assessment. It relies entirely on its prior case-law:

"In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia (no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts)), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia (no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014) and Kasparov and Others v. Russia (no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013), the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

And: "Having examined all the material submitted to it... the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

There are no case-specific facts quoted or adopted by the Court to support the necessity finding. The reasoning is entirely comparative and jurisprudential.

**(c) Prescribed by law and (d) Legitimate aim and (f) Positive obligation**

As noted in the prior analysis, these elements are not discussed. Consistently with this, no facts are identified or adopted by the Court in relation to them. Nothing to quote.
