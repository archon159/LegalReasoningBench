I will go through each element that was found to be explicitly discussed or implied, and identify the factual grounding the Court relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists:**
The Court grounds this finding by reference to its prior finding in *Navalnyy v. Russia*, without restating the underlying facts: 'The Court refers to its finding that the gathering in question fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Navalnyy v. Russia [GC], nos. 29580/12 and 4 others, §§ 110-12, 15 November 2018).'

No additional facts are stated in this reasoning section to ground the finding. The Court simply imports the earlier conclusion by reference.

**ii) Participation:**
The Court grounds this in the following facts: 'the applicants' intentions and conduct were also covered by the notion of peaceful assembly, including those of Ms Polyakova who claimed that she was acting as an observer for a group of human rights defender organisations.'

Note that Ms Polyakova's role is drawn from her own claim. The Court does not explicitly state that it verified or accepted this claim as established fact — it says she 'claimed' this — but the Court nonetheless includes her within the scope of Article 11 protection, implicitly accepting the claim as sufficient for this purpose.

**iii) Peaceful:**
The Court does not state any specific facts to ground the finding that the assembly was peaceful. It simply characterises the applicants as 'peaceful participants' and their assembly as a 'peaceful public assembly,' but no concrete factual detail (e.g., absence of violence, nature of conduct) is provided in this reasoning section to support that characterisation.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds the interference finding in the following explicitly stated facts: 'the applicants' arrests, transfer to the police station and the ensuing sanctions constituted an interference with the right to freedom of peaceful assembly under Article 11 § 2.'

The concrete acts identified as constituting interference are therefore:
- arrest of the applicants
- transfer to the police station
- the ensuing sanctions (i.e., the administrative convictions)

Additionally, the Court states the reason for these measures, which it accepts as established: 'the applicants were arrested, transferred to the police station and charged with administrative offences for the sole reason that their gathering as such had not been authorised.' This is the Court's own characterisation, not merely a party submission, and it grounds the interference finding in the absence of authorisation as the operative cause.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court's necessity assessment is grounded in the following facts:

- The measures applied were: arrest, transfer to the police station, and administrative sanctions — as stated earlier in the reasoning.
- The applicants were 'peaceful participants in the public assembly' — the Court uses this characterisation as a factual premise for its necessity assessment.
- The sole reason for the measures was that 'their gathering as such had not been authorised' — the Court treats this as an established fact and it is central to the necessity finding, as it implies no other conduct justified the intervention.

Beyond these facts, the Court does not engage in detailed factual analysis. It relies primarily on the pattern established in leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others) and states: 'Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion.' This is a negative factual statement — the absence of any distinguishing facts — rather than a positive factual grounding.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law and (d) Legitimate aim**

As noted in the prior analysis, these elements are not discussed. Accordingly, no factual grounding can be identified for them in this reasoning section.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed; no factual grounding to identify.
