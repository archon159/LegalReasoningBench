This is an important question, and the honest answer is that **the reasoning text is almost entirely devoid of specific factual findings**. The Court does not narrate or assess concrete facts about what happened at the assemblies, what the authorities did, or why. Instead, it reasons at a high level of abstraction, relying on references to prior case-law and a general characterisation of the complaints. Below is a careful account of what can and cannot be found.

---

**(a) Scope — Assembly, Participation, Peaceful Character**

The only factual grounding in the text is the following description of the applicants and the events:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence."

This single sentence is the entirety of the factual basis for the scope elements. It identifies the applicants as "organisers and participants," and refers to "public assemblies" that were "dispersed." No further facts are provided. The peaceful character of the assemblies is not grounded in any stated fact in this text.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is the same passage quoted above:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence."

The Court identifies three concrete acts as constituting the interference: dispersal of the assemblies, arrest of the applicants, and conviction for an administrative offence. These are the only facts stated. No further detail is provided about how, when, or under what circumstances these acts occurred.

---

**(e) Necessity in a Democratic Society**

This is the element most explicitly discussed, yet it is the least factually grounded in the text. The Court's conclusion rests not on specific facts but on:

1. A reference to prior leading cases:
> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014 and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

2. A general statement that no new material altered the conclusion:
> "Having examined all the material submitted to it... the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

3. The conclusion itself:
> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

**No specific facts about the proportionality of the measures — such as the conduct of the authorities, the behaviour of the applicants, the scale of the assemblies, or the reasons given by domestic courts — are cited or assessed in this text.** The necessity finding is grounded entirely in the analogy to prior cases and the absence of any distinguishing material.

---

**Overall observation**

This reasoning text is characteristic of a repetitive-case or clone-case judgment, where the Court dispenses with detailed factual analysis because the situation is treated as legally identical to already-decided leading cases. The factual record underlying the decision almost certainly exists in the case file and may be described elsewhere in the judgment (e.g., in a "Facts" section not reproduced here), but **within this reasoning section, the Court provides no independent factual assessment**. Any mapping of facts to legal elements beyond what is quoted above would be speculation.
