I will go through each canonical element where I found explicit discussion or implication, and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**
The Court does not explicitly discuss this, but treats it as given. The factual grounding is implicit in references such as "the demonstration of 31 July 2010" and "the demonstration of 10 March 2013." No specific facts are quoted by the Court to establish this element — it is simply assumed.

**ii) Participation**
The Court grounds this in the following:
- "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention"

This is stated twice — once for each case — but it is a legal conclusion rather than a factual finding. The Court does not quote specific facts establishing the applicant's presence at the demonstrations. The factual grounding is implied by the references to "his arrest and conviction following his participation in the demonstration of 31 July 2010" and "his arrest and conviction following his participation in the demonstration of 10 March 2013." These are the closest the Court comes to factual grounding for participation, but they are framing statements rather than findings of fact.

**iii) Peaceful**
The Court grounds this in the following explicitly adopted facts:
- First case: "a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner"
- Second case: "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention"

In the first case, the Court explicitly adopts the peaceful character as a factual finding. In the second case, the characterization "peaceful" is used but without a separate factual finding — it appears to be carried over from the Gafgaz Mammadov analysis. No specific facts (e.g., absence of violence, conduct of participants) are quoted to ground this characterization.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds interference in the following facts it explicitly relies upon:
- "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies."
- "The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of the sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly had a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly."

These are the facts the Court itself identifies as constituting and demonstrating interference. The specific acts — dispersal, arrest, and conviction — are treated as established facts throughout the reasoning.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

**i) Foreseeability (first case — concerns found)**
The Court grounds its concerns in the following, by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov:
- "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed"
- "the doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely Article 310.1 of the CAO, relied on by the authorities to arrest and convict the participant of an unauthorised demonstration"

No specific legislative text or concrete facts about how the law operated are quoted directly in this reasoning section. The Court relies on its prior findings in Gafgaz Mammadov rather than independently establishing facts here.

**i) Foreseeability (second case — accepted as lawful)**
The Court grounds its acceptance of lawfulness in the following:
- "the organisers did not submit proper prior notice to the BCEA of the demonstration of 10 March 2013"
- "in accordance with Article 5 § IV of the Law on Freedom of Assembly no prior written notification was required for 'spontaneous assemblies'"
- "the applicant failed to sufficiently substantiate his allegation that the demonstration in which he had participated had been spontaneous"

These are the facts the Court itself establishes and relies upon to conclude: "the Court is ready to accept that the dispersal of the demonstration of 10 March 2013 was lawful."

**ii) Accessibility**
No factual grounding identified. Not discussed.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse**
The Court grounds this in:
- "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed"

Again, this is by reference to Gafgaz Mammadov, without independent factual elaboration in this reasoning section.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not itself adopt any facts to establish a legitimate aim. The Government's submissions mention "protection of public safety and the prevention of disorder or crime," but the Court does not explicitly accept these. No factual grounding can be identified for this element in the Court's own reasoning.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the most factually grounded element. The Court relies on the following facts it explicitly adopts:

- "a failure by the authorities to take into consideration the fact that the demonstration had been notified" (first case)
- "a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner" (first case, and by reference for second case)
- "a lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention" (both cases)
- "the authorities in the present case did not adduce relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration" (second case)
- "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention" (second case)
- "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging him from participating in political rallies."
- "The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of the sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly had a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly. The chilling effect deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate."

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. No factual grounding to identify.
