I will go through each canonical element and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon, being careful to distinguish between facts the Court adopted and mere party assertions.

---

**(a) Scope**

**(i) Assembly exists**
The Court does not explicitly state facts grounding this element. The existence of demonstrations is treated as a given throughout. The closest the Court comes is its own reference to 'the dispersal of the demonstrations by the police' (in the framing of the complaints) and 'the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner.' These are the Court's own words, not merely party submissions, and they presuppose the existence of assemblies. No discrete factual finding is made.

**(ii) Participation**
The Court's own language grounds this implicitly: 'the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention' — this is the Court's own characterisation, adopted from the Gafgaz Mammadov analysis, and it presupposes that the applicants participated. No discrete factual finding is made beyond this.

**(iii) Peaceful**
The Court grounds this in its own adopted finding:
'a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner.'

This is the Court's own language, drawn from its analysis in Gafgaz Mammadov and applied to the present cases. It constitutes a factual finding that the demonstration was peaceful.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds interference in the following facts, stated in its own framing of the complaints:
'the dispersal of the demonstrations by the police and their arrest and conviction for an administrative offence.'

These three acts — dispersal, arrest, and conviction — are treated by the Court as established facts throughout. The Court also refers to 'doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely Article 310.1 of the CAO, relied on by the authorities to arrest and convict the participant of an unauthorised demonstration,' which confirms the Court's own acceptance that arrest and conviction under that provision occurred.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

**(Foreseeability and safeguards)**
The Court grounds its concerns in the following, stated as its own finding:
'the Court noted the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed.'

However, it is important to note that this is the Court referring back to its own findings in Gafgaz Mammadov, which it applies to the present cases. The Court does not identify specific legislative provisions or specific facts in the present cases that ground this finding — it relies entirely on the prior case analysis. No additional factual grounding specific to the present cases is provided.

**(Accessibility)**
No factual grounding. Not discussed.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

No factual grounding from the Court itself. The Government asserted that the dispersal 'had pursued the aim of protecting public safety and preventing disorder or crime,' but the Court does not explicitly adopt this. The Court proceeds without making a formal finding on legitimate aim, so no facts are identified by the Court as grounding this element.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court grounds its finding on four factual points, all stated as the Court's own adopted findings (drawn from Gafgaz Mammadov and applied here):

1. **Notification ignored:** 'a failure by the authorities to take into consideration the fact that the demonstration had been notified.'

2. **No sufficient reasons for dispersal:** 'a lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner.'

3. **Dubious formal ground for arrest and conviction:** 'doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely Article 310.1 of the CAO, relied on by the authorities to arrest and convict the participant of an unauthorised demonstration.'

4. **No acknowledgment of Article 11 protection:** 'a lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention.'

These four points are the Court's own findings, explicitly adopted and applied to the present cases.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

No factual grounding. The Court does not discuss positive obligations at all.
