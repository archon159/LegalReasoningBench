I will go through each canonical element and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**

The Court does not explicitly analyze this element but grounds its implicit acceptance in the following facts:

- 'it was part of a series of protests against Parliament's failure to approve electoral reform as previously planned'
- 'the demonstration itself was peaceful, with a great number of people participating'

**ii) Participation**

The Court grounds this in:

- 'By participating in the demonstration in question the applicant wished to express his disapproval of what he considered to be the authorities' failure to enhance the country's democratic process.'
- 'the primary reason for the applicant's attendance at the demonstration was to protest against Parliament's failure to adopt important legislative reforms'
- 'the applicant was able to express his protest without any impediment, even as he had apparently blocked the road in that process, until the moment he threw beans at the police officers, telling them it had been "gruel for slaves"'

**iii) Peaceful assembly**

The Court grounds this in:

- 'the demonstration itself was peaceful, with a great number of people participating'
- 'His actions were neither violent nor did they cause any injuries to the police officers and could hardly be aimed at causing physical harm to them'
- 'the conduct in question did not result in any escalation of the circumstances on the ground'
- 'his conduct was not limited to verbal expression and involved physically throwing objects – in this case dried beans – at the police officers' — notably, the Court accepts this fact but then qualifies it by noting that 'throwing these objects was not aimed at endangering the targets,' thereby maintaining the characterization of the conduct as non-violent

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds this in:

- 'the interference at issue resulted from the applicant's arrest at a demonstration and his subsequent conviction of administrative offences leading to a sanction of eight days' detention'

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court grounds this in a single legal reference:

- 'The interference in question was "prescribed by law" as it was based on Article 173 of the CAO'

No further factual grounding is provided. The Court does not identify any facts relating to foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards.

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court accepts the legitimate aim without detailed factual grounding. It states:

- 'The Court further accepts that, in the circumstances of the present case, the interference pursued the legitimate aim of preventing disorder and protecting the rights of others.'

The factual basis for 'preventing disorder' can be inferred from the Court's earlier description of the conduct:

- 'his conduct was not limited to verbal expression and involved physically throwing objects – in this case dried beans – at the police officers'
- 'the contested conduct took place in public, in front of a group of bystanders, while the officers were carrying out their duties'
- 'the applicant's disobedience in respect of the order to move away from the road at the demonstration site'

However, the Court does not explicitly link these facts to the legitimate aim finding — it simply accepts the aim. These facts are therefore only implicitly connected to the legitimate aim element.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This element has the richest factual grounding. The Court draws on the following facts:

**On the public interest nature of the demonstration and the weight of the right:**

- 'it was part of a series of protests against Parliament's failure to approve electoral reform as previously planned'
- 'By participating in the demonstration in question the applicant wished to express his disapproval of what he considered to be the authorities' failure to enhance the country's democratic process. This was a matter of public interest and contributed to the ongoing debate in society.'
- 'the primary reason for the applicant's attendance at the demonstration was to protest against Parliament's failure to adopt important legislative reforms'
- 'the demonstration itself was peaceful, with a great number of people participating'

**On the nature of the applicant's conduct:**

- 'the applicant was able to express his protest without any impediment, even as he had apparently blocked the road in that process, until the moment he threw beans at the police officers, telling them it had been "gruel for slaves"'
- 'his conduct was not limited to verbal expression and involved physically throwing objects – in this case dried beans – at the police officers'
- 'throwing these objects was not aimed at endangering the targets'
- 'His actions were neither violent nor did they cause any injuries to the police officers and could hardly be aimed at causing physical harm to them'
- 'the conduct in question did not result in any escalation of the circumstances on the ground'
- 'the contested conduct took place in public, in front of a group of bystanders, while the officers were carrying out their duties'

**On the expressive character of the conduct:**

- 'His conduct appears to have implied that the officers were "slaves"'
- 'the applicant – a politician – was seemingly conveying his opinion that the police officers had supported the ruling party which had been at the source of the failed reform'

**On the severity of the sanction:**

- 'the custodial sanction in the present case was being applied in the context of the exercise of a fundamental freedom'
- 'the sanction of eight days' administrative detention principally concerned the expressive conduct directed against the police officers'

**On the inadequacy of the domestic courts' reasoning:**

- 'none of the elements identified above were addressed as part of the domestic courts' reasoning regarding their decision to impose the custodial sanction'
- 'the grounds cited in the trial court's judgment – the applicant's "personality" and the "seriousness" of the conduct attributed to him – were sufficient, without further elaboration, to render a sanction of eight days' administrative detention proportionate' [found insufficient]
- 'while the reference to the applicant's "personality" may have concerned his history of administrative-offence convictions and the related argument made before the trial court, it does not appear that the conditions provided for by law for treating such considerations as an aggravating factor had been met as the applicable legal provisions did not allow taking into account previous administrative sanctions older than one year'
- 'As regards the "seriousness" of the applicant's conduct, this appears to refer to the necessity of punishment in general rather than the proportionality of the chosen measure and cannot therefore be considered sufficient to justify the imposition of a custodial term, however short, in the context of the applicant's exercise of his rights to freedom of expression and assembly'

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

As noted previously, the Court does not discuss positive obligations. There is no factual grounding to identify for this element.
