The factual grounding for interference is:

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the central finding, yet the factual grounding is remarkably thin. The Court states:
> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has **not found any fact or argument** capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This is a negative formulation — the Court is saying no new facts distinguished this case from the leading cases. The Court does not itself identify or quote any specific facts from the present case to ground its necessity assessment. Instead, it relies entirely on the similarity to prior cases:
> "In the leading cases of **Frumkin v. Russia**, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), **Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia**, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014 and **Kasparov and Others v. In short: the factual grounding for the necessity assessment is not drawn from the facts of the present case as described in this reasoning section, but is instead delegated by reference to the pattern established in the cited leading cases.

**Summary observation**

Across all elements, the factual grounding in this reasoning section is extremely sparse. The Court operates almost entirely through reference to prior case-law rather than through fact-specific reasoning. The only concrete facts cited anywhere in the section are (1) the existence of public assemblies, (2) the applicants' roles as organisers and participants, and (3) their arrest and conviction for administrative offences.
