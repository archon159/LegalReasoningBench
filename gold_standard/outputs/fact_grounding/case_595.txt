# Factual Grounding for Each Canonical Element

---

## (a) Scope

### (i) Has there been an assembly?
The Court grounds this on a prior finding, not on facts recited in this judgment. It states:
> 'The Court has previously held that the assembly at Bolotnaya Square on 6 May 2012 fell within the scope of Article 11 of the Convention (see Yaroslav Belousov, cited above, §§ 168-71).'

No independent factual grounding is provided in this judgment; the Court relies entirely on precedent.

### (ii) Did the applicant participate in the assembly?
No facts are explicitly stated and adopted by the Court on this point. Participation is treated as a given throughout. The closest factual grounding is implicit in the Court's description of the charges against the applicant:
> 'he was found guilty of two criminal offences, namely participation in acts of mass disorder (Article 212 of the Criminal Code) and committing violent acts against a police officer (Article 318 of the Criminal Code).'

This establishes his presence at the assembly by implication, but the Court does not explicitly adopt a factual finding of participation as such.

### (iii) Was the assembly peaceful (as regards the applicant)?
The Court grounds this on the following fact, which it treats as established from the case record:
> 'it does not transpire from the description of his offences that he was among those responsible for the initial acts of aggression which contributed to the deterioration of the assembly's initial peaceful character.'

And further, in the necessity analysis, the Court reinforces this with:
> 'there is nothing in the case file to suggest that the applicant was among those responsible for the onset of clashes between the protesters and the police.'
> 'As far as can be discerned from the indictment, the applicant threw pieces of tarmac at the height of the clashes, when the police were already arresting protesters.'

---

## (b) Interference
The Court grounds the finding of interference directly on the following fact:
> 'the applicant's prosecution and criminal conviction for participation in mass disorder constituted an interference with the exercise of the freedom of assembly.'

The underlying factual basis is the conviction itself:
> 'he was found guilty of two criminal offences, namely participation in acts of mass disorder (Article 212 of the Criminal Code) and committing violent acts against a police officer (Article 318 of the Criminal Code). He was sentenced to three years and six months' imprisonment under those provisions.'

---

## (c) Prescribed by law
The Court's finding that the interference was lawful is stated by reference to the Yaroslav Belousov precedent, without independent factual grounding in this judgment:
> 'The Court sees no reason to reach a different conclusion in this case (ibid., §§ 174-75). It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution was lawful.'

On the sub-element of safeguards, the Court grounds its finding on the following facts:
> 'the first-instance court did not verify the applicant's account of events. In particular, it rejected his statement that he had not thrown pieces of tarmac or inflicted K.'s injury as unsubstantiated, while refusing to question a defence witness, Officer Sh., who could have confirmed the applicant's statements. The appellate court did not address those drawbacks, despite the applicant's complaint.'

---

## (d) Legitimate aim
The Court states the legitimate aims but provides no independent factual grounding for why those aims were engaged. It simply asserts:
> 'It finds that the applicant's pre-trial detention and prosecution was lawful, and pursued the legitimate aims of preventing disorder and crime and the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.'

The only factual context offered is the Government's stated justification, which the Court notes but ultimately finds insufficient:
> 'The main justification advanced by the Government for such a harsh sentence was the serious risk of civil unrest existing at the time, in particular that there was a threat to political stability and public order.'

The Court does not explicitly adopt this as an established fact; it records it as the Government's submission and then scrutinises it. No factual grounding is independently established by the Court for this element beyond the existence of the prosecution itself.

---

## (e) Necessity in a democratic society
This is the most extensively factually grounded element. The Court draws on the following established facts:

**On the nature of the applicant's conduct:**
> 'he was found guilty of two criminal offences, namely participation in acts of mass disorder (Article 212 of the Criminal Code) and committing violent acts against a police officer (Article 318 of the Criminal Code). He was sentenced to three years and six months' imprisonment under those provisions for, throwing pieces of tarmac at the police, one of which hit a police officer on the hand, breaking his finger, which did not endanger the latter's life or health, and chanting anti-government slogans while attending an authorised public assembly.'

**On the absence of pre-existing intent:**
> 'the criminal file did not suggest any pre-existing intention by the applicant to participate in such acts.'
> 'there is nothing in the case file to suggest that the applicant was among those responsible for the onset of clashes between the protesters and the police.'

**On the timing and nature of the applicant's involvement:**
> 'As far as can be discerned from the indictment, the applicant threw pieces of tarmac at the height of the clashes, when the police were already arresting protesters.'
> 'The overall conduct imputed to the applicant, although involving violence, was sporadic in nature and did not attain the degree of aggression which the Court found in other cases to amount to violence calling for resolute measures.'

**On the breakdown of the sentence and the penalisation of protected expression:**
> 'causing injury to Police Officer K. entailed a sentence of one year and four months. Regard being had to the other acts imputed to the applicant, it transpires that his staying at the site of the rally and chanting anti-government slogans accounted for an additional two years and two months to his prison term under Article 212 of the Criminal Code.'
> 'Those acts, unlike throwing pieces of tarmac (already imputed under Article 318 of the Code), were peaceful and constituted an act of protest, a form of expression protected by Article 10 of the Convention.'
> 'they penalised the applicant for the political message he had intended to express by his presence at the venue and his chanting of non-violent political slogans.'

**On the lack of procedural safeguards:**
> 'the first-instance court did not verify the applicant's account of events. In particular, it rejected his statement that he had not thrown pieces of tarmac or inflicted K.'s injury as unsubstantiated, while refusing to question a defence witness, Officer Sh., who could have confirmed the applicant's statements. The appellate court did not address those drawbacks, despite the applicant's complaint.'

**On the Government's justification and its relation to the applicant personally:**
> 'The main justification advanced by the Government for such a harsh sentence was the serious risk of civil unrest existing at the time, in particular that there was a threat to political stability and public order. The gravity of the punishment was thus attributable to the general context of the applicant's assault on the policeman, not to the harm he thus inflicted.'
> 'the Court does not consider that the risks referred to by the Government – potential civil unrest, political instability and a threat to public order – had any personal relation to him.'

**On the chilling effect:**
> 'the applicant's criminal conviction, and especially the severity of his sentence, must have had the effect of discouraging him and other opposition supporters, as well as the public at large, from attending demonstrations and, more generally, from participating in open political debate. The chilling effect of the sanction imposed on him was further amplified by the large-scale proceedings in the case, which attracted widespread media coverage.'

---

## (f) Positive obligation
The Court explicitly declines to make a separate factual assessment on this element:
> 'the Court is not required to assess the authorities' alleged failure to discharge their positive obligation in respect of the conduct of the assembly at Bolotnaya Square as a separate issue under Article 11 of the Convention.'

No factual grounding is established for a positive obligation finding, as the Court folds the relevant material into the negative obligation analysis without reaching a separate conclusion. Accordingly, no factual mapping can be made for this element.
