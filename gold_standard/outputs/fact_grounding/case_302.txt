**(a) Scope — Assembly exists**

The Court does not itself make a factual finding about the existence of an assembly. The only textual basis is the framing of the complaint, which the Court reproduces without explicitly adopting as established fact:

> 'The applicants complained that their arrest and conviction had been measures used by the authorities to punish them for participating in demonstrations and/or to prevent them from doing so.'

And in the merits section, the Court refers to 'political rallies' and 'demonstrations' without grounding these references in explicit factual findings of its own:

> 'The applicants' arrest and the administrative proceedings against them could only have had the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies.'

The Court does not explicitly adopt any specific facts establishing that assemblies took place. The factual grounding is thin — the existence of assemblies is treated as given, but no facts are quoted or found by the Court itself in the reproduced text.

**(a) Scope — Peaceful**

No factual grounding whatsoever. The Court makes no reference to any facts bearing on whether the assemblies were peaceful. This element is implied purely by the absence of any contrary discussion.

**(b) Interference**

The Court explicitly grounds its finding of interference in the following facts, which it states itself:

> 'The applicants' arrest and the administrative proceedings against them could only have had the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies.'

And:

> 'Those measures undoubtedly have a chilling effect, which deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate.'

The factual grounding is therefore: **(i)** the arrest of the applicants, and **(ii)** the administrative proceedings brought against them. These are the two concrete acts the Court identifies as constituting the interference. The Court does not elaborate further on the specific circumstances of the arrests or the content of the proceedings in the reproduced text, but treats these facts as established.

Additionally, in the admissibility section, the Court implicitly confirms one factual element relevant to interference — that the arrest and conviction had a purpose connected to assembly participation:

> 'The documents included in the case file indicate that in his written appeal the applicant concerned complained that the real purpose of his arrest and conviction had been to prevent him from participating in the demonstration.'

The Court does not explicitly adopt this as an established fact, but it does accept it as sufficient to rebut the Government's admissibility objection, lending it some factual weight.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (implied)**

The Court grounds its implied finding of lack of necessity in the same facts as interference:

> 'The applicants' arrest and the administrative proceedings against them could only have had the effect of discouraging them from participating in political rallies. Those measures undoubtedly have a chilling effect, which deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate.'

The factual grounding is therefore the same two acts — arrest and administrative proceedings — and their assessed effect: discouragement of participation in political rallies and a broader chilling effect on public political debate. No additional facts are cited in the reproduced text to support this assessment.
