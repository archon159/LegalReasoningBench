## Factual Grounding for Each Canonical Element

---

### (a) Scope

**i) Assembly exists**

The Court does not explicitly discuss this element, but the factual grounding is implicit in its description of what was planned and what occurred. The Court states:

> "it is not disputed that the police carried out a number of checks on vehicles transporting participants to the rally"

> "the Regional Governor banned both the rally that UMO Ilinden – PIRIN intended to hold at Yane Sandanski's grave near the Rozhen Monastery and the one it wanted to stage in front of Yane Sandanski's monument in Melnik"

**ii) Participation**

The Court grounds this in the following finding:

> "the police ordered the members of UMO Ilinden – PIRIN's managing board and the first and second applicants to refrain from organising or taking part in the two rallies"

**iii) Peaceful**

No factual grounding can be identified. The Court does not discuss or make any finding on whether the assembly was peaceful. There is no explicit statement from the Court establishing this fact.

---

### (b) Interference

The Court grounds its interference finding on several distinct facts that it itself establishes or accepts as undisputed:

On the ban and police orders:

> "the Regional Governor banned both the rally that UMO Ilinden – PIRIN intended to hold at Yane Sandanski's grave near the Rozhen Monastery and the one it wanted to stage in front of Yane Sandanski's monument in Melnik"

> "the police ordered the members of UMO Ilinden – PIRIN's managing board and the first and second applicants to refrain from organising or taking part in the two rallies"

> "Those measures clearly indicate an intention to prevent the rally. They had a chilling effect on the individuals concerned and on the other participants in the rallies, and thus amounted in themselves to interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly"

On the vehicle checks, the Court accepts the applicants' assertion as undisputed by the Government:

> "it is not disputed that the police carried out a number of checks on vehicles transporting participants to the rally"

> "the Government did not dispute the applicants' assertion that they had taken an unusually long time and had thus delayed the rally"

On the road block, the Court accepts this as conceded by the Government:

> "The Government also conceded that, as alleged by the applicants, the police had blocked the road to the Rozhen Monastery and had thus prevented the participants in the rally from approaching their chosen site"

The Court then ties these facts to the interference finding:

> "Bearing in mind that the time and the place of the event were crucial to the participants, the Court finds that those actions also amounted to interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly"

---

### (c) Prescribed by law

The Court grounds its misgivings on four distinct factual/legal observations:

**First (no legal role for regional governors):**

> "the 1990 Meetings and Marches Act envisages no role for regional governors in the policing of rallies"

**Second (irrelevant grounds relied upon):**

> "in banning the rally the Regional Governor relied on some clearly irrelevant grounds, such as UMO Ilinden – PIRIN's lack of registration"

**Third (vague statutory basis for police orders):**

> "section 55(1) of the 2006 Ministry of Internal Affairs Act, which served as a basis for the orders issued by the police, is formulated in very general terms and gives no indication of the circumstances in which the police may use the power conferred on them"

**Fourth (no legal basis for road block):**

> "no legal basis has been cited for the police block of the road to the Rozhen Monastery"

However, the Court explicitly declines to reach a conclusion on this element:

> "the Court does not consider it necessary to determine whether the interference was 'prescribed by law' or pursued a legitimate aim, as it finds, for the reasons set out in the following paragraph, that, even if it did, it cannot be regarded as being 'necessary in a democratic society'"

---

### (d) Legitimate aim

No factual grounding can be identified for a positive finding. The Court explicitly declines to determine whether a legitimate aim was pursued. The grounds stated by the Regional Governor are mentioned in the necessity analysis but not assessed as legitimate aims:

> "Those grounds were the organisation's unregistered status, a conjectural threat to public order, the holding of meetings of other organisations at the same time and place, and lastly, and most importantly, 'propaganda against the country's sovereignty and territorial integrity'"

These are quoted in the context of the necessity analysis, not as the Court's acceptance of a legitimate aim.

---

### (e) Necessity in a democratic society

The Court grounds its finding of lack of necessity on two related facts:

**First, the repetition of previously condemned grounds:**

> "The Regional Governor's decision, which apparently prompted all of the ensuing actions of the police, relied on exactly the same grounds as those given to ban previous rallies of UMO Ilinden or UMO Ilinden – PIRIN, in respect of which the Court has, on no less than three occasions, found violations of Article 11"

> "Those grounds were the organisation's unregistered status, a conjectural threat to public order, the holding of meetings of other organisations at the same time and place, and lastly, and most importantly, 'propaganda against the country's sovereignty and territorial integrity'"

**Second, the timing of the Governor's decision relative to prior ECtHR judgments:**

> "The fact that the Regional Governor should rely on grounds which the Court had, at the time when the Governor made his decision, already found deficient is indicative of a troubling disregard for the Court's judgments and the applicants' right to freedom of peaceful assembly"

---

### (f) Positive obligation

No factual grounding can be identified for a positive obligation finding in the merits analysis. The Court does not make a positive obligation finding on the merits. The only passage touching on positive obligations in the Court's own voice appears in the admissibility section, in the context of assessing the effectiveness of a damages remedy, not as a merits finding:

> "if States were able to confine their response to such incidents to the mere payment of compensation, without putting in place effective procedures ensuring the possibility of staging such rallies, it would be possible in some cases for the authorities arbitrarily to deprive groups of individuals and organisations of their right to freedom of peaceful assembly"

This is a general principle stated in the admissibility context, not a factually grounded merits conclusion on positive obligations.
