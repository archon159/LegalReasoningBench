**Factual grounding for each element discussed:**

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding for the implied scope findings comes from the Court's own characterisation in the opening paragraph:

"The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers or participants of public assemblies**, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the sole factual basis from which the Court implicitly accepts that assemblies took place and that the applicants participated. There is no explicit factual finding on peacefulness — the Court does not quote or adopt any specific facts establishing this. The peacefulness of the assemblies is implied only structurally (by the Court proceeding under Article 11 at all), but is not grounded in any explicitly stated or adopted facts in this passage.

**(b) Interference (explicit)**

The factual grounding is the same passage:

"their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

These are the specific governmental acts the Court treats as constituting the interference. No further factual detail is provided or adopted.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

This is where the absence of factual grounding is most notable. The Court states:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This is a negative factual finding — the Court does not identify or adopt any specific facts of its own. Instead, it grounds its necessity assessment entirely by reference to prior case-law:

"In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

The necessity finding is thus grounded in legal precedent rather than in any specific facts from the present case that the Court explicitly adopted.

**(c), (d), (f)**

As noted in the prior analysis, these elements are not discussed. Consequently, no factual grounding can be identified for them, and none is forced here.
