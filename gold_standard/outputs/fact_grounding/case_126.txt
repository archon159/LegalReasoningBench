I will go through each element where the Court made a finding (explicit or implied), and identify the factual grounding for each.

---

### (a) Scope

**(i) Assembly exists / (ii) Applicant participated**

The Court grounds both in the same factual statement:

> "The Court notes that the applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Georgios church in Nicosia. The demonstration was dispersed and some of the demonstrators, including the applicant, were arrested."

**(iii) Peaceful (implied: within scope despite violence)**

The Court does not make an explicit finding that the assembly was peaceful. Instead, it adopts the Commission's factual findings about the violent character:

> "The Court notes that in the case of Chrysostomos and Papachrysostomou, the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order on the demarcation line in Cyprus."

And from the UN Secretary General's report, which the Court explicitly adopts:

> "the UN Secretary General stated that the demonstrators had 'forced their way into the UN buffer zone in the Ayios Kassianos area of Nicosia', that they had broken 'through a wire barrier maintained by UNFICYP and destroyed an UNFICYP observation post' before breaking 'through the line formed by UNFICYP soldiers' and entering 'a former school complex'."

Despite these facts, the Court proceeds to analyze interference and justification without excluding the assembly from scope. There is no affirmative factual statement grounding the implicit conclusion that the assembly remains within scope — the Court simply proceeds without explanation.

---

### (b) Interference

Grounded in the same facts as scope (i) and (ii):

> "The Court notes that the applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Georgios church in Nicosia. The demonstration was dispersed and some of the demonstrators, including the applicant, were arrested."

---

### (c) Prescribed by law

Grounded in the identification of specific legal provisions:

> "This interference had a legal basis, namely sections 70 and 71 of the Cypriot Criminal Code (see paragraphs 29-30 above) and section 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law (see paragraph 33 above)."

And the Court adds a further legal basis specific to the "TRNC" context:

> "the Court recalls its finding that when, as in the Foka case, an act of the 'TRNC' authorities was in compliance with laws in force within the territory of northern Cyprus, it should in principle be regarded as having a legal basis in domestic law for the purposes of the Convention."

No sub-element facts (foreseeability, accessibility, safeguards) are identified. The Court does not ground these sub-elements in any specific facts.

---

### (d) Legitimate aim

The Court grounds its implicit acceptance of legitimate aims in the following facts, which it explicitly adopts:

First, the violent character of the demonstration:

> "the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order on the demarcation line in Cyprus."

Second, the violation of the UN buffer zone and "TRNC" territory:

> "the demonstrators had forced their way into the UN buffer zone. According to the 'TRNC' authorities, they also entered into 'TRNC' territory, thus committing offences punishable under the 'TRNC' laws."

On the territorial finding specifically, the Court adds:

> "the Court notes that it does not have at its disposal any element capable of casting doubt upon the 'TRNC' authorities' finding that the area where the accused had entered was 'TRNC' territory."

---

### (e) Necessity in a democratic society

The Court grounds its proportionality finding in several factual elements, all explicitly adopted:

The violent character and breach of UN lines (adopted from the Commission and UN Secretary General):

> "the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order on the demarcation line in Cyprus."

> "the demonstrators had 'forced their way into the UN buffer zone in the Ayios Kassianos area of Nicosia', that they had broken 'through a wire barrier maintained by UNFICYP and destroyed an UNFICYP observation post' before breaking 'through the line formed by UNFICYP soldiers' and entering 'a former school complex'."

The nature of the intervention as provoked by violence rather than political motivation:

> "the intervention of the Turkish and/or Turkish-Cypriot forces was not due to the political nature of the demonstration but was provoked by its violent character and by the violation of the 'TRNC' borders by some of the demonstrators."

The territorial violation:

> "the Court notes that it does not have at its disposal any element capable of casting doubt upon the 'TRNC' authorities' finding that the area where the accused had entered was 'TRNC' territory."

The Court also invokes the wide margin of appreciation as a legal/doctrinal ground (not a factual one):

> "having regard to the wide margin of appreciation left to the States in this sphere."

---

### (f) Positive obligation

The Court mentions the positive obligation only as a general principle and does not ground it in any specific facts of the case. It states:

> "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens."

> "they cannot guarantee this absolutely and they have a wide discretion in the choice of the means to be used."

No case-specific facts are mapped onto this principle. It is invoked as background doctrine only, and no factual grounding for a positive obligation finding (or its absence) is provided.
