## Factual Grounding for Each Canonical Element

---

### (a) Scope

**(i) Assembly exists**

The Court does not explicitly discuss this as a threshold question, but the following facts ground the implicit acceptance that an assembly existed:

- "Mr Nagibin's arrest during the demonstration"
- "the LDPR's authorised gathering"
- "Mr Nagibin's peaceful presence at the LDPR's gathering"
- "Even assuming that Mr Nagibin's peaceful presence at the LDPR's gathering amounted to staging a demonstration of his own"

**(ii) Participation**

The Court does not explicitly discuss this as a threshold question, but the following facts ground the implicit acceptance that Nagibin participated:

- "Mr Nagibin's arrest during the demonstration"
- "Mr Nagibin's peaceful presence at the LDPR's gathering"
- "the leaflets distributed by him"

**(iii) Peaceful**

The Court grounds its assessment of peacefulness on the following facts:

- "It was not argued that Mr Nagibin's behaviour had been considered provocative by other participants or that the leaflets distributed by him had been intimidating or capable of inciting violence and disturbing public order"
- "there is no indication that the organisers of the LDPR's gathering viewed Mr Nagibin's presence as a separate public event or were troubled by his actions in any way"

Note: The first of these is the Court's own observation about what was *not* argued; the second is the Court's own finding about what the record does *not* show. Neither derives from a party submission that the Court adopted — they are the Court's independent assessment of the evidentiary record.

---

### (b) Interference

The Court grounds its finding of interference on the following facts, which it notes were **not disputed by the parties**:

- "Mr Nagibin's arrest during the demonstration"
- "his subsequent conviction constituted an interference with his right to freedom of assembly"

The Court explicitly adopts these as established: "It has not been disputed by the parties that Mr Nagibin's arrest during the demonstration and his subsequent conviction constituted an interference with his right to freedom of assembly."

---

### (c) Prescribed by law

The only factual grounding the Court provides is the identification of the legal instrument:

- "That interference was based on Article 20.2 § 1 of the CAO, which establishes administrative liability for a breach of the procedure for holding a public event."

As noted in the prior analysis, the Court does not go on to examine the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. No further factual grounding can be identified for those sub-elements, because the Court does not discuss them.

---

### (d) Legitimate aim

The Court does not ground its acceptance of legitimate aim in specific facts from the record. It simply states it is "ready to accept" the aims of "prevention of disorder" and "protection of the rights and freedoms of others." No specific facts are cited in support of this acceptance. The Court's acceptance appears to be a concession for the sake of argument rather than a fact-grounded finding.

---

### (e) Necessity in a democratic society

This is the most extensively fact-grounded element. The Court relies on the following facts:

**On the domestic courts' failure to provide reasoning:**
- "The domestic courts found the first applicant guilty of staging an unauthorised public event while rejecting his arguments that he had simply joined the LDPR's authorised gathering"
- "They omitted to indicate, however, any factual basis for their findings in that regard"
- "The domestic decisions contain no analysis as to what the alleged differences were between the LDPR's and Mr Nagibin's public events in terms of the message they conveyed and targeted audience"

**On the absence of evidence of disruption or provocation:**
- "there is no indication that the organisers of the LDPR's gathering viewed Mr Nagibin's presence as a separate public event or were troubled by his actions in any way"
- "It was not argued that Mr Nagibin's behaviour had been considered provocative by other participants or that the leaflets distributed by him had been intimidating or capable of inciting violence and disturbing public order"

**On the failure to justify the absence of prior authorisation:**
- "it was incumbent on the domestic courts to establish why the authorities had not authorised Mr Nagibin's event in the first place (see paragraph 1 above), in particular, what the public interest at stake was and what risks were represented by his event"
- "Their decisions, however, contain no such assessment and thus fall short of the requirements of Article 11 read in the light of Article 10 of the Convention"

**On the legal characterisation of unlawfulness:**
- "Even assuming that Mr Nagibin's peaceful presence at the LDPR's gathering amounted to staging a demonstration of his own, the absence of its prior authorisation and the ensuing 'unlawfulness' of the action do not as such justify his arrest, removal from the venue of the event and subsequent administrative conviction"

---

### (f) Positive obligation

As noted previously, the Court does not discuss positive obligations. No factual grounding can be identified for this element, as it is simply absent from the reasoning.
