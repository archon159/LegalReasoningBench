I will work through each element where I found explicit discussion or implication, and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists / ii) Participation**

The only factual grounding is the Court's own framing in the opening paragraph:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the Court's own characterisation of the situation. No further factual detail is provided. The Court does not describe the nature, location, size, or purpose of the assemblies.

**iii) Peaceful character**

No factual grounding can be identified. The Court says nothing about the conduct of the assemblies or the behaviour of participants. The peaceful character is implied structurally by the Court's willingness to apply Article 11, but there is no factual statement in the text that supports this.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is the same passage as above:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers and/or participants of public assemblies, namely the dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offences."

These are the concrete governmental acts the Court identifies as constituting interference: dispersal, arrest, and conviction for administrative offences. No further factual detail is provided about the circumstances of any of these acts.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the element most explicitly discussed, yet the factual grounding is notably thin. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it and having taken into account the issue of compliance with the six-month time-limit under Article 35 § 1 of the Convention... the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This tells us the Court reviewed the material but does not identify any specific facts from that material. The Court then states:

> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity finding is grounded primarily in the Court's prior case-law — specifically the leading cases of *Frumkin v. Russia*, *Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia*, and *Kasparov and Others v. Russia* — rather than in any specific facts of the instant cases. The Court notes only that those cases "already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present cases," without specifying what the similarity consists of factually.

In short: **no specific facts of the instant cases are quoted or identified** as the basis for the necessity finding. The reasoning is entirely doctrine- and precedent-driven.

---

**Overall observation**

The reasoning text is strikingly sparse in its factual grounding across all elements. The only facts the Court explicitly states are: (1) the applicants were organisers and/or participants of public assemblies; (2) those assemblies were dispersed; (3) the applicants were arrested; and (4) they were convicted of administrative offences. Beyond these four facts, the Court's conclusions rest on precedent and a general reference to "all the material submitted," without identifying any specific facts from that material.
