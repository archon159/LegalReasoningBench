**Factual grounding for each element discussed:**

---

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding available in the text is the following description from the opening paragraph:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This passage provides the factual basis for all three implied scope elements:
- That assemblies existed ("public assemblies")
- That the applicants participated ("organisers or participants")
- Peacefulness: **no factual grounding can be identified**. The Court does not quote or adopt any facts establishing that the assemblies were peaceful. Peacefulness is implied structurally by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis, but no specific facts are cited to support it.

---

**(b) Interference (explicit)**

The factual grounding is the same passage as above:

> "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

These are the concrete governmental acts the Court identifies as constituting the interference. No additional facts are cited.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

This is where the absence of factual grounding is most notable. The Court's finding that the interference was not necessary in a democratic society is grounded not in facts from the present case but in a reference to prior case-law:

> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

And:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

The Court thus explicitly declines to identify specific facts from the present case as the basis for its necessity assessment, instead relying on the similarity to prior cases. **No case-specific facts are quoted or adopted by the Court in support of the necessity finding.**

---

**Summary observation:**

Across all elements, the Court's factual engagement is minimal. The only facts explicitly adopted by the Court are the bare description of the governmental measures (arrest, dispersal, conviction). For the necessity assessment — the central substantive element — the Court relies entirely on precedent rather than on any facts specific to the applicants' situations.
