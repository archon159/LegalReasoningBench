I will go through each element that was found to be either implied or explicitly discussed, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Has there been an assembly?**
The only factual grounding is the following phrase from the opening paragraph:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of **public assemblies**, namely their arrest in relation to the **dispersal of these assemblies**"

This is the Court's own characterisation of the situation, not merely a party submission.

**ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The only factual grounding is again from the opening paragraph:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers or participants of public assemblies**"

**iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
There is **no factual grounding** in the reasoning text for this element. The Court does not mention any facts relating to the peaceful or non-peaceful character of the assemblies. The peaceful nature is implied only by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis, but no facts are cited to support this.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is found in the opening paragraph:
> "their **arrest** in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their **conviction for administrative offences**"

These are the concrete governmental acts the Court identifies as constituting the interference. No further factual detail is provided.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

There is **no independent factual grounding** for this element beyond what has already been quoted above. The Court does not cite any specific facts about, for example, the conduct of the authorities, the proportionality of the police response, the behaviour of the applicants, or the circumstances of the dispersal. Instead, the Court grounds its necessity finding entirely by reference to prior case-law:
> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. **Having regard to its case-law on the subject**, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity conclusion is thus grounded in the leading cases (*Frumkin*, *Navalnyy and Yashin*, *Kasparov and Others*) rather than in any independently stated facts about the instant case.
