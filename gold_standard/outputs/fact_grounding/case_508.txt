# Factual Grounding for Each Canonical Element

---

## (a) Scope

### i) Has there been an assembly?

The Court does not explicitly discuss this element. The factual grounding is implied by the Court's description of the events:

- For Mr Pleshkov: **"the reason for the deputy prefect's refusal to approve the location of the 'picket' against pension reform was the prospect of disruption to the life of the community or a breach of the rights and interests of those not participating in the event"** — the Court treats this as a public event falling under Article 11 without discussion.

- For Ms Astrakhantseva: **"the planned location of the public gathering had special significance as they wanted to commemorate the anniversary of a historical event, namely the first post-war political protest held in Pushkin Square on 5 December 1965. They also wanted to urge nearby authorities to respect freedom of expression and assembly"** — again treated as falling within scope without explicit analysis.

No explicit factual grounding for a formal scope determination exists.

---

### ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate?

No explicit factual grounding. The Court implies participation through its description of the applicants as organizers who sought approval. The closest factual basis is:

- **"The applicants intended to hold public events at specific locations and considered the alternative sites proposed by the authorities as being ill-suited to the purposes of the events."**

No explicit scope analysis is conducted on this point.

---

### iii) Was the assembly peaceful?

No explicit factual grounding for a formal peacefulness determination. The closest the Court comes is in the Astrakhantseva section, where it states as a general principle applied to the facts:

- **"This consideration holds particular relevance for simultaneous assemblies occurring at the same place and time. However, it should not serve as a justification for a blanket prohibition on hosting more than one assembly at the same place and time, provided they remain peaceful and do not pose direct threats of violence or serious danger to public safety."**

This implies the Court found the applicants' assembly to be peaceful, but no explicit factual finding is made.

---

## (b) Interference

The Court grounds its finding of interference in the following explicitly accepted fact:

- **"It is undisputed by the parties that the authorities' refusals to approve the locations for the public events in question amounted to an interference with the applicants' right to freedom of assembly."**

The Court accepts this as established on the basis that it is undisputed. No further factual elaboration is provided for the interference finding itself.

---

## (c) Prescribed by law

The Court raises concerns but does not make a definitive finding. The factual grounding for its concerns is:

- **"With regard to the lawfulness of the interference, the Court has previously asserted that domestic law granted an excessively broad discretion to the executive authorities in proposing a change of location for public events, these powers often being used in an arbitrary and discriminatory way."**

- **"While the Supreme Court Ruling provided welcome clarifications for the judiciary... further measures will be necessary to address the general issues identified earlier."**

These are grounded in the Court's prior case-law findings rather than specific facts established in the present case. No new facts are identified by the Court in this judgment to ground the lawfulness analysis. The Court explicitly declines to pursue this element further:

- **"However, given that a more conspicuous problem arises with respect to the necessity of the interference, the Court will not limit its examination under Article 11 of the Convention in the present case to the lawfulness of the interference only."**

---

## (d) Legitimate aim

The Court accepts the legitimate aim on the basis of the Government's argument, with one factual qualification it itself introduces:

- **"The Court is prepared to accept the Government's argument that the interference in question pursued the legitimate aims of ensuring public safety and protecting the rights and freedoms of others, even if in Mr Pleshkov's case the concerns related to public safety were rather vague."**

The factual grounding for the qualification ("rather vague") is found in the Court's description of Mr Pleshkov's case:

- **"The refusal, however, contained no specific explanation as to why the deputy prefect had formed such a belief, for instance, a reference to heavy pedestrian or vehicle traffic, an assessment of the size of the pedestrian pavement in front of the State Duma building against its capacity to accommodate twenty participants, or specific security concerns."**

The Court accepts the legitimate aim despite this vagueness, so the factual grounding supports acceptance rather than rejection.

---

## (e) Necessity in a democratic society

This is the most extensively grounded element. The facts are as follows, organized by applicant:

### Mr Pleshkov

**On the insufficiency of the original refusal:**
- **"The refusal, however, contained no specific explanation as to why the deputy prefect had formed such a belief, for instance, a reference to heavy pedestrian or vehicle traffic, an assessment of the size of the pedestrian pavement in front of the State Duma building against its capacity to accommodate twenty participants, or specific security concerns."**

**On the domestic courts' failure to assess specific facts:**
- **"In the domestic court decision dealing with the case, the basis for upholding the deputy prefect's refusal related exclusively to traffic issues."**
- **"The domestic courts failed to assess specific facts that allegedly made it impossible to hold the 'picket' of twenty participants at the chosen location, as required by the Supreme Court Ruling."**

**On the categorical exclusion of the location:**
- **"The deputy prefect's categorical exclusion of such a high-profile location as being unsuitable for public events for safety reasons imposed a substantial burden of justification on the national authorities, including the domestic courts."**
- **"There is no indication in the domestic courts' decisions that the security concerns clearly outweighed the adverse impact of such an exclusion on the event organiser's interests."**

**On the failure to assess the alternative location:**
- **"It was important for the 'picket' planned by Mr Pleshkov to have been conducted in close proximity to the State Duma building, as it is one of the highest representative bodies of the respondent State whose national pension policy the protesters aimed to criticise."**
- **"The domestic courts paid little attention to the importance of the event's intended location, instead broadly stating that '[the applicant] was offered an alternative location accessible to the public'."**
- **"They failed to examine whether holding the 'picket' in Lermontov Park would have fundamentally altered the character of the protest due to its distance from the target audience."**

**Conclusion on Mr Pleshkov:**
- **"The domestic courts cannot be said to have applied standards which were in conformity with the principles embodied in Article 11 of the Convention and to have struck a fair balance between the competing interests."**

---

### Ms Astrakhantseva and others

**On the significance of the chosen location:**
- **"The planned location of the public gathering had special significance as they wanted to commemorate the anniversary of a historical event, namely the first post-war political protest held in Pushkin Square on 5 December 1965. They also wanted to urge nearby authorities to respect freedom of expression and assembly."**

**On the insufficiency of the Moscow government's reasoning:**
- **"The Moscow government reasoned their rejection of the request by reference to New Year celebrations already planned in Pushkin Square, without providing any details such as the expected number of participants or the type of arrangements, such as installations, which could have reduced the available space."**

**On the domestic courts' reasoning:**
- **"The domestic courts agreed with the Moscow government's assessment about the risks to public order and safety, but did so without providing an estimated attendance figure for the New Year celebrations, merely mentioning it as a popular cultural event, or the overall capacity of the venue."**

**On the failure to consider alternative arrangements:**
- **"Even if a large number of participants in the celebrations was expected, it was incumbent upon the authorities to at least consider a solution that would have allowed both events to proceed peacefully, for example offering the applicants a non-peak hour for their gathering."**
- **"The applicants indeed requested an alternative time slot that would not overlap with the concurrent event."**
- **"The authorities adopted a formalistic attitude and made no genuine efforts to explore in a timely manner potential solutions for accommodating the applicants' gathering."**

**On the unsuitability of the proposed alternative location:**
- **"The applicants sought to commemorate the anniversary of a protest held in Pushkin Square. The significance of their event was thus closely tied to the chosen venue."**
- **"The applicants' message also targeted the authorities located near Pushkin Square."**
- **"The Court is not convinced that Sokolniki Park, situated eight kilometres away from Pushkin Square, was a suitable location for effectively conveying the message of the applicants' gathering. Not only it was a less frequented place, but it was also located far away from the seats of the authorities targeted by the event's intended message."**

**Conclusion on Ms Astrakhantseva:**
- **"The authorities, including the domestic courts, failed to propose acceptable alternative arrangements to the applicants, such as a non-peak hour time slot or an alternative venue suitable for conveying the message. They have thus failed to provide 'relevant and sufficient reasons' to justify the restrictions imposed on the applicants in exercising their right to freedom of assembly. In such circumstances, these restrictions cannot be said to have been necessary in a democratic society."**

---

## (f) Positive obligation

The Court does not explicitly frame this as a positive obligation analysis. The factual grounding for the implied positive obligations is embedded within the necessity analysis above. The specific facts the Court uses to ground the implied obligation to facilitate assembly are:

- **"The applicants indeed requested an alternative time slot that would not overlap with the concurrent event. However, the authorities adopted a formalistic attitude and made no genuine efforts to explore in a timely manner potential solutions for accommodating the applicants' gathering."**

- **"The domestic courts paid little attention to the importance of the event's intended location, instead broadly stating that '[the applicant] was offered an alternative location accessible to the public'."**

- **"They failed to examine whether holding the 'picket' in Lermontov Park would have fundamentally altered the character of the protest due to its distance from the target audience."**

These facts ground the Court's implied finding that the government failed to meet its obligation to make good-faith efforts to facilitate the assemblies, but since the Court does not explicitly label this as a positive obligation analysis, no separate factual grounding exists beyond what is already quoted under necessity.
