I will go through each element that was found to be either implied or explicitly discussed, and identify the factual grounding from the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**
The only factual grounding is the following phrase from the opening paragraph:
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of **public assemblies**, namely the **dispersal of these assemblies**"

The Court itself uses the word "assemblies" and refers to their dispersal, which constitutes the Court's own characterisation of the events.

**ii) Participation**
> "disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers or participants** of public assemblies"

The Court itself characterises the applicants as organisers or participants.

**iii) Peaceful character**
No factual grounding can be identified. The Court says nothing about the character of the assemblies — whether they were violent or peaceful. The peaceful nature is implied only by the Court's willingness to proceed with the Article 11 analysis, but no fact is stated to ground this.

---

**(b) Interference**

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely the **dispersal of these assemblies, as well as their arrest followed by their conviction for administrative offence**"

These are the concrete governmental acts the Court identifies as constituting the interference: dispersal, arrest, and conviction for an administrative offence. The Court adopts this characterisation in its own voice.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is where the factual grounding is notably thin. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And:

> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity finding is grounded not in any specific facts quoted or adopted from the record, but rather by reference to prior case-law — specifically *Frumkin v. Russia*, *Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia*, and *Kasparov and Others v. Russia* — in which "the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case." The Court's reasoning is essentially that the present case is sufficiently similar to those leading cases that no new factual analysis is required.

**In short: no specific facts from the present case are quoted or adopted by the Court to ground the necessity finding.** The assessment rests entirely on the similarity to prior cases and the absence of any new material that would justify a different conclusion.
