This is an important question that requires careful attention to what the Court actually established factually, as opposed to what it merely asserted or referenced.

---

**(a) Scope — Assembly exists / Participation**

The only factual grounding offered is the following statement from the opening paragraph:

> "disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the Court's own characterisation of the situation. It establishes that there were "public assemblies," that the applicants were "organisers or participants" of those assemblies, and that the assemblies were "dispersed." No further factual detail is provided in this reasoning section.

**(a) Scope — Peaceful character**

No factual grounding can be identified. The Court does not mention any facts bearing on whether the assemblies were peaceful. The peaceful character is implied by the Court's willingness to apply Article 11 protection, but no facts are cited to support this.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is the same passage as above:

> "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

These are the specific governmental acts identified as constituting the interference: arrest, dispersal, and administrative conviction. No further factual elaboration is provided.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

No factual grounding can be identified in this reasoning section. The Court does not cite any specific facts to support its conclusion that the interference was not necessary in a democratic society. Instead, it relies entirely on a reference to prior case-law:

> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014, and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

And:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

The Court thus acknowledges that it examined material but does not disclose what that material contained or how specific facts mapped onto the necessity analysis. The necessity conclusion is reached by analogy to prior cases, not by explicit factual reasoning in this text.

---

**Overall observation:** The factual grounding in this reasoning section is strikingly thin. The Court relies heavily on a repetition formula referencing prior leading cases and a general assertion that no new facts or arguments were found. This is characteristic of a repetitive-case procedure (sometimes called a "clone case" or "well-established case-law" procedure under Rule 28A or similar), where the Court dispenses with detailed reasoning by reference to structurally identical prior judgments.
