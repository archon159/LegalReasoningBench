**Factual grounding for each element discussed or implied:**

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding is the following passage from the introductory paragraph:

"The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the Court's own characterisation of the situation, not merely a party submission. It establishes that there were assemblies and that the applicants were organisers or participants. There is no factual grounding in the text for the peacefulness of the assemblies — the Court does not mention this at all.

**(b) Interference (implied)**

The factual grounding is the same passage:

"their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

These are the concrete governmental acts that the Court treats as constituting interference. No further factual detail is provided in this reasoning section.

**(c) Prescribed by law**

No factual grounding can be identified, as this element is not discussed at all.

**(d) Legitimate aim**

No factual grounding can be identified, as this element is not discussed at all.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the only element explicitly discussed, yet the factual grounding is notably thin. The Court does not identify any specific facts about the circumstances of the individual assemblies, the conduct of the authorities, or the proportionality of the specific measures applied to each applicant. The only passage that could be said to provide factual grounding is again:

"their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

Beyond this, the Court's necessity conclusion rests entirely on its prior case-law rather than on facts specific to the present case:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The Court thus acknowledges that it examined the material but explicitly declines to ground its conclusion in case-specific facts, instead relying on the pattern established in the leading cases (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others).

**(f) Positive obligation**

No factual grounding can be identified, as this element is not discussed at all.
