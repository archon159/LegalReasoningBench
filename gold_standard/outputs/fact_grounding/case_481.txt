# Factual Grounding for Each Canonical Element

---

## (a) Scope

### i) Assembly exists

The Court grounds this in the following facts, stated as given throughout the reasoning:

> "the prohibition of an assembly he had intended to hold on All Saints' Day in commemoration of the Salzburg Jews murdered by the SS during the Second World War"

> "The applicant's assembly was clearly intended as a counter-demonstration to protest against the gathering of Comradeship IV, an association which undisputedly consists mainly of former members of the SS."

### ii) Participation

> "the applicant, being a member of parliament, essentially wished to protest against the gathering of Comradeship IV"

> "The applicant emphasises that the main purpose of his assembly was to remind the public of the crimes committed by the SS and to commemorate the Salzburg Jews murdered by them. The coincidence in time and venue with the commemoration ceremony of Comradeship IV was an essential part of the message he wanted to convey."

Note: The second quote is introduced with "The applicant emphasises," which suggests it originates as a party submission. However, the Court does not explicitly distance itself from these facts and proceeds to reason on their basis — treating them as the established description of the assembly's purpose. The Court does not separately verify or adopt them with explicit language of acceptance, so this should be read with some caution.

### iii) Peaceful

> "They envisaged peaceful and silent means of expressing their opinion, namely the carrying of commemorative messages, and had explicitly ruled out the use of chanting or banners. Thus, the intended assembly in itself could not have hurt the feelings of cemetery-goers."

> "it was not alleged that any violent incidents had occurred on previous occasions"

---

## (b) Interference

> "It is common ground that the prohibition in issue constituted an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly"

The factual basis for the interference is the prohibition itself:

> "the prohibition of an assembly he had intended to hold on All Saints' Day"

> "the domestic authorities imposed an unconditional prohibition on the applicant's assembly"

---

## (c) Prescribed by law

> "It is common ground that the prohibition in issue constituted an interference with the applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly which was prescribed by law, namely by section 6 of the Assembly Act"

The Court provides no further factual grounding for the sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, or safeguards. The legal basis (section 6 of the Assembly Act) is simply accepted as common ground. No factual analysis is offered beyond naming the provision.

---

## (d) Legitimate aim

> "It is common ground that the prohibition in issue... served legitimate aims recognised in Article 11 § 2 of the Convention, namely the 'prevention of disorder' and 'the protection of the rights and freedoms of others'."

The factual grounding for these aims is found in the Court's description of the competing interests at stake:

> "The applicant's right to freedom of peaceful assembly and his right to freedom of expression have to be balanced against the association's right to protection against disruption of its assembly and the cemetery-goers' right to protection of their freedom to manifest their religion."

> "the Constitutional Court had particular regard to the fact that All Saints' Day was an important religious holiday on which the population traditionally went to cemeteries to commemorate the dead and that disturbances caused by disputes between members of the assembly organised by the applicant and members of Comradeship IV were likely to occur in the light of the experience of previous years."

The Court accepts these as the factual context grounding the legitimate aims, though it ultimately finds the prohibition disproportionate.

---

## (e) Necessity in a democratic society

This element has the richest factual grounding. The Court draws on the following facts:

**On the weight of the applicant's interest:**

> "the unconditional prohibition of a counter-demonstration is a very far-reaching measure which would require particular justification, all the more so as the applicant, being a member of parliament, essentially wished to protest against the gathering of Comradeship IV and, thus, to express an opinion on an issue of public interest"

> "The applicant's assembly was clearly intended as a counter-demonstration to protest against the gathering of Comradeship IV, an association which undisputedly consists mainly of former members of the SS."

> "The coincidence in time and venue with the commemoration ceremony of Comradeship IV was an essential part of the message he wanted to convey."

**On the limited threat posed by the assembly:**

> "the assembly was in no way directed against the cemetery-goers' beliefs or the manifestation of them"

> "the applicant expected only a small number of participants"

> "They envisaged peaceful and silent means of expressing their opinion, namely the carrying of commemorative messages, and had explicitly ruled out the use of chanting or banners. Thus, the intended assembly in itself could not have hurt the feelings of cemetery-goers."

> "it was not alleged that any violent incidents had occurred on previous occasions"

**On the failure to consider less restrictive alternatives:**

> "the Court is not convinced by the Government's argument that allowing both meetings while taking preventive measures, such as ensuring police presence in order to keep the two assemblies apart, was not a viable alternative which would have preserved the applicant's right to freedom of assembly while at the same time offering a sufficient degree of protection as regards the rights of the cemetery's visitors"

**On the domestic authorities' failure to weigh the applicant's interests:**

> "The Court finds it striking that the domestic authorities attached no weight to this aspect of the case."

> "the domestic authorities imposed an unconditional prohibition on the applicant's assembly. The Court therefore finds that they gave too little weight to the applicant's interest in holding the intended assembly and expressing his protest against the meeting of Comradeship IV, while giving too much weight to the interest of cemetery-goers in being protected against some rather limited disturbances."

**On the insufficiency of the aim of protecting Comradeship IV:**

> "It is undisputed that the aim of protecting the gathering of Comradeship IV does not provide sufficient justification for the contested prohibition. This has been clearly pointed out by the Constitutional Court. The Court fully agrees with that position."

---

## (f) Positive obligation

The Court grounds the positive obligation discussion in the following facts:

**On the general existence of positive obligations:**

> "States may be required under Article 11 to take positive measures in order to protect a lawful demonstration against counter-demonstrations (see Plattform 'Ärzte für das Leben', cited above, § 34)."

This is stated as a legal principle rather than grounded in specific facts of the case.

**On the Constitutional Court's invocation of positive obligations under Article 9:**

> "The Constitutional Court dismissed this approach as being too narrow... It went on to say that the prohibition was nevertheless justified or even required by the State's positive obligation under Article 9 to protect persons manifesting their religion against deliberate disturbance by others. In arriving at that conclusion, the Constitutional Court had particular regard to the fact that All Saints' Day was an important religious holiday on which the population traditionally went to cemeteries to commemorate the dead and that disturbances caused by disputes between members of the assembly organised by the applicant and members of Comradeship IV were likely to occur in the light of the experience of previous years."

**On why the positive obligation did not require the prohibition:**

The Court does not separately state a factual basis for rejecting the positive obligation argument as such. Instead, it folds this into the proportionality analysis, relying on the same facts identified under (e) above — particularly the peaceful nature of the assembly, the availability of less restrictive measures (police presence), and the limited nature of the anticipated disturbances:

> "it was not alleged that any violent incidents had occurred on previous occasions"

> "allowing both meetings while taking preventive measures, such as ensuring police presence in order to keep the two assemblies apart, was not a viable alternative" — which the Court rejects, implying that such measures would have sufficed to discharge any positive obligation toward cemetery-goers.
