## Factual Grounding for Each Canonical Element

---

### (a) Scope

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**
The Court does not explicitly discuss this element, and no specific facts are marshalled to establish it. The nature of the event as a "demonstration" is simply assumed. No factual grounding can be identified beyond the bare reference to "a demonstration on 25 January 2004."

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**
The Court grounds this implicitly in the following fact, which it treats as established:
> "the decision to reject the applicant party's application to hold a demonstration on 25 January 2004"

This establishes that the applicant party applied to hold the demonstration, i.e., attempted to participate as organiser. No further facts are marshalled.

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**
The Court grounds this on two facts it explicitly treats as established:

First, the applicant party's prior conduct:
> "the applicant party had a record of numerous protest demonstrations held in 2002 which were peaceful and at which no violent clashes had occurred"

Second, the absence of evidence of violent intent:
> "there was nothing to suggest in the applicant party's actions that it intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities or with supporters of the governing party"

Additionally, the Court's assessment of the slogans is relevant here:
> "The Court notes that such slogans should be understood as an expression of dissatisfaction and protest and is not convinced that they could reasonably be considered as a call to violence even if accompanied by the burning of flags and pictures of Russian leaders."

---

### (b) Interference

The Court grounds the finding of interference on one core fact, accepted by both parties:
> "the decision to reject the applicant party's application to hold a demonstration on 25 January 2004 amounted to 'interference by [a] public authority' with the applicant's right to freedom of assembly under the first paragraph of Article 11"

No further factual elaboration is provided — the refusal of authorisation is itself the interference.

---

### (c) Prescribed by law

The Court makes no factual findings on this element. It simply records that the parties did not dispute it:
> "The parties do not dispute that the interference was lawful within the meaning of Article 11 of the Convention."

No facts are marshalled by the Court to ground this conclusion. It is accepted as conceded.

---

### (d) Legitimate aim

The Court explicitly declines to make a finding on this element. Accordingly, no facts are marshalled by the Court to ground a conclusion on legitimate aim. The Government's submissions on national security and public order are noted but not adopted by the Court:
> "The Court, for the reasons set out below, does not consider it necessary to decide this point and will focus on the proportionality of the interference."

No factual grounding can be identified for this element.

---

### (e) Necessity in a democratic society

This is the most extensively fact-grounded element. The Court draws on the following established facts:

**On the political context and narrow margin of appreciation:**
> "at the material time the CDPP was a minority parliamentary opposition party with approximately ten per cent of the seats in Parliament, while the majority Communist Party had approximately seventy per cent of the seats"

> "the interference concerned a demonstration in which the applicant party intended to protest against alleged anti-democratic abuses committed by the Government and against the Russian military presence in the break-away Transdniestrian region of Moldova"

> "Given the public interest in free expression in respect of such topics and the fact that the applicant party was an opposition parliamentary political party, the Court considers that the State's margin of appreciation was correspondingly narrow and that only very compelling reasons would have justified the interference"

**On the assessment of the slogans:**
> "The Court notes that the Chişinău Municipal Council and the domestic courts considered that the slogans 'Down with Voronin's totalitarian regime' and 'Down with Putin's occupation regime' amounted to calls to a violent overthrow of the constitutional regime and to hatred towards the Russian people and an instigation to a war of aggression against Russia."

> "The Court notes that such slogans should be understood as an expression of dissatisfaction and protest and is not convinced that they could reasonably be considered as a call to violence even if accompanied by the burning of flags and pictures of Russian leaders."

> "the applicant party's slogans, even if accompanied by the burning of flags and pictures, was a form of expressing an opinion in respect of an issue of major public interest, namely the presence of Russian troops on the territory of Moldova"

**On the risk of clashes between demonstrators:**
> "even if there was a theoretical risk of violent clashes between the protesters and supporters of the Communist Party, it was the task of the police to stand between the two groups and to ensure public order"

**On the applicant party's prior conduct:**
> "the applicant party had a record of numerous protest demonstrations held in 2002 which were peaceful and at which no violent clashes had occurred"

> "there was nothing to suggest in the applicant party's actions that it intended to disrupt public order or to seek a confrontation with the authorities or with supporters of the governing party"

---

### (f) Positive obligation

The Court's discussion of positive obligations is grounded in the following facts:

**On the risk of counter-demonstrators (used to derive the positive obligation to protect rather than ban):**
> "even if there was a theoretical risk of violent clashes between the protesters and supporters of the Communist Party, it was the task of the police to stand between the two groups and to ensure public order"

**On the chilling effect of failing to protect:**
> "Their members must be able to hold meetings without having to fear that they will be subjected to physical violence by their opponents. Such a fear would be liable to deter other associations or political parties from openly expressing their opinions on highly controversial issues affecting the community."

Note, however, that these last two passages are stated as general legal principles rather than findings of fact specific to this case. The Court does not separately establish facts showing that the government failed its positive obligation — this conclusion is subsumed into the necessity analysis rather than grounded in distinct factual findings.
