I will go through each element that was found to be either explicitly discussed or implied, and identify the factual grounding in the reasoning text.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists / ii) Participation**

The only factual grounding in the text is the following phrase:

> "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences"

and

> "organisers or participants of public assemblies"

These are the only facts the Court relies on (or implies reliance on) for scope. No further factual detail is provided — the Court does not describe the assemblies, their location, size, purpose, or duration.

**iii) Peaceful**

There is no factual grounding whatsoever for the peaceful character of the assemblies. The Court does not mention any facts bearing on this question. The implication of peacefulness rests entirely on the structural logic of the analysis (the Court proceeds to assess Article 11 without suggesting the assemblies fell outside its protection), not on any stated facts.

---

**(b) Interference**

The factual grounding is:

> "their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences"

These are the only concrete facts the Court identifies as constituting the interference. No further detail is provided about the nature of the arrests, the dispersal methods, or the administrative convictions.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the most substantively discussed element, yet the factual grounding is remarkably thin. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This sentence acknowledges that material was submitted and examined, but does not identify or quote any specific facts from that material. The Court's necessity finding is grounded not in explicitly stated case-specific facts, but in:

1. The reference to prior leading cases:
> "In the leading cases of Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, ECHR 2016 (extracts), Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, 4 December 2014 and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, 3 October 2013, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

2. The general reference to case-law:
> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject"

In short: **no case-specific facts are explicitly stated as the basis for the necessity finding**. The Court treats the present case as sufficiently similar to prior leading cases to warrant the same conclusion, without spelling out which facts make it similar.

---

**Overall observation**

The reasoning text is extremely sparse in its factual grounding. Across all elements — scope, interference, and necessity — the only concrete facts explicitly stated are that the applicants were organisers or participants of public assemblies, that these assemblies were dispersed, that the applicants were arrested, and that they were convicted of administrative offences. No further facts are stated, adopted, or quoted from party submissions. The Court's analysis rests primarily on analogy to prior case-law rather than on detailed factual reasoning.
