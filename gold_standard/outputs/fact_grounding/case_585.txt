I will go through each element where I found explicit discussion or implication, and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**
The Court's own language provides the factual grounding:
> "the dispersal of the public assembly on 16 October 2006"

The Court itself uses the phrase "public assembly," which is its own characterisation of the event, not merely a party submission.

**ii) Participation**
The Court does not itself make an independent factual finding on participation. It refers to "the arrest of the third, fourth, fifth and sixth applicants" in connection with the assembly, which implies their presence, but this is thin. The Government's submission that "the applicants had taken part in an unauthorised picket" is not explicitly adopted by the Court as an established fact. **No clear factual grounding can be identified beyond the implied inference from the arrest.**

**iii) Peaceful character**
The Court provides no factual grounding for this. It simply applies Article 11 without questioning the peaceful character of the assembly. **No factual grounding can be identified.**

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds interference in the following facts, stated in its own reasoning:
> "the dispersal of the public assembly on 16 October 2006 and the arrest of the third, fourth, fifth and sixth applicants"

These are the Court's own words, not merely party submissions, and they constitute the factual basis for the implied finding of interference.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

Not discussed. **No factual grounding to identify.**

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

Not discussed. **No factual grounding to identify.**

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

The Court's necessity assessment is grounded in the same core facts it identifies:
> "the dispersal of the public assembly on 16 October 2006 and the arrest of the third, fourth, fifth and sixth applicants were not 'necessary in a democratic society'"

Beyond naming these acts, the Court does not elaborate on specific facts underpinning the necessity analysis. Instead, it grounds its conclusion procedurally:
> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And by reference to prior case law:
> "In the leading cases (see, for example, Frumkin v. Russia, no. 74568/12, §§ 100-42, 5 January 2016; Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia, no. 76204/11, §§ 49-75, 4 December 2014; and Kasparov and Others v. Russia, no. 21613/07, §§ 82-97, 3 October 2013) the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

So the factual grounding for necessity is minimal and largely delegated to the precedents cited. The Court does not independently analyse the specific facts of this case in any depth.

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

Not discussed. **No factual grounding to identify.**
