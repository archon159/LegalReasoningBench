Here is the analysis of factual grounding for each relevant canonical element:

---

**(a) Scope — implied**

The only factual grounding available in the text is:

> "the restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events"

This phrase implies that there were public events (assemblies) and that the applicants were affected by restrictions on them. However, this is thin: the Court does not describe the specific events, who participated, or whether they were peaceful. No richer factual grounding is provided in this reasoning section. The implication of scope rests almost entirely on the Court's decision to proceed to the merits, not on any explicit factual findings stated here.

---

**(b) Interference — explicit**

The factual grounding is the same phrase as above:

> "the restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events"

This is the only factual basis offered for the finding of interference. The Court does not describe the specific acts of the authorities in any further detail within this reasoning section.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law — explicit**

The Court's finding that the "quality of law" requirements were not met is not grounded in any specific facts stated in this reasoning section. The Court simply asserts:

> "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements"

No specific legal provisions are named, no features of those provisions are described, and no facts about their application are recounted. The factual and legal grounding is entirely delegated to the Lashmankin precedent by the statement:

> "In the leading case of Lashmankin and Others v. Russia, no. 57818/09 and 14 others, 7 February 2017, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case."

**Conclusion: No independently stated factual grounding in this text for the prescribed-by-law finding.**

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society — explicit**

The same observation applies here. The finding that the interferences were not "necessary in a democratic society" is stated as a conclusion:

> "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the Convention's 'quality of law' requirements or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'"

No facts are cited to support this conclusion within this reasoning section. Again, the grounding is delegated to Lashmankin:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion on the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This sentence acknowledges that material was submitted and examined, but does not identify or quote any of it. **Conclusion: No independently stated factual grounding in this text for the necessity finding.**

---

**Overall observation:**

The reasoning section is almost entirely conclusory and cross-referential. The only facts explicitly stated in the text are the generic description of "restrictions imposed by the authorities on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events." All substantive factual and legal grounding is implicitly delegated to the Lashmankin judgment and to unspecified "material submitted" to the Court, neither of which is reproduced or summarized here.
