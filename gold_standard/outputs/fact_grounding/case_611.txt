**Factual grounding for each element discussed or implied:**

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding in the reasoning text is the following sentence from the opening paragraph:

"The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as **organisers or participants of public assemblies**, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the sole factual basis from which the Court implies that assemblies existed, that the applicants participated, and (weakly) that the assemblies were peaceful. It should be noted, however, that this sentence describes what the applicants *complained of*, not facts the Court itself has independently verified or explicitly adopted. The Court does not separately state "we find that there were assemblies" or "we find that the assemblies were peaceful." The closest the Court comes to adopting these facts as established is the later reference to "the applicants' freedom of assembly" as an operative concept, which presupposes the scope elements are met.

**(b) Interference (implied)**

The factual grounding is again the opening paragraph:

"their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

These are the concrete governmental acts that constitute the interference. As with scope, the Court does not explicitly adopt these as proven facts in a separate finding — they appear in the description of the applicants' complaints. However, the Court's later reference to "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly" treats interference as an established operative fact.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicitly discussed)**

This is the only element for which the Court provides any reasoning, but notably the factual grounding is extremely thin. The Court states:

"Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This is the entirety of the Court's factual engagement on necessity. The Court does not identify any specific facts about the conduct of the assemblies, the nature of the dispersal, the proportionality of the arrests, or the severity of the convictions. Instead, it grounds its necessity assessment entirely on the absence of distinguishing facts or arguments, combined with reference to its prior case law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov and Others).

**Overall observation:**

The reasoning text is strikingly sparse in its factual grounding across all elements. The Court does not make independent factual findings; it relies on the characterization of the applicants' complaints in the opening paragraph and on the absence of new material to distinguish the case from prior leading judgments. For elements **(c) prescribed by law**, **(d) legitimate aim**, and **(f) positive obligation**, no factual grounding can be identified because those elements are not discussed at all.
