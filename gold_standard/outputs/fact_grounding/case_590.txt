This is an important question that requires careful attention to what the Court actually establishes factually, as opposed to what it merely asserts as a legal conclusion.

---

**Preliminary observation**

The reasoning section is strikingly thin on factual grounding. The Court does not narrate facts, does not quote evidence, and does not reproduce party submissions that it explicitly adopts. It reasons almost entirely by reference to prior case law and by assertion. This severely limits what can be identified as factual grounding for each element.

---

**(a) Scope — Assembly, Participation, Peaceful character**

The only factual grounding offered is the following sentence:

> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the sole factual basis from which the Court implies the existence of assemblies and the applicants' participation. However, it should be noted that this sentence characterizes the applicants' *complaints*, not facts the Court has independently established. The Court does not separately state "we find that assemblies took place" or "we find that the applicants participated."

Regarding the **peaceful character** of the assemblies: there is no factual grounding whatsoever. The Court neither quotes facts nor adopts any submission establishing that the assemblies were peaceful.

---

**(b) Interference**

The only factual grounding is again the same sentence:

> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

The facts of arrest, dispersal, and conviction for administrative offences are the factual basis from which interference is implied. Again, however, these are presented as the applicants' complaints rather than as independently verified findings of fact by the Court.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the element most explicitly discussed, yet it has the least factual grounding in this excerpt. The Court states:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

And:

> "Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The necessity assessment is grounded not in specific facts but in:
1. A reference to unspecified "material submitted to it"
2. A reference to prior case law (specifically *Frumkin v. Russia*, *Navalnyy and Yashin v. Russia*, and *Kasparov and Others v. Russia*)

No specific facts — such as the manner of dispersal, the conduct of authorities, the nature of the offences charged, or the severity of sanctions — are quoted or adopted by the Court as the factual basis for its necessity finding.

---

**Overall conclusion**

The factual grounding across all discussed elements is minimal and largely confined to a single sentence characterizing the applicants' complaints. The Court's reasoning is driven almost entirely by reference to precedent rather than by engagement with the specific facts of the case. This is characteristic of a repetitive-case procedure (sometimes called a "clone case" approach), where the Court treats the matter as already resolved by leading judgments and does not re-examine the factual record in detail.
