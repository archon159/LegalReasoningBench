I will go through each canonical element where I found explicit discussion or implication, and identify the factual grounding the Court itself relied upon.

---

**(a) Scope**

**i) Assembly exists**
The Court does not explicitly discuss this as a legal element, but grounds it in the following factual references:
- "her arrest and conviction following her participation in the demonstration of 17 April 2011"
- "her arrest and conviction following her participation in the demonstration of 26 January 2013"

No further factual elaboration is offered by the Court on this element.

**ii) Participation**
Same factual grounding as above:
- "her arrest and conviction following her participation in the demonstration of 17 April 2011"
- "her arrest and conviction following her participation in the demonstration of 26 January 2013"

**iii) Peaceful character**
The Court grounds this in the following facts it explicitly accepts:
- First case (via Gafgaz Mammadov): "the lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration, which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner"
- Second case: "the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention"

The Court thus treats the peaceful character as an established fact in both cases, not merely as a party assertion.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court grounds the existence of interference in the following facts:
- "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging her from participating in political rallies."

This is the only passage where the Court directly links the State's concrete acts to their impact on the applicant's freedom. The factual acts underpinning interference are:
- dispersal of the demonstrations
- the applicant's arrests
- the applicant's convictions

No single passage formally declares these to constitute "interference" as a doctrinal step, but these facts are treated throughout as the relevant State acts under scrutiny.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

**i) Foreseeability**
The Court grounds its concerns in the following:
- "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed"
- "doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely, Article 310.1 of the CAO, invoked by the authorities for arresting and convicting the participant in an unauthorised demonstration"

For the second case, the Court accepts lawfulness on the following factual basis:
- "The Court observes that the organisers did not give the BCEA prior notice of the demonstration of 26 January 2013."
- "the applicant has failed to sufficiently substantiate her allegation that the demonstration in which she participated was a spontaneous one."

**ii) Accessibility**
No factual grounding identified. The Court does not discuss this sub-element.

**iii) Safeguards against abuse**
The Court grounds this in:
- "the existence of serious concerns about the foreseeability and precision of the legislation governing public assemblies, and about the possibility of public assemblies being abusively banned or dispersed"
- "doubts about the credibility of the formal ground, namely, Article 310.1 of the CAO, invoked by the authorities for arresting and convicting the participant in an unauthorised demonstration"

---

**(d) Legitimate aim**

The Court does not itself establish any facts grounding a legitimate aim. It does not explicitly accept any of the Government's stated aims as established. The Government's submissions mention national security, rights of others, and prevention of disorder or crime, but the Court neither quotes nor adopts these as factually grounded. No factual grounding can be identified for this element from the Court's own reasoning.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

This is the most factually grounded element. The Court relies on the following facts:

First case:
- "the failure by the authorities to take into consideration the fact that the demonstration had been notified"
- "the lack of relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration, which had been intended to be peaceful and had been conducted in a peaceful manner"
- "the lack of any acknowledgment that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention"

Second case:
- "the authorities in the present case have not adduced relevant and sufficient reasons justifying the dispersal of the demonstration"
- "The authorities also failed to acknowledge that the act of participating in an unauthorised peaceful demonstration was by itself protected by Article 11 of the Convention"

On chilling effect (applicable to both cases):
- "The dispersal of the demonstrations and the applicant's arrests and convictions could not but have the effect of discouraging her from participating in political rallies."
- "in the second case the sanction applied to the applicant under Article 298 of the CAO was very harsh. She was ordered to pay AZN 2,000, whereas at the material time the minimum wage in the country was AZN 93.5."
- "The measures applied in the present cases and the fear of sanctions that could potentially be applied against participants and organisers of unauthorised peaceful assemblies undoubtedly have a chilling effect on the exercise of freedom of assembly. This deters other opposition supporters and the public at large from attending demonstrations, and, more generally, from participating in open political debate."

---

**(f) Positive obligation**

No factual grounding identified, as the Court does not discuss positive obligations at all in this judgment.
