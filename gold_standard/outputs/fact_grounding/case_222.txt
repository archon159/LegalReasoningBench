## Factual Grounding for Each Canonical Element

---

### (a) Scope

**(i) Has there been an assembly?**

The Court grounds this implicitly in the following fact:

> "The applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Kassianos school in Nicosia."

**(ii) Did the applicant (try to) participate in the assembly?**

Same factual basis as above:

> "The applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Kassianos school in Nicosia."

**(iii) Was the assembly peaceful?**

The Court does not explicitly rule on peacefulness at the scope stage and does not exclude the assembly from Article 11's scope on grounds of violence. The factual grounding for the violence — which the Court treats as relevant to necessity rather than scope — is:

> "The Court notes that in the case of Chrysostomos and Papachrysostomou, the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order on the demarcation line in Cyprus."

And, adopted from the UN Secretary General's report:

> "the demonstrators had 'forced their way into the UN buffer zone in the Ayios Kassianos area of Nicosia', that they had broken 'through a wire barrier maintained by UNFICYP and destroyed an UNFICYP observation post' before breaking 'through the line formed by UNFICYP soldiers' and entering 'a former school complex'."

No factual grounding can be identified for a positive finding of peacefulness at the scope stage, because the Court simply does not address this sub-element explicitly.

---

### (b) Interference

The Court grounds the interference finding in:

> "The applicant and other women clashed with Turkish-Cypriot police while demonstrating in or in the vicinity of the Ayios Kassianos school in Nicosia. The demonstration was dispersed and some of the demonstrators were arrested."

---

### (c) Prescribed by law

The Court grounds the "prescribed by law" finding in:

> "This interference had a legal basis, namely sections 70 and 71 of the Cypriot Criminal Code (see paragraphs 22-23 above) and section 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law (see paragraph 26 above)."

On the specific question of whether TRNC laws can constitute a valid legal basis, the Court grounds its reasoning in the prior holding from *Foka v. Turkey*, which it adopts:

> "the 'TRNC' was exercising de facto authority over northern Cyprus and that the responsibility of Turkey for the acts of the 'TRNC' was inconsistent with the applicant's view that the measures adopted by it should always be regarded as lacking a 'lawful' basis in terms of the Convention."

And:

> "when, as in the Foka case, an act of the 'TRNC' authorities was in compliance with laws in force within the territory of northern Cyprus, it should in principle be regarded as having a legal basis in domestic law for the purposes of the Convention."

The sub-elements of foreseeability, accessibility, and safeguards against abuse have no identifiable factual grounding in the text, as the Court does not discuss them.

---

### (d) Legitimate aim

The Court does not independently establish facts to ground the legitimate aim finding. It notes the Government's submission of aims (national security, public safety, prevention of disorder and crime) and then proceeds directly to the necessity analysis. The factual grounding for the legitimate aim is effectively subsumed into the necessity analysis, where the Court establishes:

> "the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order on the demarcation line in Cyprus."

And:

> "the demonstrators had 'forced their way into the UN buffer zone in the Ayios Kassianos area of Nicosia', that they had broken 'through a wire barrier maintained by UNFICYP and destroyed an UNFICYP observation post' before breaking 'through the line formed by UNFICYP soldiers' and entering 'a former school complex'."

These facts implicitly support the existence of a legitimate aim (prevention of disorder, public safety) without the Court making this mapping explicit.

---

### (e) Necessity in a democratic society

The Court grounds its proportionality/necessity finding in the following facts:

On the violent character of the demonstration:

> "the Commission found that the demonstration on 19 July 1989 was violent, that it had broken through the UN defence lines and constituted a serious threat to peace and public order on the demarcation line in Cyprus."

On the specific conduct of the demonstrators, adopted from the UN Secretary General's report:

> "the demonstrators had 'forced their way into the UN buffer zone in the Ayios Kassianos area of Nicosia', that they had broken 'through a wire barrier maintained by UNFICYP and destroyed an UNFICYP observation post' before breaking 'through the line formed by UNFICYP soldiers' and entering 'a former school complex'."

On the territorial dimension:

> "the demonstrators had forced their way into the UN buffer zone. According to the 'TRNC' authorities, they also entered 'TRNC' territory, thus committing offences punishable under the 'TRNC' laws."

On the Court's acceptance of the territorial claim:

> "the Court notes that it does not have at its disposal any element capable of casting doubt upon the respondent Government's statement that the area entered by some of the demonstrators was 'TRNC' territory."

On the reason for the intervention:

> "the intervention of the Turkish and/or Turkish-Cypriot forces was not due to the political nature of the demonstration but was provoked by its violent character and by the violation of the 'TRNC' borders by some of the demonstrators."

---

### (f) Positive obligation

The Court mentions the positive obligation principle but does not ground it in any case-specific facts. It is stated only as a general legal principle:

> "the authorities have a duty to take appropriate measures with regard to demonstrations in order to ensure their peaceful conduct and the safety of all citizens."

No case-specific factual grounding can be identified for this element, consistent with the earlier finding that the Court does not analyze positive obligations as a standalone element in this case.
