This is an important question, and the honest answer is that **the reasoning text is almost entirely devoid of specific factual grounding** for any of the canonical elements. The Court's reasoning is conducted at a very high level of abstraction, relying on a reference to a leading case rather than on case-specific facts. Let me go through each element:

---

**(a) Scope (assembly, participation, peaceful character)**

The only textual basis is the description of the complaints:

> "The applicants complained principally of the prohibition on holding peaceful assemblies or restrictions on the location, time or manner of conduct of public events."

This is a characterization of the applicants' complaints, not a Court finding of fact. The Court does not explicitly adopt this characterization as an established fact — it merely records what the applicants complained of. Therefore, **there is no factual grounding that the Court itself has established** for the scope elements.

---

**(b) Interference**

The Court states:

> "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the 'quality of law' requirements established by the Convention or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

The Court uses the word "interferences" as a given, but **provides no specific facts** — no description of what acts or omissions by the government constituted the interference. The existence of interference is assumed rather than grounded in identified facts.

---

**(c) Prescribed by law**

The Court states:

> "the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were either based on legal provisions which did not meet the 'quality of law' requirements established by the Convention or were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

Again, **no specific facts are cited** to support the conclusion that the quality of law requirements were not met. There is no quotation of the relevant legal provisions, no analysis of their wording, accessibility, or safeguards.

---

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society**

Same passage as above. **No specific facts are cited** to support the conclusion that the interference was not necessary in a democratic society. There is no proportionality analysis, no weighing of competing interests, no reference to specific government conduct.

---

**Overall observation**

The Court's entire factual and legal reasoning is effectively outsourced to the leading case:

> "In the leading case of Mustafa Hajili and Others v. Azerbaijan, nos. 69483/13 and 2 others, 6 October 2022, the Court already found a violation in respect of issues similar to those in the present case. Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion on the admissibility and merits of these complaints."

This is a **repetitive case** decided under the Court's pilot judgment or leading case procedure, where the Court dispenses with full individual reasoning by reference to a structurally identical prior case. The factual grounding for all elements exists, if at all, in *Mustafa Hajili and Others*, not in this reasoning text.
