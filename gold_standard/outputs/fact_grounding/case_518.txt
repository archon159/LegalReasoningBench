**Factual grounding for each discussed element:**

**(a) Scope (implied)**

The only factual grounding available in the text is the following description from the opening paragraph:

> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

This is the Court's own framing of the situation, not merely a party submission. It establishes that there were "public assemblies," that the applicants were "organisers or participants" of them, and that the assemblies were "dispersed" (rather than, for example, suppressed because they turned violent). The reference to "dispersal" implicitly supports the peaceful character of the assemblies, though the Court does not make this reasoning explicit.

No further factual grounding for the scope elements can be identified in the text.

**(b) Interference (explicit)**

The factual grounding is the same passage:

> "The applicants complained principally of disproportionate measures taken against them as organisers or participants of public assemblies, namely their arrest in relation to the dispersal of these assemblies and their conviction for administrative offences."

The Court adopts this characterisation as its own framing. The specific governmental acts identified as interferences are: (i) arrest in relation to the dispersal of the assemblies, and (ii) conviction for administrative offences.

**(e) Necessity in a democratic society (explicit)**

The Court's necessity finding is stated as follows:

> "Having examined all the material submitted to it, the Court has not found any fact or argument capable of persuading it to reach a different conclusion as to the admissibility and merits of these complaints. Having regard to its case-law on the subject, the Court considers that in the instant case the interferences with the applicants' freedom of assembly were not 'necessary in a democratic society'."

Notably, the Court does **not** ground this conclusion in any specific facts drawn from the case record. Instead, it grounds it entirely in the absence of distinguishing facts or arguments, and by reference to its prior case-law (Frumkin, Navalnyy and Yashin, Kasparov). No concrete facts from the instant case are quoted or relied upon to support the necessity assessment.

**Summary observation:**

The reasoning is strikingly thin in its factual grounding. The only facts the Court explicitly adopts are those in the opening paragraph (arrest, dispersal, administrative conviction). The necessity conclusion — the central finding — rests not on case-specific facts but on the absence of any reason to depart from prior case-law. This is characteristic of a repetitive-case procedure where the Court treats the matter as settled by its leading judgments.
